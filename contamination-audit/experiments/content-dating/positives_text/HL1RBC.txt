INTRODUCTION 

Because vehicle hazard warning lights have been required by federal regulations on new vehicles for almost ten years, most vehicles are now equipped with such lights and their use is common. 

Sometimes called "four-way flashers," these lights flash simultaneously on each side of the vehicle, to the front and to the rear, for the purpose of indicating that the vehicle represents a hazard to other traffic. Many states have adopted laws authorizing or requiring these lights and regulating their use. Among these laws, there is a rather substantial difference in provisions dealing with use. One state law prohibits vehicle hazard warning lights unless they are red.

This Commentary reviews the current status of state laws and federal regulations concerning vehicle hazard warning lights in the context of comparable provisions of the Uniform Vehicle Code.2/

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations require the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on any vehicle which is stopped upon the traveled portion of a highway or the shoulder of a highway for any cause other than necessary traffic stops. These flashing lights may be used in addition to but not in lieu of other required warning devices (three emergency reflective triangles) which are to be put in place as soon as possible but in any event within ten minutes. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations do not specifically prohibit the use of four-way flashers on a moving vehicle. However, the Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety has taken the position that such use is not consistent with the required use, and that section 392.22 (a) of the regulations implicitly prohibits the use of four-way flashers in any circumstances except as specified in that section.. 

18/ Under this interpretation, a vehicle subject to the regulations would not be allowed to display vehicle hazard warning lights while moving, even if the vehicle constitutes a traffic hazard because of its slow speed. 

Permitted Use

Uniform Vehicle Code 

19/ Uniform Vehicle Code § 12-220 (a) provides that the purpose of vehicle hazard warning lights is to warn the operators of other vehicles of the presence of a vehicular traffic hazard requiring the exercise of unusual care in approaching, overtaking or passing. 

This language clearly does not require or contemplate that a vehicle making use of such lights will necessarily be stopped or disabled, although stopped or disabled vehicles will frequently make use of such lights. The Code contemplates that the operator of any vehicle, regardless of the speed at which the vehicle is traveling, may find it desirable to warn the drivers of other vehicles approaching from the front or rear that his vehicle, or some condition located in proximity to his vehicle, constitutes a traffic hazard. 

The Code does not restrict the use of vehicle hazard warning lights to vehicles which are stopped or disabled. It proceeds on the assumption that under certain circumstances a slower moving vehicle can be as much a hazard to traffic as a stopped vehicle.

FEDERAL PREEMPTION 

The United States Constitution specifies that federal laws have precedence over the laws of the states.44/ If there is a conflict between a federal law and a state law, the state law is preempted.45/

Of course, it is not always easy to determine when such conflicts exist. Where Congress specifically addresses the preemption issue in the statute, it may be much easier to determine the effect upon state laws. The law might specify exclusive federal jurisdiction, for example, in which case any state laws in the field would be preempted. Or the law might provide for some kind of concurrent federal and state jurisdiction, perhaps allowing state laws which are identical to, or which do not conflict with, the federal law. Even where such preemption clauses exist in federal laws, however, the courts sometimes find it difficult to define the extent of the preemption specified by the law.

The Supreme Court has specified that the courts must determine under the circumstances of each particular case whether the state law "stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress. 1146/ If the state law does constitute such an obstacle, it is preempted. 

In making this determination, the Court has developed and consistently utilized three tests of super session.47/ Any one - 13 -

or more of the tests may be employed in determining whether a state law is preempted in a particular case. 

The first test is whether "the scheme of federal regulation is so pervasive as to make reasonable the inference that Congress left no room for the States to supplement it." 48/ If the federal law completely covers the field of possible regulation, this gives evidence of Congressional intent to preclude state regulations.49/ Where, on the other hand, Congress has only occupied a limited field, the states are generally free to protect their interests by additional or supplementary legislation which does not conflict with the federal act.50/ The courts look for a clear manifestation of preemptive intent; there is no presumption that a federal statute was intended to supersede state laws.51/ This is particularly true where Congress legislates in a field which the states have traditionally occupied, and especially with respect to the states' police powers.52/

The second test of supersession is whether the federal law touches a field "in which the federal interest is so dominant that the federal system must be assumed to preclude enforcement of state laws on the same subject."53/ This is saying no more than that some problems are national in their nature, and that only one uniform system of regulation is appropriate. The third test of supersession is whether enforcement of the state law "presents a serious danger of conflict with the administration of the federal program. 54/ 

Where it is not possible to comply with both the federal and the state law, there is obviously a conflict, and the state law must give way. 55/ But no such conflict will be implied, 56/ and the courts will attempt to give effect to both the state and federal laws if this can be done without impairing the federal superintendence of the field.57/ 

Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards 

Scope of preemption.

The federal standards are issued under authority of the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 

1966. Section 103 (d) of that Act provides as follows with respect to state laws: 

14

Whenever a Federal motor vehicle safety standard established under this subchapter is in effect, no State or political subdivision of a State shall have any authority either to establish, or to continue in effect, with respect to any motor vehicle or item of motor vehicle equipment any safety standard applicable to the same aspect of performance of such vehicle or item of equipment which is not identical to the Federal standard. 58/

As noted in the discussion above, where Congress enacts a law in a field which has traditionally been occupied by the states in the exercise of their police power, preemption will not be implied.59/ Nonconflicting state laws are preempted only if there is a very clear manifestation of Congressional intent to preempt.60/ 

Section 103 (d) certainly constitutes a clear manifestation of intent to preempt some state laws. The question is, just what state laws were intended to be preempted. 

None of the state laws discussed in this Commentary is "identical" to the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard in regard to vehicle hazard warning lights. Nor is it reasonable to suggest that the state laws should be amended to be identical to the federal standards. These state laws are intended to apply to the user or owner of a motor vehicle while it is being operated on a highway. They are intended to assure that vehicle hazard warning lights are maintained on a vehicle in good working condition. 

They are intended to be enforced by a police officer on the road, or by an inspector at a vehicle inspection station. The purpose is to require the owner to replace burned out lamps, to keep the lights free of mud, and to take other steps necessary to assure that he will be able to display an effective hazard warning when needed. To require these state laws to conform to the extremely technical manufacturer's design standards contained in the federal regulations would render them ineffective for their desired purpose. 

Yet if they are not "identical," section 103 (d) seems to say they are preempted.

Is it possible that Congress, in providing for federal design standards, intended to preempt all of these nonidentical state laws which regulate the condition of vehicles in use? Ultimately, the courts may have to answer this question. So far, the cases which have involved preemption under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act have not been very helpful. 

15

One group of cases involved an item of equipment, a supplementary headlight called Super Lite, which is not clearly covered by the 

Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. In one of the cases the courts held that the light was not covered by the federal standards, so there was no preemption.6 61/ 

In several other cases, the courts found that the light was covered by the federal standards.. 62/ The Circuit court decided, however, that there was no preemption because the state law and the federal standard did not relate 63/ to the "same aspect of performance" of Super Lite. Section 103 (d) preempts nonidentical state standards only if they relate to the same 64/ aspect of performance.

Unlike the Super Lite, vehicle hazard warning lights are rather clearly regulated by the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. Also, it would appear that the state laws regulate the "same aspect of performance" as the federal regulations. That is, many of the state laws, like the federal regulation, specify the color, location, operating mode and visibility requirements for vehicle hazard warning lights. 

Most of the state laws differ from the federal regulations in that they merely authorize rather than require the lights. Some of the state laws also regulate the use of the lights, and the federal regulations do not do this. Perhaps these differences could be used to conclude that these state laws and the federal regulations do not regulate the "same aspect of performance" of the lights. difficult to speculate because none of the courts which relied upon a narrow interpretation of the "same aspect of performance" standard 65/ was able to define that phrase. Probably most state laws on vehicle hazard warning lights do regulate the same aspect of performance as the federal regulations. If so, the Super Lite cases suggest that the existing state laws on vehicle hazard warning lights are preempted.

A more recent case involved Pennsylvania law requiring state approval of motor vehicle equipment as a precondition to the sale and use of such equipment within the state. 66/ state.66/

The District 

Court distinguished laws applying prior to the first sale to a consumer, from those which apply after the first sale. The former are preempted altogether, even if they are identical to the federal standards, while the latter are preempted unless they are identical to the federal standards. The court observed that the state law requires submission of the equipment for testing and approval, and prohibits sales prior to such approval, while the federal regulations provide for self-certification of compliance with standards by the manufacturer. Even if the state and federal standards are otherwise identical, this different method of enforcing the standards is contrary to Congressional intent and is thus preempted.67/ It's 16 

---

Like the Super Lite cases, the Pennsylvania case does not reach and resolve the issue of preemption of state laws designed to regulate the condition of vehicles in use. But the cases suggest a literal interpretation of section 103(d); if such laws are not identical, they are preempted.

Such preemption might be avoided by interpreting the section 103 (d) requirement that any state standards be "identical" as requiring something less than verbatim conformity. Perhaps only substantive identity, or a lack of any conflicting provisions, is all that is required. Without specifically speaking to the issue, the District Court in the Pennsylvania case suggests such an interpretation of "identical."68/

This interpretation has also been suggested by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration.69, None of the Super Lite cases turned on interpretation of the "identical" requirement, although two of the courts noted that the state laws were clearly not identical to the federal regulations.70/ While a loose interpretation of "identical" might be used to avoid preemption of state laws regulating the condition of vehicles in use, such an interpretation may be undesirable if it allows the states to maintain design standards applicable to manufacturers which are not strictly identical to the federal standards. The Pennsylvania case resolved this problem by finding a Congressional intent, albeit one not clearly expressed in the law, that state laws applicable prior to the first sale to a consumer are preempted even if they are "identical" to the federal standard.

Another possible construction of section 103 (d) relates to the incidence of the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. The burden of compliance with these standards is on the manufacturer, distributor and dealer, with respect to new motor vehicles prior to the first sale to a consumer, rather than on the vehicle user or owner after it has been sold.71/ The standards regulate the design of new motor vehicles; generally they do not regulate the condition, adjustment, or use of equipment on motor vehicles being operated on the highways.72/ The legislative history makes it clear that Congress intended to establish a single uniform set of design standards, so that manufacturers would not have to be concerned with a multiplicity of diverse standards relating to vehicle design.73/ Section 103 (d) could be interpreted as requiring any state law imposing a vehicle design standard applicable to manufacturers, distributors or dealers to be absolutely identical to the federal standard relating to the same aspect of performance of the same item of equipment. But where a state law applies to a vehicle user, rather than to a manufacturer, distributor or dealer, it would not be considered a "safety standard" and section 103 (d) would not result in any preemption. 17 

---

Would such an interpretation allow the states to maintain a law which clearly conflicts with the federal standards, as long as it applies to the user rather than the manufacturer? Could a state, for example, have a law which prohibits all use of vehicle hazard warning lights, even though it does not prohibit the manufacture and sale of cars equipped with such lights? Since such a law applies to the user rather than the manufacturer, it would not be preempted as a nonidentical safety standard under section 103 (d). Such a law would nevertheless be preempted.74, Section 103 (d) is not the sole test of preemption; it is only one test. A law which passes this one test of preemption must still pass those tests of preemption which have been developed by the courts over the years. If a state law, even though not preempted under section 103 (d), stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress, it must fall. A state law prohibiting use of an item of equipment required by the federal standards would clearly thwart the federal purpose in requiring the lights to be on vehicles. Such a law would be preempted. State laws applicable to vehicle users would not have to be "identical" to the federal standards under this interpretation. It would only be necessary that the state law be enforceable without interfering with the federal objective or impairing the federal superintendence of the field.

This interpretation is suggested by several sources. the Super Lite decisions, for example, concluded that Congress intended federal regulation of the automobile manufacturing industry, but did not intend to take from the states the role of regulating vehicles in use.

In light of this history, the conclusion must be reached that Congress has intended that the Federal Government assume the dominant role in the field of performance standards for new motor vehicles. Although the states retain their traditional position with respect to used motor vehicles, and particularly with respect to the operation and condition of automobiles and motor vehicle equipment, their role with respect to the performance characteristics of new motor vehicles is now subordinate to Federal control. 75/

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, in correspondence, has also suggested the importance of the incidence or purpose of the state law to the preemption question.

18

The guiding principle that we would apply to this situation is that State requirements that regulate the design of motor vehicles must be identical to the Federal standards, with the qualifications stated above. It was clearly the intent of 

Congress to provide for uniformity of regulation of the manufacturers in areas where the Federal agency has acted, and they did so by the identity requirement of section 103(d). By contrast, State requirements concerning the condition or adjustment of vehicles generally do not affect the requirements placed on manufacturers, and therefore do not fall within the section 103 (d) identity provision.76/

In the same and other correspondence, however, the National 

Highway Traffic Safety Administration has rejected the concept that the preemption question turns on whether the State law applies to pre-sale or to on-the-road vehicles. 

Our conclusion, therefore, is that the preemption provisions of section 103 (d) apply to any State laws or regulations that have the effect of regulating the same aspect of performance, and the same vehicles, as one or more Federal standards, regardless of whether the State provision is enforced against new (pre-sale) vehicles or those in 77/ use.

In the Pennsylvania case, the court based its ruling on the incidence of the federal regulations, distinguishing state laws which apply before the sale from those which apply after the sale. 

The court, however, ruled that the "identical requirement of section 103 (d) applies only to laws which apply after the sale the opposite of the interpretation which we are suggesting here.78/ 

State laws. Only one of the state laws discussed in this 

Commentary clearly conflicts with the federal standards. The 

New Mexico law generally prohibits flashing lights, but specifically authorizes the use of flashing red lights as warning lights on disabled or parked vehicles.79/ This law would prohibit use of the amber hazard warning lights required on the front of a vehicle by the federal standards. 

- 19 -

Many of the state laws differ from the federal standards. 

Several, for example, specify that the lights may be amber or white on the front, while the federal standards require amber.80/ 

Most of the state laws establish minimum visibility requirements for the lights, while the federal standards specify minimum photometric values.81/ Although such differences represent potential conflicts, none of the current state specifications appears to be inconsistent with the current federal standards. 

That is, a vehicle which complies with the federal standards, probably also complies with all of the State standards, except as noted above regarding New Mexico.

Some state laws which regulate the use of vehicle hazard warning lights could be preempted even though the federal standards do not regulate the use of the lights and do not even specify the purpose of vehicle hazard warning lights. If a state enforced a law which prohibited any use of headlights except during the daytime, this would effectively thwart the federal purpose in requiring new vehicles to be equipped with headlights, even though the federal standards do not regulate the use of headlights. Such a state law would stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the full purposes of Congress in enacting the law, and would thus be preempted. Although it is less clear, a state law which prohibits the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle may also stand as an obstacle to the objectives of the federal law. 

Although the federal standards do not specify the purpose of vehicle hazard warning lights, it may be assumed that they are required to be on the vehicle so that the drivers can give an effective hazard warning when necessary. If a state law interferes with that use, it may be preempted.

Conclusion. The extent of preemption under the National 

Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act is very unclear. It appears from the few available cases that the state laws on vehicle hazard warning lights discussed in this Commentary are preempted if they are not identical to the federal standards. None of the laws is identical in the sense of verbatim conformity. On the other hand, only one of the laws actually conflicts with the federal standards. 

Thus depending upon the interpretation given the word "identical, the federal act preempts either all of the state laws, or only one of of them. Under another possible interpretation, only state laws with the same incidence as the federal standards would be required to be strictly identical to avoid preemption; state laws with a different incidence would be preempted only if they conflict with the federal regulations. - 20 

---

As a practical matter, since most of the state laws do not conflict, and the one conflicting state law does not appear to be enforced, it appears unlikely that a case will arise. 

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations 

Power to regulate interstate commerce. Under the U.S. 

Constitution, the power to regulate interstate commerce is given to Congress. 82/ Nevertheless, it is well established that in the absence of conflicting Congressional legislation, the states have the authority to enforce laws which govern matters of local concern, even when those laws affect or regulate interstate commerce, so long as they do not constitute an unreasonable burden on such commerce.83/

This authority is especially well established in regard to the states use of the police power to promote highway safety. 84/

It seems clear that the states may require motor vehicles, including those moving in interstate commerce, to be equipped with vehicle hazard warning lights, and to use them under specified conditions, unless there is a conflicting federal law or regulation. 39ition 

Scope of preemption.

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations are issued under authority of Part II of the Interstate Commerce Act.86/ 

The Act contains no specific preemption section describing its effect on state laws which regulate vehicles in interstate commerce. The Act does specifically provide that it does not interfere with the exclusive power of states to regulate intrastate commerce.87/ 

The regulations promulgated under authority of the Act do specify their effect on state laws. The regulations make it clear that the states are allowed to enforce laws relating to safety when compliance with the state law will not prevent full compliance with the federal regulations by persons subject thereto.88/ 

The regulations also specifically require motor vehicles in interstate commerce to be operated in accordance with the laws of the state in which they are being operated, provided only that if a federal regulation imposes a higher standard of care, the operator must comply with the federal standard.89/ These regulations clearly anticipate that the states will impose safety regulations which apply to vehicles in interstate commerce, and such state regulations will be preempted only if they conflict with the federal regulations. 

The courts have also interpreted the Interstate Commerce Act as not preempting state safety regulations in the absence of a conflict with the federal regulations.90/ The courts have noted - 21 -

the intention of Congress for the Interstate Commerce Commission to cooperate with the states in the administration of the federal law, and have concluded that it is very clear that the federal law does not entirely displace the state laws,, but does so only when there is a clear and distinct conflict.91/

Thus the scope of preemption under the Motor Carrier part of the Interstate Commerce Act is relatively clear. First, in order to be preempted, the state law must apply to vehicles which are subject to the federal regulations. Generally, this means vehicles in interstate or foreign commerce.. 92/ Second, the law must conflict with the federal regulation. If a state law conflicts with the federal regulations, it is preempted, but only in its application to vehicles operating in interstate or foreign commerce. 

The states are free to regulate intrastate commerce as they see fit.

State Laws. Do any of the state laws conflict with the federal regulations? There is potential conflict in terms of requirements for use of the lights, or prohibition of such use, under certain conditions.

As previously noted, the federal regulations require the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a motor vehicle which is stopped on a highway for any reason other than necessary traffic stops. 

The applicable section provides as follows: 

(a) Turn signals.

Whenever a motor vehicle is stopped upon the traveled portion of a highway or the shoulder of a highway for any cause other than necessary traffic stops, the driver of the stopped vehicle shall immediately flash the two front and two rear turn signals simultaneously as a vehicular traffic hazard warning and continue the flashing until he places the warning devices required by paragraph (b) of this section in use on the highways. The flashing signals shall be used during the time the warning devices are picked up for storage before movement of the vehicle. The flashing lights may be used at other times while a vehicle is stopped in addition to, but not in lieu of, the warning devices required by paragraph (b) of this section.93/

It is our interpretation that the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations do not prohibit the use of vehicle hazard warning lights 

22

on a moving vehicle.

The section quoted above does not specifically prohibit use of the lights on a moving vehicle, nor does any other section of the regulations appear to do so. Compliance with a state law requiring use of the lights on a moving vehicle would not in any way prevent full compliance with the federal requirement for use on a stopped vehicle.94/ Also, the federal regulations require compliance with state laws except where the federal regulations impose a higher standard of care than the state law.95/ 

If a state law requires use of the lights when the federal regulations would not, the state rather than the federal regulation imposes the higher standard of care. Hence we conclude that the federal regulations do not prohibit use of the lights on a moving vehicle and that state laws which require such use do not conflict with the federal regulations. There would be no preemption of state laws under this interpretation. 

The Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety, however, has taken the position that use of the lights on a moving vehicle is not consistent with the required use, and that the section of the regulations quoted above implicitly prohibits any use of the lights except as specified in that section.96/

In construing the meaning of a regulation, the courts give substantial weight to the interpretation of that regulation by the responsible administrative agency. The courts have ruled that the "ultimate criterion" is the administrative interpretation, which is controlling "unless it is plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation. 977 

Therefore, the Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety interpretation that their regulations prohibit the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle must be given great weight. Whether that interpretation is "erroneous or inconsistent with the regulations" can only be decided ultimately by the courts. Of course, it must also be observed that the Bureau could amend the regulations to clearly specify the prohibition, thus removing any doubt. For the purposes of this Commentary, therefore, we will treat the administrative interpretation as controlling. Hence the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations prohibit the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle, and this prohibition applies generally to motor vehicles operating in interstate or foreign commerce. Any conflicting state laws would be preempted in their application to motor vehicles which are subject to the federal regulations. - 23 - 

---

As previously indicated, only one state law and the turnpike regulations of one other state require use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle. The New York State Thruway 

Authority regulation requires trucks equipped with simultaneous flashers to use them when traveling on the Thruway at a speed of less than 40 mph.98/ The Pennsylvania law requires the driver of any vehicle equipped with simultaneously flashing signals to use them whenever the vehicle is not maintaining at least the established speed limit. If outside a business or residence district the driver must use such lights if his vehicle is unable because of weather, grade or similar factors to maintain a speed of at least 25 mph, or a speed consistent with the normal flow of traffic.997 Both the New York Thruway regulation and the Pennsylvania law appear to be partially preempted by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations. That is, these laws do not apply to and may not be enforced against motor vehicles in foreign or interstate commerce which are subject to the federal regulations. The 

New York and Pennsylvania laws are not preempted in their application to other traffic, however. They may still be enforced against vehicles not subject to the federal regulations. 

No other state laws appear to be preempted by the federal regulations. There are some other minor differences between the federal regulations and the seven state laws which require use of vehicle hazard warning lights under certain circumstances, but these differences do not constitute conflicts. Drivers would be able to comply with both the state law and the federal regulation. 

Also there is no conflict created by the 32 state laws which authorize but do not require use of the lights. 

It should be noted that if the Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety changed its opinion and decided to require use of the lights on a slowly moving vehicle, the laws of eight states which now prohibit such use of the lights would be preempted in their application to vehicles in interstate commerce.

Conclusion. Preemption under the Motor Carrier part of the 

Interstate Commerce Act is reasonably clear. Only state laws which conflict with the federal regulations are preempted. They are preempted only in their application to motor vehicles in interstate or foreign commerce which are subject to the federal regulations. The application of state laws to other traffic in not affected. - 24 -

As interpreted by the Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety, the federal regulations prohibit a vehicle which is subject to those regulations from using vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle. The effect of this interpretation is to preempt the 

New York Thruway regulation and the Pennsylvania law which require use of the lights on certain slow-moving vehicles, but these laws. are only preempted in their application to vehicles which are subject to the federal regulations. 

The effect of this preemption is to create nonuniformity of practice within these two states. Motor vehicles which are not subject to the federal regulations must ignore the state rule, using the lights only when stopped.

SUMMARY OF LEGAL ISSUES 

Use of the lights. The most significant issue involves use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle. Currently, use of vehicle hazard warning lights on a moving vehicle is prohibited as to any vehicles subject to the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, regardless of where they are operating. 

The basis for the current Bureau of Motor Carrier Safety position is that highway safety will be best served by reserving the four-way flashing signal to denote a specific kind of hazard, 100/ a stopped vehicle.

Without disputing the merits of this position, we wonder whether it can ever be effectively implemented by the Bureau. Vehicle hazard warning lights are now present on most vehicles. The authority of the Bureau, and its ability to preempt state laws, extends to only a small part of the total vehicle population. If most vehicles can and do display four-way flashers to denote a moving vehicular hazard, it is very doubtful that the Bureau's regulations can effectively reserve the fourway flashing signal to denote a stopped vehicle hazard only. Use of four-way flashers on a moving vehicle is required under certain conditions by one state, and one state tollroad authority. Such use is specifically allowed by 32 states, and another eleven do not specifically prohibit it. Also a number of states now require or authorize the use of four-way flashers on various kinds of moving vehicles such as farm tractors and implements of husbandry, highway maintenance and snow removal vehicles, pilot vehicles for oversized loads, tow trucks, mail delivery vehicles and some others. 101/

Only eight states have laws which agree with the Bureau's position prohibiting the use of four-way flashers on moving vehicles. - 25 

---

There is need for a broad based policy decision regarding the use of vehicle hazard warning lights. A policy which could be uniformly implemented in all jurisdictions and for all vehicles would be very desirable.

If the broad based policy decision is to prohibit the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on moving vehicles, the following should be done: 

1. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations should be amended to clearly specify such a prohibition. 

2. The Uniform Vehicle Code and the laws of 43 states should be revised to incorporate such a prohibition. 

3. Pennsylvania and the New York Thruway Authority should repeal their requirements for use of the lights on certain moving vehicles. 

4. All state laws providing for required or permissive use of four-way flashers on moving farm vehicles or special purpose vehicles should be amended, substituting some other lighting device for this purpose.

If the broad based policy decision is to permit use of vehicle hazard warning lights on moving vehicles whenever the driver is giving warning of a vehicular hazard, the following should be done: 

1. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations should be amended to permit such use of the lights. A specific amendment would be desirable to reverse the effects of the Bureau's current interpretation. 

2. The laws of eight states should be amended to permit use of the lights on a moving vehicle. 

If the broad based policy decision is to require the use of vehicle hazard warning lights on slow moving vehicles or under other specific circumstances, the Federal Regulations, the Uniform Vehicle Code, and the laws of almost all the states would need to be amended to implement such a policy. 

Validity of state law. A second issue involves the validity of all the state laws in light of the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. While only the New Mexico law is in conflict with the 

Federal Standards, the extent of preemption under the federal law is very unclear. This issue does not have great significance in terms of vehicle hazard warning lights, but is important in terms
