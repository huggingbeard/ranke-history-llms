FOREWORD 

This report is of primary use to European production production managers, superintendents, and methods engineers who are concerned with productivity problems, although the general discussion of productivity and the presentation of man-hours per unit in the manufacture of Fractional Horsepower Motors may be of interest to government, labor and trade association officials in Europe.

A study of the technical portions of this report should make it possible for these plant officials to compare manufacturing operations in their plants with the operations in one or more of the plants in this study. 

The products covered in this report were selected on the basis of requests made by European industry groups and transmitted to the Bureau of Labor Statistics by the Mutual Se curity Agency. The plants selected for the case studies are intended to provide examples of the manufacture of these products in American plants but are not intended to represent a statistical sample of the American industry. The criteria used in selecting plants for study are that the product made by the plant be as nearly similar as possible to the product specified in the original European request, that the plant selected maintain records in in suffi- cient detail to provide data, and that the cost of collecting the data be minimized.

CHAPTER 1: THE GENERAL REPORT 1/ 

A tremendous demand for small electric motors to power appliances in American homes and drive light tools in United States factories has led manufacturers of fractional horsepower motors to adopt mass production techniques. The producers 2/ of these small motors sell to many purchasers of relatively small volume, and to a number of economically powerful appliance and light-tool manufacturers who buy in large quantities. This large number of buyers and sellers creates a highly competitive market which forces fractional horsepower motor manufacturers to constantly study their operations in order to reduce costs. Successful producers have adopted standardization of certain parts for various types or sizes of motors, subdivision of operations for mechanization and greater labor efficiency, and line assembly techniques. As a result one well-integrated plant in this report produces a sewing machine motor in 66 man-minutes of of total factory labor. Another highly integrated company makes a 1/4 horsepower general purpose motor with 1-1/2 manhours of total factory labor.

The low cost of small motors and the consumer products in which they are used in relation to workers' earnings, and the low cost of the light tools in which small small motors are used in relation to their usefulness, have created a large and expanding market for these products.

Description of Fractional Horsepower 

Motors An electric motor converts electrical energy to mechanical energy. 

It can do this because an electric current flowing through a wire wound around a piece of iron creates magnetic field. If a similarly created electromagnetic force is situated within such a magnetic field it tends to react with it and be forced out of the field. In an electric motor this force is used to turn the motor shaft which then can provide mechanical power to drive other machinery. 

Physically, an electric motor consists of three major parts: is 

1. The housing, or case, shaped roughly like a closed cylinder with bearings centered in the ends. 

The housings for small motors are usually formed from sheet metal or are machined from diecastings or gray iron castings. 

2. The stator, or field, is a cylinder of of soft soft iron or thicker steel which fits inside the housing. 

Wire wound into slots in the inner periphery of the stator carries the electric current that creates the magnetic field. The projecting parts of the stator core between the slots become, alternately, north and south magnetic poles when electricity flows through the windings. 

3. The rotor, or armature, fits into the hollow of the stator but does not touch it inasmuch as the shaft extending through the rotor's center is supported by the bearings in the housing ends. The rotor carries a second set of windings in slots in its outer periphery to carry the electric current that creates a second magnetic field to react with the stator magnetic field and produce torque or turning force.

In addition, motors may contain mounting brackets, a commutator, and carbon brushes for transmitting current to the rotor windings while it is turning; centrifugal switches to disconnect starting devices; and thermal cut-outs to prevent the motor from overheating.

A fractional horsepower motor is classified as "a motor built in a frame smaller than that having a continuous rating of 1 horsepower, open construction, at 1,700-1,800 r.p.m." 3/. 

The motors included in this study are designed to operate using electric current from an ordinary house-lighting circuit. In the United 

States this is usually 115 volts, single phase, 60-cycle, alternating current.

The principal difference between the motors is in the construction of the rotor and the method of energizing its windings. In the induction type the rotor bars (windings) are usually copper bars, or aluminum cast into the rotor core slots and short-circuited by end rings. Alternating current in the stator windings creates a rotating magnetic field which induces current in the rotor windings, thus magnetizing the rotor core which is then pulled around at a speed slightly below the speed of rotation of the magnetic field of the stator. 4.

The squirrel-cage induction motor is the most popular type of fractional horsepower motor. It is sturdy, simple in construction, and has constant speed characteristics.

Speed depends on the number of magnetic poles in the stator and rotor and the frequency of the electric current. Typical uses for these motors are in small tools, refrigerators, ironers, and washing machines. 

In the universal, brush-type motors, the shaft carries a commutator as well as the rotor core. Wires from the rotor windings are connected to commutator segments. The rotor windings receive current through stationary carbon brushes which 

Sweep the rotating commutator segments and are connected in series with the stator windings to an electric circuit. Universal motors give the same performance on single phase alternating or direct current. Their speed adjusts to the workload, decreasing as the load increases. Universal motors are used for such items as drills, vacuum cleaners, and sewing machines. 

Manufacturing Process 

The broad divisions of the manufacturing process consist of the fabrication and subassembly of the three major motor parts - housing, stator, and rotor plus their final assembly. All plants except F, which buys complete stators and rotors and preformed housings, manufacture subassemblies by line methods. These parts are then stored to be requisitioned as needed for the final assembly line except in Plants A and B. 

In these two particular plants subassembly lines can feed directly into the final assembly line because methods have been analyzed to achieve balanced operations through large parts of the process. some A brief outline of the oper, ations necessary to manufacture fractional horsepower motors is presented in this section. Greater detail is provided in the case studies of operations in each plant in chapter II. 

Housing. The housings for fractional horsepower motors can be formed to dimensions from sheet metal or made from ferrous or nonferrous castings. Holes must be stamped or drilled, the housings painted, and bearings and other parts attached. 

Only Flant B blanks and forms its housings from raw sheet steel. 

Plants D and F also use sheet-metal housings but buy them preformed.

Sheet-metal housings are perforated on punch presses for bearings which support the shaft, assembly bolts, mounting screws or brackets, outlets for electrical leads, oil holes, and usually air vents.

Plant E using light drilling machines.

The heavier gray-iron castings of Plants A and C are machined for holes and to dimensions on automatic drilling machines and lathes. own

Stator. Stator cores are made of laminations of thin steel riveted together. Slots are insulated, main and auxiliary windings are wound and then pressed to shape in these slots, wire leads to brushes and the electric line are prepared, the entire assembly is dipped in insulating varnish and is baked, and then the stator assembly is machined, cleaned, and inspected. 

Laminations are stamped in

Plants A, B, and Dand purchased prestamped by the two other plants which fabricate the stator. Punch presses operating at 100 to 150 r. p. m. perform the blanking operation which produces stator and rotor laminations simultaneously from sheet metal about 

1/40" thick. In all plants except F, (which buys the stator), the correct number of laminations for each motor is ascertained by weighing, and the stack then riveted together to become the stator core. In Plant C the rivets are formed by a diecasting process. Twenty-five laminations were used in the motors reported by 

Plants B and E, 55 by Plant D, and

Tote boxes and hand trucks are used to move materials for these operations except in Plant A which has conveyorized the stator fabrication and assembly line. 

Before the wire windings are assembled to the core, the slots are lined with special insulating paper.

The main coils can be wound directly into the stator slots or wound on forms and then assembled to the core. 

Auxiliary coils used to start the induction type motors are also wound by Plants A and C but purchased by 

Plant B. Coils must be must be formed and pressed back into the slots. After wire leads from the windings are protected with tape the assembly is dipped in insulating varnish and baked to drive out all moisture. 

Dipping and baking operations are conveyorized only in Plants A and B. 

The cleaning of excess varnish, and inspection and testing are usually the final operations on the stator.

In Plant A the stator is assembled to the center band of the housing to form an integral subassembly. 

Rotor. Initial operations of stamping and weighing laminations for the rotor core are similar to those for the stator except that the rotor laminations are skewed to reduce magnetic noise. However, the rotors of the three induction motors (Plants 

A, B, and C) are of the squirrel-cage type in which the "windings" are aluminum or copper bars copper bars in the rotor core slots connected around their ends by end rings. No commutator is used because the bars need not be physically connected to an electric circuit. The windings of the universal type motors (Plants D, E, and

F) are of wire connected to commutator segments which receive electric current through carbon brushes. 

Rotor cores are machined to fit inside the stator core and onto the shaft. The shaft also carries the commutator of the universal motor and usually a small fan to circulate cooling air. Balancing, testing, and inspection complete the operations for the rotor subassembly.

Both Plants A and C die-cast the bars and end rings for their squirrel-cage rotors. In Plant B short lengths of copper wire are fixed in the rotor core slots with a riveting machine and then soldered to bind together the laminations and end rings.

In Plants D and E wire is wound into the rotor slots by machine after -7-

the core has been pressed onto the shaft. The commutator is then pressed onto the shaft and wire leads from the rotor windings are staked to commutator segments with presses and soldered. The rotor assembly of the universal motor is then dipped in insulating varnish and baked, after which excess varnish must be removed.

The commutator must be turned on a lathe so that all segments will contact the carbon brushes. 

Shafts are machined on automatic bar machines in Plants A, B, and C, and with a cylindrical grinder in Plant D.

Dynamic balancing is practiced in all plants.

Plant and Product Characteristics 

The six plants in this study differ widely in size, machinery, variety of products made, and degree of integration of manufacturing operations. Three of the plants provided data on brush-type motors generally similar in size and design. The other three motors, although all of the induction type, range from 1/100 to 1/4 horsepower and are dissimilar in several design characteristics.

As a result of the differences among the plants and motors, the case studies in chapter II of this report present a cross section of methods used in the United States in the manufacture of fractional horsepower motors. 

The plants in this study range in size from about 25 employees (Plant F) to more than 1,500 (Plant A).

The variety of products made, including various models of motors as well as other electrical and nonelectrical products, generally increases with the size of the plants in this report. The smallest plant makes only a few models of a few models of similar motors and models of a single additional light-weight electrical product. The largest plant manufactures not only a wide line of fractional and integral horsepower motors, but other electrical and metal products as well.

Decisions to purchase parts or to subcontract some operations are common, especially among small-size plants. A relatively high volume, or the use of expensive automatic equipment for a particular part by some supplier - even a competitor may result in a lower cost by purchase than by manufacture. Even fully integrated plants sometimes subcontract parts of manufacturing operations when short-term demand is high. 

Despite the above differences among plants, they tend to be similar in several respects. All plants engage in methods and design analysis and time study of operations. Although major design or method changes are not particularly frequent for the small motors studied, the cumulative effect of many small changes in machinery, methods, design, holding or positioning devices, sequence of operations, and layout of the plant more than pay the costs of the engineering personnel used. In the two smallest plants in this report (E and F) these engineering services tend to be part-time and are informally organized. In the other plants full-time engineers are employed, with Plant A using more than 200 professional and nonprofessional workers in its design, methods, and standards departments.

All plants make a great effort to maintain a high rate of production when lines are in operation. If production of sufficient units cannot be justified by orders on hand or by inventory conditions, the entire operation is suspended and workers are shifted to other products. 

For example, Plant D states that the final assembly line of the reported model is closed if for any for any reason' production of 500 a day cannot be scheduled. The majority of plants in this report do not produce these motors continuously for long periods of time. All of the companies will companies will accept small orders for motors of special design, but it is generally uneconomical for a purchaser to order specially designed motors in these sizes in lots of fewer than 200 because all design and tooling costs will be charged to the special order.

Case Study Data on Productivity and Factory Performance FRACTIONAL HORSEPOWER MOTORS CHAPTER II: CASE STUDIES Introduction 

This chapter contains six factory performance case studies on the manufacture of an assortment of fractional horsepower motors. The size and design of the motors, and the degree of integration of manufacturing operations vary among the reporting plants. Therefore, no attempt is made to compare the efficiency of one plant with another. It is hoped that the technician interested in production problems will be able to find descriptions to cover operations with which he is concerned and will be be able to evaluate the efficiency of the methods reported in relation to methods with which he is familiar. The man-hours shown per motor for individual operations are averages for production runs of a few days to a month. Like all averages they represent a theoretical performance; actual performance at individual operations may have required more or less time on any one motor. Plants and operations are described as fully as permitted by the information supplied and the necessity of concealing the identity of the reporting plants. 

When the reader examines the man-hours for individual operations shown on the following pages he should not assume that a relatively high rate of output implies unusual physical exertion by the worker. Although it is generally known that productivity in the United States is high and increasing, it is not always understood that that the high level of output per hour of work is being achieved more by introducing powered material movement and automatic machinery, devising plant layouts, and developing methods which methods which tend to convert the worker from a source of energy to a controller of machines, than by a greater physical exertion of the worker.

Plant A: General Description Building. This is a modern steel-framed and brick multistory building with 500,000 square feet of floor area. Production of the fractional horsepower motor reported takes place on a single floor level. 

Layout is open and uncrowded.

Machinery. This plant uses automatic machinery wherever savings warrant. Special jigs, gages, and fixtures are used throughout its operations. Materials are moved by conveyor and lift truck. 

Employment. More than 1,500 production and related workers are employed.

Operating Practices Variety of Products Made. This plant produces a wide line of fractional and integral horsepower motors, electrical appliances, and other metal products. It will design and manufacture custom motors on request. 21

If the special design is a slight variation from the standard model, a bench is placed near the assembly line where one or two workers fabricate and install the special parts and return the motor to the line. If a completely different kind of motor is scheduled, an entirely separate bench is set up and many hand operations are used.

Size of Runs. Using line produc tion methods and 2 shifts, 1,500 general purpose 4 horsepower motors are produced each day. The second shift is not as fully manned as the first and produces only 400 of the total. If a survey of orders on hand and an inventory fail to justify constant output, workers are shifted to the production of other items, and small orders are filled either from stock or by operating only the final assembly line and drawing on stocked parts. This company will accept orders for 500 or more custom motors of this type and rating, or 

2,000 or more of the standard motors.

Professional Engineering Serv ices.

This plant divides its en- gineering services into three departments: design, with 50 employees; methods, with 147; and standards, with 25.

The design department develops the basic motors and other products manufactured, using as many standard or common parts as possible. It also designs special motors for customers, using standard motors as a basis.

The methods department determin es the processing method for all manufactured parts. This department designs and makes tools and buys machinery for the entire plant. It responsible for all jigs, fixtures, dies, and gages. It also sets up the process charts for each group of is operations. The standards department makes time studies, sets rates of pay for all jobs, and issues the process charts. 

Company officials illustrated the value of their engineering departments by noting that the average hourly earnings of employees have increased steadily but labor cost per motor has not increased in the past several years.

The production control control system used for planning and scheduling the many manufacturing operations carried out in this plant is described in chapter III of this report. 

This company pays its direct labor incentive wages with a guaranteed minimum hourly wage and extra pay for above-standard output. Indirect labor receives hourly wages.

Product Description 

Plant A reported on a 14 horsepower, 110-120 volt, 60 cycle A.C. split phase, general purpose squirrel cage induction motor; Nema frame 

No.56; 1,725 r.p.m.; with centrifugal starting switch and sleeve bearings. 

Manufacturing Process

The housing for Plant A's 1/4 

horsepower motor is made from two cast iron end bells and a center band of sheet steel. Both end castings and base are machined for bolt holes and to receive bearings and the center band. Additional drilling and tapping is necessary on one end to attach the starting switch and plastic terminal plate which are also fabricated in the plant. Center bands are sheared from sheet steel, are rolled, and then are welded along the seam. The 77

Ta at Si ol he TE bei יב Bir 10 S. is motor base, a resilient type designed to absorb noise and vibration, blanked and formed from sheet steel on punch presses. Clamps to hold the base to the end bells are also made from sheet steel and are then nickelplated. All other housing parts are sprayed with enamel. 

The stator and rotor cores are constructed of 59 iron laminations. 

The stator laminations are riveted together. Main coils are machine wound into the core after the slots are insulated. The phase-splitting auxiliary coils are wound separately and blocked into the slots with the main coils. The stator is dipped in insulating varnish and baked, and then pressed into the center band of the housing to form a major subassembly. 

After 59 rotor laminations are stacked and skewed, the aluminum bars and end rings for the squirrel-cage winding of these induction motors are die-cast into the slots - 3 at a time. The rotor is annealed at high temperatures and then shrink-fitted to the machined shaft. A centrifugal actuator which acts on the starting switch to cut out the starting winding once the motor has attained speed is also assembled to the shaft. 

For final assembly, conveyors carry parts directly from subassembly lines or from storage areas. 

Detailed descriptions of the individual operations for making the above parts and for final assembly are given in the accompanying schedules.

Plant B: General Description 

Buildings. The company operates in a 35-year-old, 1-story brick and steel-framed building with about 

14,000 square feet in the motor manufacturing department. 

Machinery. Plant B is highly mechanized, using high speed equipment and conveyors for materials movement. 

Employment.

The plant has about 150 employees. 

Operating Practices Variety of Products Made. Motor production is highly integrated. 

Small motors are made for incorpora- 1 1 XX tion into small electric home appliances also manufactured by this plant, or for sale to other appliance producers.

Size of Runs. Output is about 

300 motors a day. Line production methods and conveyorized assembly are used. Several inspections insure that the finished product meets 

NEMA, AIEE, and plant specifications.

The minimum size run has been 480, and the largest, 1,700 over a 5-day period. This firm usually produces a full month's supply of a particular motor in a 4 or 5-day production run. When the particular motor reported is not being manufactured, the personnel (and to some extent, the factory facilities) are used in the production of other products. - 65 -

Professional Engineering Serv ices. Five engineers and allied electrical equipment using Parts integrated line production. are employed are fabricated from purchased materials. for design and redesign of the motors and for time study, and are considered to be effective in reducing costs. Constant minor changes in design were reported. This company will make specially designed or custom motors on any size order but reports that the cost of tooling for less than 200 is usually prohibitive. 

No variations from standard models were being made at the time of the report. Certain parts are designed to be interchangeable among several of the motors produced by this company.

The well developed production control system of this plant is described in chapter III of this report.

All direct workers are paid incentive wages. New workers are given on-thejob training under a foreman's supervision. Indirect workers, who constitute about 40 percent of all factory labor, include tool con- trol and tool maintenance men, design, method analysis, and time study, janitors and building maintenance men, storeroom employees, service repair, shipping and receiving, plating, inspectors, supervisors, and production controllers.

Product Description 

Plant B reported on a 1/100 horsepower 4 pole, 110-120 volt, 60 cycle, A.C. shaded pole, induction type motor with self alining bearings, for use with such equipment as fans, small oil burners, and business machines.

Manufacturing Operations 

This plant produces its motors raw 

To make the housing, sheet steel is sheared, blanked, drawn, and perforated in a series of heavy-duty presses and the housing edges chamfered in a lathe. The operations on the front and rear housing are similar except for an additional perforation in the rear housing as an outlet for the cord. After the twomotor brackets are sheared, stamped to shape, and spotwelded to the front housing, the housing parts are sprayed with paint. 

Laminations for the stator and rotor cores are stamped simultaneously. Both cores are riveted. The copper rivets of the rotor core serve as the squirrel- cage winding. The stator coils are wound by hand before pressing into stator core slots. 

The stator is then dipped into insulating varnish and baked. The rotor is balanced after assembly of the core to the shaft. Subassembly of the rotor and stator, and final assembly into the housing is conveyorized.

Product Description 

One-eighth horsepower, 60 cycle, 115 volt, 1,800 r.p.m., single phase, sleeve bearing, NEMA frame, induction type, oil burner motor with centrifugal starting switch and manual reset thermal cutout to prevent overheating. Rough iron castings for housing and prestamped laminations for stator and rotor cores are purchased.

Manufacturing Process 

The two housing parts of this totally enclosed motor are purchased as rough iron castings. They are machined on a six station vertical turret lathe. Machining time is about half that formerly needed with a single spindle turret lathe. Rotor and stator laminations are purchased prestamped. This company recently started assembling stator cores by using a die-casting machine and shooting molten aluminum through holes in the laminations. Formerly, 2 men could produce 350 cores per day by riveting. With the die cast method, 2 men produce 500 a day.

Material costs are also lower for die-casting than for riveting. The stator is wound, using an electronically controlled machine. The squir rel-cage winding of the rotor is aluminum die-cast into the slots. 

Final assembly is conveyorized.

Product Description re- 

Plant E reported on a 1/15 horsepower, universal (AC or DC brush type), 5,000 r. p. m., 115 volt, 60 cycle, motor.

Manufacturing Process no This plant does stamping operations. It buys die-cast housings and laminations which are prestamped. 

It drills, taps, and assembles bearings to the housings. Fabrication is not continuous because the housings are sent to subcontractors for painting. Operations on the two ends or halves - are similar except for additional drilling and assembly for the name plate on the shaft end. 

The rotor and stator cores are made by riveting 25 steel laminations purchased prestamped. The field coils are wound by machine before assembly and forming in the stator core. The armature coils are machine-wound into the rotor core. The wound rotors and stators are dipped in insulating varnish and baked.

This motor is a brush type and the leads from the armature windings are staked and then soldered to the commutator segments. Final as- sembly is done at eight stations.

TECHNICAL DATA Production Planning and Control 

In all the plants of this study production control is a set of procedures whereby production orders are issued in a manner which will coordinate sales, materials, plant facilities, and available personnel. 

This is followed up to determine whether the expected performance has been accomplished. Where the desired coordination has not been accomplished, adjustment is made by changes in instructions. In each plant communication of these orders and information between the key personnel is generally made by means of a system of forms. 

Plant A, with more than 1,000 employees, maintains an elaborate system of forms which are routed from the production control department to all departments involved. 

The movement of these forms to from the production control department determines the initiation, control, and and adjustments of the manufacturing process. 

Plant B's production control department arranges the incoming orders and plans manufacturing runs according to available facilities. The production foremen are responsible for the daily scheduling of men and machines, their decisions being governed by required completion dates shown on the production order. company has a system of forms including shop order, bill of materials, inventory controls, and requisitions. 

A special feature is a move

This order which indicates the number of items, operations performed, and inspection status of each manufacturing lot as it passes from one department to the other. The production control department is is thus informed of the progress of the work in process as it moves through the plant. 

Plant C has a scheduling department that initiates and schedules the production runs through the plant. A unique procedure is followed by this plant. A special multiform invoice with 12 copies is filled out when a sales order is received. The shop copy goes to the scheduling department where the job is scheduled and the necessary job tickets are issued. The job tickets list the work to be done and parts required by the various departments. 

These job tickets are distributed to the department foremen. The triplicate copy of the multiform invoice goes to the stockroom where, with the aid of a drawing list ( bill of materials), the stockroom personnel accumulate the required parts and material and deliver them to the departments involved. 

The direct worker punches in out on and out on his time card for each job. The job number and shipping order (obtained from job ticket) are entered on the time card. The timekeeper posts this information on the second sheet of the job ticket. This is used to record the job costs. - 119 -

Production Planning and Control in 

Plant E

Plant E is a small plant with approximately 50 employees. The production planning and control in this organization depends largely on communications between key people; namely, the office manager who schedules production, and the production foreman who assigns the work in the most appropriate manner to fulfill the production goals. 

As customers' orders arrive at the plant, the superintendent issues to the production foreman a shop order indicating total quantity and number to be manufactured daily along with instructions and drawings. The office manager gets a copy of the shop order. It is his responsibility to schedule the overall. production, coordinating all the orders on file and developing a comprehensive schedule in advance. He also analyzes the customer order and, with the storekeeper's advice, orders all the necessary materials. He endeavors to maintain a 30-day inventory of all materials, parts, and components in the storeroom. 

The production foreman receives the shop order and production schedule. He requisitions the materials needed from the storeroom and assigns men and machines to the necessary operations for producing the motors.

The foreman must make work assignments for each man and machine following the broad goals stipulated by the office manager's production schedule. He must see that materials are provided at the proper places along the production line in the right amounts at the right time. He must see that output is developing according to schedule. In this connection, the foreman submits production and inspection reports to the superintendent who gives management the information to effect control over the manufacturing operations. The office manager receives copies of the production and inspection reports so that he can match performance with the goals he has scheduled. The production and inspection reports reveal data on quantity and quality of product manufactured. The data, together with sales and shipment reports, become the basis for managerial decisions by the president of the company. The production control system of this small company depends more on the key men, especially the production foreman, rather than on a routing and dispatching of forms between production control, production departments, stores department, cost accounting department, payroll department, and production manager.

This plant does use certain basic forms illustrated on the following pages. The shop order from the superintendent includes an instruction card card (fig. 18) for each operation required which specifies the operation, part name, tools required, and standard times. Another form used is the rate card (fig. 19) which summarizes all the operations and pieces per hour for each part.

A specialized form for the armature line is the armature operation card (fig. 20) which lists each operation name and quantity produced for each operation. The rate card and operation card aid the production foreman in filling out production summaries for reporting to the superintendent.

Labor Background 

Workers' earnings, along with the earnings of other groups in the

United States, have increased in re al as well as in monetary terms along with an almost continuous improvement in productivity. The

Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated that between 1909 and 1947 the average real weekly earnings of factory workers doubled, whereas hours worked per week declined one-fifth. These substantial improvements in income could not have been achieved except for the increased output per man-hour of work made possible by a constantly improving technology of production. Although all workers have benefited from greater productivity, workers in industries with strong unions have generally been in the forefront in obtaining a share of the gains from increased productivity. 

In addition to greater weekly pay and shorter hours per week, workers have also chosen to take some of the returns from greater productivity in the form of "fringe benefits" paid holidays, vacations, pensions, rest periods, etc. Despite the advantages arising from technological change, resistance to the introduction of new methods and machinery has occurred on many occasions in the past because of the threat of unemployment and the natural resentment of changes. Examples of management and labor working together and successfully coping with this problem are given in appendix I.

Adjustment to Technological Change 

Labor and management in the 

United States have always been faced with problems arising from changes in processes and the introduction of new machinery. The problems generally include determination of earnings, workload, work schedules, 'displacement of special skills by new machinery and methods and, in some cases, actual displacement of workers. 

Over the past 25 years, with the more rapid growth of labor unions, adjustments and solutions to the se problems, in many instances, have been reached on a case-by-case basis as a result of negotiations between the two parties. The success of the solutions and adjustments has depended almost entirely on the attitudes and procedures with which labor and management approached each case and only incidentally on the technical details of the change itself. 

Moreover, negotiations over technological change in many fields of manufacturing indicate that the experience gained from a case in one industry is applicable to problems of change in other industries. Consequently the methods and ideas developed in negotiations over technological change are change are used in many industries besides the one in which a problem first appeared.
