CHAPTER ONE INTRODUCTION 

DEFINITION. Forestry is that art which is concerned with adequately supplying the world with forest products. The more important products are those for which the forest furnishes the raw material; recreation and aesthetic stimulation for which the forest is the environment; and the protection uses of the forest such as watershed protection, the prevention of erosion, and the deterring of wind and shifting sand. 

American forestry may be premised on the production within the United States of all the forest products utilized by the oitizens and industries of the nation. Such a precept is in keeping with a nationalistic philosophy. From an international viewpoint there is no need for each nation to balance forest production and consumption within its borders as long as the world at large furnishes an adequate supply of forest products in the most efficient manner.

In general, forestry is subject to the same economic laws as is any other business. 

Because of man's limitations, a certain amount of failure, as in other businesses, is to be expected. A success is dependent upon having an economic opportunity above the margin, the development of market opportunities in competition with other materials, and in maintaining a high standard of management. Likewise, the question of success or failure of forestry enterprises is not one of general application but rather one which requires an answer for each particular forestry opportunity. It is just as difficult to say that forestry always pays or does not pay as to say the same of the grocery or any other business. Until man can develop an economic system which prevents failure and losses, the forester must accept the risks of his business and premise his success upon furnishing the greatest amount of utility per unit of value. 

PECULIARITIES OF FORESTRY. Although forestry is subject to the same general economic laws as are other lines of endeavor, its peculiarities cause the various economic tendencies to react somewhat differently than in other commodities. It is desirable, therefore, to recognize some of the more important of these peculiarities. 

In the first place, the forest is quasi-public in nature. 

Its value often depends, primarily, upon the non-commercial uses of recreation and protection rather than the commercial use of raw materials for wood products. These non-commercial uses are very valuable to the public at large but seldom can the private owner receive a revenue for them. Likewise, the fire risk is

mainly caused by the citizenry and as such is beyond the control of the private owner. Perhaps in no other business does the public have such a large interest outside the control of the owner. 

In the second place, the business of forestry sometimes requires an investment which produces no returns for a long length Investments in such projects must necessarily be limited of time. to the government and to those private individuals and concerns who have large enough fortunes to enable them to create a trust for the future. On the other hand, all forestry opportunities do not require the foregoing of an annual income for an extended length of time. It is extremely likely, that a thorough investigation of the property of a large number of private concerns having holdings of virgin stumpage would reveal that a sustained yield basis would enable them to cut their stands with a maximum of profits. The bogy of the long-time investment is one which may melt when the attention becomes fixed on the individual tracts of forest land. 

In the third place, stability of management is of supreme importance. Sustained yield requires the maintenance of a sufficient supply of growing stock with the proper distribution of age classes. Any over-cutting of the stand necessitates a reduction of future profits. History is replete with examples of nations and individuals over-cutting their forests to meet curront expenses. The present practice of American lumbermen of handling their tract as a mine rather than as a crop is closely analogous to unstable management. Likewise, vested rights are easy to grant and hard to eliminate.

The forest practice of Europe is often badly handicapped by the existence of the rights of the user and other vested rights. 

In America the recreationalist, particularly the hunter and fisherman, is rapidly gaining a vested right in American timberlands.

In the fourth place, forestry is characterized by diversity. The product, while non-perishable, is extremely diverse. Likewise, the technique of the work and the character of the economic opportunity is quite changeable. Therefore, intelligent and forceful management becomes of prime importance to the success of any forestry business. 

Finally, forestry has certain inheritances from the past which are handicaps to its advancement. Owners are prone to subscribe to the belief that lumbering and forestry are profitless. They are not, therefore, interested in the pos➡ sibilities of sustained yield and certain other steps of progress. Woods workers are inclined to regard low quality material as valueless, thereby making it difficult for efficient managers to institute changes designed to lower costs of production. 

Likewise, the prospective buyer is often inclined to regard wood as of inferior quality; consequently, he often fails to buy the material which gives him the greatest utility value per unit of cost. This general tendency to regard the wood products industry with an inferiority complex coupled with the odium growing out of the results of the land distribution policy of the nation and with which the lumberman is often regarded, has undoubtedly deterred progress in forestry.

СЛ As in other lines of endeavor, the solution of the forestry problem must come about through hard, intelligent work. New ideas should be received open-mindedly and considered as intelligently as the known facts will allow. Action premised on knowledge and reason is the only means of improving the forestry situation.

CHAPTER TWO THE IMPORTANCE OF FORESTRY 

Historically the forest has always been commonly used by civilized peoples. In pre-historic days the forest was man's home; it furnished him with fuel for his fire, and, to a certain extent, his food. Modern civilization has created a metal age 

Nevertheless forest proby developing the products of the mine. ducts are used in a greater variety of ways and usually in greater quantities than was the case a few centuries ago. 

SERVICE TO AMERICAN PIONEERS. 

The American pioneer usually believed the forest a hindrance to progress. This viewpoint was a natural outgrowth of the pioneer's environment. 

That which is common has a low value while that which is scarce is highly valued; consequently, the very commonness of the forest gave its products a low value, regardless of their utility. Furthermore, the forest was a shelter for the lurking Indian or other enemy; it made agriculture difficult; it impeded travel and caused the unwary to become lost; and lastly, the danger of large forest fires destroying life and property was sometimes great. 

From a utility standpoint, the forest furnished the pioneer with his house, his furniture, his fuel, his fencing and, to a certain extent, his food, tools, and implements. 

Likewise, the record of the early exports of forest products from the early Atlantic settlements clearly indicates the importance of the forest as a source of actual wealth to the pioneer, while many of the extra hardships suffered by the settlers on the great plains can be readily traced to a lack of sufficient forest products.

SERVICE TO MODERN AMERICA. 

The rising value of forest products, due to their growing scarcity, has undoubtedly curtailed the service of the forest to our people. However, the maximum prosperity of a country does not require that it should maintain a maximum possible supply of any product or group of products, but that it should maintain a supply of each product sufficient for the maximum utility of all the wealth of the nation. Therefore, the maximum amount of forest products desired must depend upon the ability of those products to produce a maximum of utility per unit of cost. Whenever it becomes more efficient to use other materials, forest products should be replaced thereby. The importance of forest products must therefore depend upon their ability to be of service to mankind in reference to their cost of production. air.

From a recreational standpoint, the forest furnishes a very high grade product. It requires healthful exercise in pure 

The environment is not the putrid one of the ordinary pool hall but one of enticing intellectuality and high morality. 

Likewise, the cost to the individual is moderate, particularly in reference to its utility. The nation is greatly benefited by such recreation.

From a protectional forest standpoint, the forest may be of supreme importance to the surrounding country. The destruction of fertile fields by shifting sands and floods is a type of destruction which strikes at the very life blood of a nation. The service of the forest in ameliorating the destructive effects of wind and water and in supplying a more constant water supply for

irrigation and hydro-electric development cannot be measured. 

Industrially the forest is important only in the field of wood products. As a nation we produce and consume approximately forty per cent of the wood products of the world. The wood products industries employ over 900 thousand wage earners, pay over 900 million in wages each year, and produce products valued at over three billion dollars. Certain sections of our country are largely dependent on the forest for their economic prosperity. In these sections a serious curtailment of the production of wood products would result in a large decrease of taxable wealth, the necessity for a serious re-adjustment on the part of the laborers, and the crippling of transportation, markets and other economic activity in those regions. 

From the standpoint of national defense, the forest is extremely important. Wars require large amounts of materials to be produced in a short period of time. Since one of the properties of wood is the ease with which it can be shaped to desired form, large quantities are used for war purposes. The success of an army depends, in part, upon the supplies furnished for its activities. Any nation which depletes its forests will, therefore, find itself seriously handicapped in times of war.

CHAPTER THREE THE DEVELOPMENT OF AMERICAN FORESTRY 

Forestry in Colonial America was decidedly limited.

Al- Governmental regulation of private forest enterprise was unnecessary except in the matter of protecting the markets for wood products. Reservations of timberland were made only to insure the navy of adequate material for wooden ships until the advent of the steel ship made such reservations unnecessary. though the direct effect of Colonial forestry upon present American forest policy was extremely slight, the indirect effect caused by the thought of the period and reflected in the land policy of the United States was great. 

NATIONAL LAND POLICY. Practically all the lands except those along the Atlantic seaboard have been distributed by the national government. Aside from the 259 million acres given the central government by the original thirteen states, the national land domain was secured by the national government. The public land tended to unite the nation and its method of distribution encouraged democracy and equality of opportunites by eliminating castes built around land ownership. Unfortunately, the reckless distribution, occasioned by an office rather than a field administration, encouraged selfishness and allowed fraud to become common.

In the experimental era before 1820, Congress followed a policy of selling the land for the needed revenue. The first attempt to sell in large units was a failure and resulted in a policy of selling to the highest bidder in blocks of 320 acres (later 160 acres). This policy allowed the speculators to out-

bid the settler and caused the new states to make a drive for the right to handle the distribution of the lands within their borders. 

The pre-emption principle, granting the settler the first right to buy at a stated price ($1.25 per acre), was definitely adopted in 1841 after its gradual development from 1820. The pre-emption law was not repealed until 1891. Since The pre-emption law, while in force, was used extensively to get title to all kinds of natural resources. it allowed thirty days to locate and a year to pay, it enabled lumber operators to file, cut the timber, and move to the next quarter section where the process was repeated. As there were no field inspections made by the federal government, this form of "timber stealing" was common and lucrative. 

The free homestead policy was adopted in 1862 and has not been repealed. The principle of the homestead law is that the worker and home builder is entitled to the entire reward of developing the raw agricultural land. The area available to any one citizen is 160 acres except in certain sections of the country. A residence of three years (originally five years) is required. Likewise, certain improvements and cultivation are required as set by the law. As administered, the applicant filed his intention to homestead a given tract and swore that the land was not mineral, that he was a qualified applicant, that he was not acting as an agent of anyone, that he would fulfill the terms of the law, and that the homestead was for himself. Patents to the land were issued whenever witnesses (of the homesteader's choosing) swore that the applicant had met the requirements of

the law as to cultivation and improvements, Since the General Land Office employees received the fees paid, graft became easy, as it was to everyone's interest to grant homesteads. The failure to provide a field inspection of the property enhanced the opportunity for fraud. In fact, large areas of timberlands were blocked-up under the homestead law The fraudulent homesteading of timberlands and graft in connection therewith was finally stopped during Roosevelt's administration by a series of sensational trials. 

Large grants of lands have been made by Congress for various purposes In the Southwest the title to the Spanish grants was given to the holders thereof. The swamp lands in each state were given to the state concerned, a gift which resulted in graft and conflicting claims. Grants for education were made by giving certain sections in each township to the states for educational purposes. Likewise, grants of large acreages were made for higher education.. Grants of alternate sections for a strip often forty miles wide were made to railroads which built their lines in the West. Smaller grants of the same type were made for military roads. Finally, a grant of 

160 acres was made to each veteran of the Civil War. 

Mineral land distribution after a hectic history, has finally resulted in the general leasing law of 1920 for all nonprecious mineral lands. Under this law the Secretary of Interior can offer any public lands for lease. A small rental is charged for the use of the land and a royalty is charged for all minerals extracted.. The leases try to prevent monopoly, protect the laborand prevent a wasteful use of the resource.. The terms of the ers

Precious minerals are handled under the old Spanish law based upon the principle that discovery of such minerals gave the first right to develop and development gave ownership. 

Lode claims are limited to areas 600 by 1500 feet and placer claims to twenty acres legal subdivision. 

TIMBERLAND POLICY. No particular timberland policy was developed during the period of land distribution as all land was believed to be agricultural in nature. In 1855 the General Land Office was given authority to protect the public lands from timber trespass but no funds were appropriated until 1872. No particular good was accomplished by the enforcement of this act as settlenents were made on the basis of the value of the timber taken. 

From the lumberman's viewpoint, stealing government timber was still a proposition of everything to gain and nothing to lose. 

For the government, it resulted in an expenditure of over half a million dollars and a return of slightly less than the cost. 

The free timber act of 1878 merely legalized the common practice of miners and settlers in using public timber for domestic purposes. Certain forestry provisions such as piling the brush and cutting no trees less than eight inches in diameter were in the law. However, there was no attempt made to enforce these provisions.

The timber culture act of 1873 was the outgrowth of the necessity for trees in the prairie region. Under this act the government gave 160 acres to any resident provided he would plant and maintain for eight years, forty acres of it in trees. 

Since there was no field enforcing agency, the act resulted in few trees being matured and a good deal of public lands being transferred to private individuals. The law was repealed in 1891.

THE EFFECT OF THE LAND POLICY ON THE PEOPLE. The rapid dis tribution of the public domain to private owners, especially to small owners, has resulted in developing the so called typical American traits of character. In the days of the public domain any citizen could, without a great deal of capital secure a portion of the public domain. If he disliked the wages or working conditions of his life, he had but to "go west and grow up with the country." Our public domain was the melting pot where men learned the lessons of hard work, sacrifice, thrift and equality. Today, with our melting pot practically gone, we must

rely upon those traits of character developed in the pioneer days, to devise a government which will insure a reasonable equality of opportunity to its citizens and at the same time give a sufficient reward for worthwhile effort.

Unfortunately, the beneficial development of character and equality of opportunity was partially overcome by the petty graft which accompanied the land distribution. The initial mistake of considering all land agricultural made it necessary to stretch the law to cover lands not truly farm lands. Next, the lax administration of the law made graft easy. As a result, the average citizen was apt to think that government property belonged to the first man who could obtain control of it. For this reason, any governmental business has difficulty in protecting the interests of the government from encroachment by private individuals or concerns.

THE EFFECT OF THE LAND POLICY ON THE RESOURCES 

In a new country rich in resources, the first exploitation is always superficial and wasteful. As the region develops the methods of using the resources become more and more efficient until, finally, each resource is being put to its highest and best use. In the main, agricultural land has followed this trend in the United 

States. 

Unluckily, other national resources, when forced upon the basis of agricultural lands, failed to follow the normal trend and produced chaotic results. All the natural resources have suffered the same economic history as have the timberlands. 

Bricfly, the resources passed into private ownership, only to be

As a result, the sale value beused for speculative purposes. came much higher than the actual value. By the end of the speculative period, the speculator had obtained a big share of the legitimate profits of the lumberman and had left him a heritage of an unwise investment. Next, mounting taxes and protectional charges, combined, at times, with poor financial programs created a situation whereby each individual's personal welfare call for marketing the timber in as short a time as possible. This forces a superficial and wasteful exploitation of the entire resource and at the same time tends to forestall action necessary for the perpetuation of the timber crop. 

THE CONSERVATION MOVEMENT. Until late in the nineteenth century the people of our country commonly regarded our resources as inexhaustible and our lands illimitable. The abundance of all kinds of resources caused some of them to be considered as an obstacle to the proper development of the country. Since the laws and customs of a country reflect the ideas of the people, much of the economic waste in the resources of the country can be traced to the erroneous idea of inexhaustible resources and illimitable land.

The conservationist recognizes that natural resources are of three types: first, those that are practically unlimited in amount, like water and air; secondly, those that are limited in quantity but which may be renewed, as forests; and finally, those which not only are limited in supply, but also are not renewable, such as minerals. Naturally each type of resource requires a treatment different from that accorded the others.

The two immediate objectives of conservation are to reduce waste in every form and to build permanent industries based upon our natural resources, The principle, "the greatest good to the greatest number for the longest length of time", sometimes requires the sacrifice of present gains for greater future gains. In such instances, it is necessary for the government either to own or, at least, control the exploitation of the resource. 

THE DEVELOPMENT OF GOVERNMENTAL FORESTRY. 

Governmental forestry is an outgrowth of the conservation movement. It resulted from a long and continuous educational campaign to secure the backing of public opinion. Governmental forestry, both state and national, has enjoyed a steady growth since its inception. 

Federal forest work started in 1876 with the appointment of a special agent by the Department of Agriculture to study the forestry conditions in the United States. Ten years later the 

Division of Forestry was created in the Department of Agriculture. 

For several years its activities were limited by small appropriations. Gradually, however, the field work expanded until, in 1901, the division became the Bureau of Forestry. In 1905 the 

Bureau of Forestry became the Forest Service. 

Federal forestry work is mainly centered in the Forest 

Service. The National Park Service in the Department of Interior administers the National Parks for recreational purposes. A limited amount of forestry is also practiced on the Indian Reservations.

The closely related work of tree diseases is handled by the Bureau of Plant Industry while insect enemies of the forest are studied by the Bureau of Entomology.

The Federal Forest Service has been the outstanding forestry organization in the United States. It has taken a large area of inaccessible forest land, stopped timber and grazing trespass, developed a system of roads and trails, and, in the main, has demonstrated the nature of the forestry program from a field basis. More important than the handling of 157 million acres of forest land, is the leadership the Forest Service has shown which has been reflected in the business policy of the private owners. Credit for initiating and developing fire protection is due the Forest Service. It is hoped that the private operators will follow, as well, the Forest Service's lead in silviculture and later, regulation. The most outstanding service of the Forest Service to forestry has been its research and educational work, whereby our basic knowledge of forestry has been greatly increased both professionally and from the standpoint of public opinion.

State forestry has developed concurrently with federal forest work. Each state, however, has developed its own particular program. In general, the Northeastern states have led in the development of state forest programs. With the development of state forest activity in the South during recent years, practically all the states now have some form of forestry activity. Although each year sees some development of state forestry activities, there is still a good deal of room for extension of the work. 

Municipal forestry programs are extremely limited. A few cities have forest properties for watershed facilities. Likewise, a few have developed fairly extensive city forestry organ-

izations to handle city parks.

Counties, as a rule, have little to do with forestry matters. Los Angeles county in California is an outstanding example of the possibilities in such work. 

School forests are being developed by the leading forest schools of the country. Such forests, however, are small in area and are used for demonstration, research, or instructional purposes. A few privately endowed colleges have been given areas of woodland upon which the practice of forestry offers a possibility of income. 

THE DEVELOPMENT OF INDUSTRIAL FORES TRY. The development of industrial forestry has lagged behind that of governmental forestry. 

A few individuals early developed their forest properties on a forestry basis. However, before 1920 the only semblance of real industrial forestry was found in the pulp region of the northeast. Today, a number of the larger lumbering concerns have announced the adoption of a forestry policy. In the woods, such policies may mean anything from the instigation of fire protectional activities to simple silvicultural procedures. Examples of private forest properties being regulated from a sustained yield standpoint are still uncommon. Since the nature and amount of industrial forestry which may be practiced within the next few years is very problematical, the situation is one of extreme importance to the nation and the profession of forestry.

Added to the effect of the development of agriculture and the loss by fire, is the cutting of stands for lumbering purposes. In some instances the sawmill left behind cut-over lands taken over by the farmer, but in many cases, only a heritage of stump land of little if any utility was left behind. 

THE PRESENT LAND SITUATION. At the present time, little forest land is being cleared for farm purposes. The unprofitableness of agriculture and the opportunites offered in industry have tended to cause the men to seek employment elsewhere than on the farm. 

In a like manner, the growing realization of the value of forests and the resultant efforts of forest protection has tended to reduce the losses caused by fire. Until recently, however, little has been done to stop the wasting of the land utility by the lumbering process. 

According to present figures, there are 469 million acres of forest land of which 138 million acres are in virgin timber, 81 million are devastated and worthless, and the remainder is more or less restocking by natural means with little or no effort to forest the lands scientifically. The forest growth on these cut-over areas is estimated to be about a quarter of the growth which should be secured from them. 

The ownership of the forest land is mainly private in nature. There are 127 million acres of farm wood lots and over 

242 million acres in large timberland holdings. The federal government holds approximately 89 million acres of which 81 millions are in National Forests. States, counties, and municipalities own the remaining 11 million acres.

The location of the forest land is roughly analogous to that of a doughnut, as the forest land is well distributed about the prairie region. In the East and South are found over 3492 million acres while nearly 61 million acres are found in the Rocky Mountains with 59 million acres on the Pacific Coast. It is not to be inferred, however, that the present distribution of standing timber is in the same proportions. The Eastern forests are mainly cut out and the Rocky Mountain forests are very inferior from a lumbering standpoint. The result is a heavy concentration of the present forest volume in the far West. On the Pacific Coast is practically 40% of the standing timber of the nation. 

STATUS OF THE LUMBER INDUSTRY. The forest products industries, of which lumbering is still the one of major importance, forms one of the most important units of the industrial life of our country. The distribution of the industry throughout the various states is not uniform even though some phases of the wood products industry is carried on in every state in the Union. The principal lumbering states are now found in the South and on the Pacific Coast; the Northeast is noted as the center of the pulp and paper industry; the Central and Lake States are the center for the furniture and minor wood products. 

The cutting out of any particular state has not meant the complete elimination of all lumber production in that state. 

On the contrary, many of these states maintain a sizeable production of lumber for many years after the state has lost its status of a "principal lumber producing state". The stage, how-

ever, when a state must change from a lumber exporting to a lumber importing one, is usually frought with many hardships and economic losses due to the rapid change in economic activities.. 

UNSTABILITY OF THE LUMBER INDUSTRY. In general, the lumber industry is in a very unstable condition due, mainly, to the effect of the inflated values paid for stumpage during the period of speculation. During this period the sales value of stumpage became much higher than the actual value resulting in a good share of the timberland being bought at too high a price. In such transactions the legitimate profits of the lumberman were realized by the speculator. As a result, it is claimed that there is no profit in lumbering but that all the profits were earned in timberlands.

Since the major cause of unstability of the lumber industry is found in the price paid for stumpage, it would seem that the logical step would be for the individuals concerned to write off their losses due to their unwise buying. On the contrary, however, the timberland owner endeavors to find the cause and the cure in the present situation.

Since stands of timber are not perishable except for losses by fire, insects, and disease, timberland owners can hold their property until forced to liquidate by the pressure of mounting carrying charges, This continual forced liquidation now takes the form of cutting stands before they are ready, which in turn keeps the lumber prices low and the industry in a chaotic condition..

ECONOMIC BASIS FOR PROFIT, Profit is not, except in monopolies, entirely controlled by the individual or firm. It is, roughly, the difference between the market price of the product and the cost of production. In a free and competitive market, the normal or long-time average market price is equal to the cost of production for the marginal unit. The marginal unit is known as the one which barely pays its way. 

The marginal unit, as ordinarily considered, is produced by those concerns which by reason of low quality management, low quality timber, rough land which necessitates high logging costs, inefficient equipment, or poor location, have costs of production higher than other mills. In reality, however, it seems likely that the marginal unit occurs in nearly every establishment due to the operation of the law of diminishing

If every business manager were all wise and if economic adjustments were made instantly, there would be no products made below the margin (at a cost greater than the market value). 

In practice, however, such a condition seldom exists, thereby resulting in below or sub-marginal goods being produced. Since sub-marginal goods are produced at a loss, their production tends to reduce profits and to force the failure of the sub-marginal concern. 

The lack of a perfect balance between supply and demand which causes the production of sub-marginal units, also causes the market price to fluctuate in cycles. The result is that the position of the marginal concern varies with the cycle of market price. As the price increases the marginal unit is one 

When the cycle of constantly increasing cost of production. The marginal concern thus becomes a poorer and poorer one. reverses, the opposite trend is true, thereby necessitating the elimination of the marginal concerns. This economic phenomenon is common in all fields of business. 

The perpetual imperfection in the balance of supply and demand and the consequent cycle of market values explains, in a large measure, the failure of lumber concerns and the necessity for the profits of the good years being used to offset the losses of the poor seasons. It also indicates that the economic soundness of the lumber industry cannot be measured by the average profit or loss earnings or by the number of failures.

It does indicate, however, that any concern near the margin can expect very little in the way of profits. If profits are desired in the lumber industry, as elsewhere, it behooves the entrepreneur to make sure his concern is well above the margin. 

FORCED LIQUIDATION AND THE MARGIN. Since the market value has a material effect upon the exact placement of the marginal unit, it follows that the forced liquidation of timber holdings has a decided effect on the marginal unit. The general effect is to cause a number of sub-marginal concerns to operate, thereby moving the marginal unit so as to decrease the earnings of all operators and to cause some concerns to become marginal or below when otherwise they would be slightly above margin. 

The result is, of course, disastrous to profits in the lumber industry.

These differences are reflected in the average costs of production and selling prices for the individual mills. As long as these differences are greater than the original cost of the stumpage, there need be no fear that all lumber concerns will fail to make a profit.

Forced liquidation will not result in cutting timber with a cost of production, not including stumpage, greater than the sales price as available capital is soon utilized and the concern forced out of business.

Part of this reduction in demand is due to the emergence of the United States from a pioneer country. In new communities, construction is urgently needed and as a consequence the building is mostly of a temporary nature. Wood, being the material nearest at hand and easiest to work is pressed in service in large quantities. As the country develops, the building becomes of a more permanent type, thereby reducing the demand for wood products.

First, the rapid expansion of the pulp industry into fields heretofor occupied only by lumber, thereby bringing the phases of the wood products industry into active competition with one another. Secondly, the situation calls for better marketing practice. Specialty lumber and guaranteed characteristics have appeared under the tutelage of the more progressive

concerns. The ultimate effect of specialty marketing in other fields has been to curtail the opportunity of smaller concerns, to increase the importance of the management phase of the industry, and to create consumer confidence in the product. 

SPECIALTY MARKETING AND LIQUIDATION OF LUMBER HOLDINGS. Since specialty marketing tends to take such products out of the competitive fields, it forces the non-specialty lumber to compete for the remainder of the market. The result is a lowered market value for the non-specialty product. Since a specialty product requires absolute uniformity of product and a trade mark popularized by national advertising, only the larger and more progressive concerns are able to create a specialty product. 

The smaller and less efficiently managed plants will, therefore, ultimately be faced with the stigma of producing an inferior product. The result of such a stigma is to tend toward a lower price for the non-specialty lumber. This tendency will eliminate a certain amount of the effect of forced liquidation of stumpage from the specialty product and place the burden on the smaller mills.

The effect of specialty marketing upon decreasing the amount of forced liquidation of stumpage is entirely problematical. 

It would seem that the lowering of the market value for the mills mainly concerned with liquidation would tend to curtail their activities. However, these mills are usually operated by the lesser experienced lumbermen, know less of their costs of operation, and are quite often actuated by motives of speculation; consequently, the exact effect is very difficult to estimate.

CHAPTER FIVE THE FORESTRY PROBLEM IN GENERAL 

Had the forestry problem been recognized as such a The century ago, it is likely that it would not have become as serious as it is today. By allowing the timberland question to drift, the best forest lands passed into private ownership. main objective of these owners has been "to mine" rather than "to forest" this vast acreage. The result has been the familiar trek of the sawmill across the continent until now it is centered in the last great stand of virgin timber in our country. 

Estimates of the number of years which the present supply of timber will last, vary widely. In the main, such estimates are immaterial as the question is one dealing with the most profitable action to be taken under the present situation. 

If, under present conditions, "to mine" is better than "to forest", the present policy of timber mining should be continued. 

The measuring stick to be used, however, must not be the real or fancied welfare of those who happen to own the present timberland. After all, the real ownership rests with the people as a whole. Likewise, the present so called owners are but, in reality, custodians of the property and as such should manage the tracts for the best interests of the real owners--the people as a whole.

In the United States, it is customary to allow the owner wide latitude in his use of the property. In most cases the constant balance between supply and demand will cause a fairly reasonable utility of the property to be brought about. Due to the limitations of human knowledge, it is very unlikely

that the most efficient use of all property will be secured. 

As a consequence, there is a constant movement of the market price of any commodity.

This causes a change in the amount of commodity produced because more units tend to be produced as the market price increases and less when the price drops.

In forestry, the product represents the result of fifty to a hundred or more years. It is also true that a forest crop has no definite characteristic of "ripeness" in the sense of wheat or other farm crops. It follows, therefore, that fluctuations in market prices merely cause a greater of less cut of the timber already produced. The maintenance of sufficient growing stock throughout the ages will not, therefore, result from the usual balancing of supply and demand. 

Unless some specific action is taken on the part of the timber land owner, the present practice of timber mining will eventually force a reduction in the amount of wood products consumed or the importation from abroad. The only other action possible is the deliberate attempt to grow crops of trees on forest lands. In all likelihood, the next century will see all three of these forces in operation, the exact strength of each tendency depending upon the steps taken to solve the forestry problem. 

REDUCTION IN THE AMOUNT OF FOREST PRODUCTS USED. A reduction in the amount of forest products used does not mean a sudden catastrophe. It might result in increased economic welfare but in all likelihood it would cause a slow but steady weakening of our economic structure. The process is found in every line

of economic activity and is brought about by the ever present tendency to substitute the cheaper for the more costly economic good. Thus, the young laborer replaces the old, the new model the old automobile, and so on.. 

The constant tendency to replace any present item with a better one causes the substitution of other materials for wood whenever the price of the two are such that wood giveɛ the lesser utility per unit of cost. As the price of wood products increases or as the price of its competitors decreases, the tendency will be for the competitor to replace the wood. 

Likewise, as the apparent utility of the competitors increases in respect to the apparent utility of wood,. the substitution of wood, the substitution will precede

It is not to be inferred that the substitution of a better material for wood is a pure economic gain. Any substitution requires a redistribution of wealth. In the substitution for wood, the effect is to destroy the wealth represented in the skill, knowledge, and machinery for wood products production. Since the substitution is a gradual and continuous affair, its major effects may go unnoticed; yet, over a period of time it may result in the complete ruination of one section of the country while another group profits enormously. 

The substitution of a superior article for an inferior one is ordinarily considered a progressive step. In the case of wood, the substitution is made mainly because the cost of production of wood products has increased rather than decreased in the cost of the substitute per unit of utility. Insofar as this is true, the economic gain to the country at large is

negative. The principal gain is that resulting to the individuals and regions connected with the production of the substitute material. 

Nevertheless, if the cost of production of wood products must increase, then the operation of the principle of substitution must be accepted in the best interests of the country.

The Since wood is used in a variety of ways and widely distributed, the reduction in the amount of wood used by the United States causes an enormous amount of adjustment. cost of the adjustment is necessarily large even though it is spread over a large number of people and the exact cost is very difficult to determine. Some of the general tendencies may be indicated by the trend in the Eastern part of the United States at the present time. Some of these specific tendencies are: 

1. In the general building use of lumber, the cost of a small ready-cut house increased over 200 per cent between 1915 and 1923. Such an increase indicates the growing difficulty of the poorer classes in financing a home of their own. Such classes are being forced into poorer environments and lower relative standards of living by the increased price of lumber. 

For large buildings the use of wood is being reduced to a minimum As the amount of wood used for such buildings decreases, the difficulty of substituting for the remainder increases. Wood, therefore, approaches a superior bargaining position wherein it can force a larger price to be paid for its product. The ultimate effect may mean a low rate for other materials, lower wages for the labor of construction, or a higher cost to the owners resulting in higher rents.

On the average farm, curtailment of the use of lumber means poorer buildings and fences. It, therefore, tends to decrease the desirability of farm life due to the poorer type of house and the added difficulty to the work. Stock and equipment tend to receive less protection, which adds to the cost of farming. 

3. The railroads are large users of wood for ties, cars, and buildings, Increased prices of lumber must result in increased railroad rates, decreased dividends, or reduced wages. 

More serious, however, is the effect on reducing the mileage of the railroad and the rolling stock. Lack of railroad extension results in large losses to certain sections needing the transportation facilities. Likewise, the increased cost of rolling stock tends to force a more complete utilization thereof, resulting in added storage costs to the consumer. 4. 

The wood products industries are most vitally affected, as an increase in the cost of their raw material necessarily causes an increase in the cost of their product resulting in a lessened demand. The smaller demand is generally due to the poorer classes being forced out of the market thereby decreasing the standard of living of this class. 

The growing shortage of timber also causes the small concern to be forced out of business. A shortage of timber adds to the risks of the business necessitating higher risk charges and larger inventories of raw material at the factory. The resultant higher prices call for higher quality products and efficient management. The competition between concerns increases with the shortage thereby eliminating the concerns nearest the

The increased efficiency of the remaining plants is brought about by the introduction of more machines and efficiency systems. More machines mean less laborers and consequent costly adjustment on the part of the laborers. Efficiency systems mean the making of machines of the men, the reduction of their initiative and interest in the welfare of their country, and in the forced retirement of the laborer at a younger age. The The shortage of timber also creates a speculative opportunity for those keen enough to recognize the situation. principle of substitution does not work perfectly due to the tendency of the people to use that to which they are accustomed. 

Since wood is an old established product, consumers will not ordinarily change to another product until they are sure the substitute is better. This tendency to lag allows the price of wood products to be forced higher than a competitive price with a substitute would ordinarily permit. This increase in price may be passed on to the stumpage; consequently, the speculator who buys stumpage in those species where in acute shortages are rapidly occurring can reap generous rewards at the expense of the consumer. 

Aside from the wastes in the form of machinery, skill, and knowledge which result from a general depletion of the wood products industry, there is the added loss to the security of the nation. Since wood products are highly desirable in times. of war, any nation which allows its stands to be depleted, its wood working machinery wrecked, and the skill and knowledge of woodworking on the part of the laborer dissipated, can expect

difficulty in securing the necessary supply of wood products for its defense in time of war. 

The economic considerations of the reduction in the use of wood can only indicate the tendencies. The actual amount of loss suffered by the nation is dependent upon the amount of substitution made. As long as the substitution actually results in a greater amount of utility per unit of cost, the nation ordinarily benefits.

In most cases of substitution, the substitute must advertise and otherwise pay the cost of convincing the consumer of the wiseness of the change. Under the competituve system the substitution is often pushed too far for the economic welfare of the consumer, thereby necessitating a reversal of the process of substitution. If it is necessary to substituto other materials for wood, it is desirable to keep the rate of substitution slow enough to enable the consumer to determine the proper amount of substitution to be made.

The rate of change in substitution is important not only because a slow rate tends to allow a proper amount of substitution to take place but it also allows a better amelioration of the loss to the particular individuals. Sudden economic changes always cause distress, in the main, on the part of those least able to pay.

A decrease in the rate at which other materials are substituted for wood can be secured only by decreasing the costs of production or by increasing the apparent utility of the product. 

These two items, therefore, become the crux for any successful

solution of the forestry problem.

IMPORTATION FROM ABROAD. The theory of the protective tariff is strictly against the importation of forest products as it is believed that American industries are absolutely essential to American strength. On this basis, a protective tariff on wood products is highly desirable. 

The protective tariff, however, merely results in the aggrandisement of the many by the few. A protective tariff invariably raises the price to the consumer if it prevents importations from abroad. The result of such a protective tariff is to increase the rate of substitution and to decrease the general efficiency of the economic structure of the country. The increased cost of living to the many consumers is undoubtedly greater than the increased wealth of the few lumbermen who benefit by the protective tariff.

Under a free trade policy the amount of wood products imported must depend upon the relative costs of production, The foreign importer must not only pay his own costs of production but the cost of transporting the material to the United States as well. Whenever the shortage of timber in the United 

States curtails the supply sufficiently to increase the price to a point which makes importation profitable, the American consumer will be benefited by the free trade policy. 

By the very nature of the economic structure, every nation's exports and imports must balance over a period of time. 

Therefore, the more wood imported the more of something else is exported. The policy of free trade does not, therefore, have the crippling effect of the protective tariff.

Dumping, or the selling of materials in other countries at less than the domestic price and frequently below the cost of production, is sometimes resorted to by the producers in various countries. Dumping was, in all probability, an American device growing out of our protective tariff policy. At any rate 

American producers often sell in foreign markets at less than the American price and frequently below the cost of production in either country. Some countries have tried to prevent dumping by anti-dumping laws while others have resorted to the tariff. 

In reality, dumping is not an unmixed evil. 

Dumping of foreign lumber in American markets undoubtedly curtails production by American mills and tends to unstabilize the American industry. On the other hand, the American consumer is enabled to buy his wood products at very favorable prices. The general tendency of dumping would therefore enable the average American to trade a smaller amount of time for the foreigner's products than would otherwise be true. In short, imports and exports are balanced on a basis of gold and not utility value. Dumping on American markets is an excellent means of having the workers in foreign countries enhance the wealth of our country. Unfortunately the losses are paid by a few but such a condition is not uncommon in economic phenomena. 

The necessity of an adequate supply of wood products during war times is the only real reason for protecting the lumber industry. As long as the industry is more than ample for the supplying of war products, no concern need be felt in this direction. 

It may prove undesirable to import from abroad to the

extent that any nation or group of nations may attain monopolistic control of the product. With the present wide distribution of forests and forest land such a situation is extremely unlikely.. 

THE BUSINESS OF GROWING FORESTS. Undoubtedly, a large portion of the wood products of our country will be grown on the forest lands scattered throughout the nation.. As long as wood has real utility to the consumer there will be a demand for it.. Likewise, the consumer will pay the costs of production.. 

The amount of forest growing desirable directly depends upon the cost of production.. The cheaper the wood products are, the greater the demand.. Since, in the long run, the cost of production on the margin must equal the market price, the price also determines the amount of land which may profitably be forested. As the market price increases, the marginal acre becomes poorer and the amount of land which can be used profitably for forest production increases. At the same time, the opportunity to import from abroad and the amount of substitution increases. The amount of forests produced by growth is dependent, therefore, upon the operation of the principle of substitution and the possibilities of importation from abroad.. 

For those nations which have established the business of growing crops of trees, the process of development passed through certain definite stages. During the time typified by large areas of virgin stumpage, lumbering was the dominant phase of forestry with little or no attention being paid to silviculture.

The next stage was typified by large areas of

cut-over land, lumbering becoming a secondary phase of forestry with silviculture the dominant one. The final stage is one typified by a balance of growth with the cut; silviculture and lumbering being but two essential phases of the forestry operation. 

At the present time the United States is at the end of the first stage. If the nation is to maintain itself as a timber producing country, it will be necessary to develop the practice of silviculture on a large scale.

CHAPTER SIX THE FOREST LAND PROBLEM 

The forest land problem is that problem which is concerned with securing the maximum perpetual use of the forest lands of the country. The solution of the problem demands that forest lands be placed on a sustained yield basis by having the amount cut equal to the amount grown, that good silvicultural. technique be practiced, and that the species grown be the ones best adapted to the areas from a standpoint of markets as well as from the silvicultural viewpoint. 

CLASSIFICATION OF LANDS. Forest lands may be defined as those lands which will produce a greater net income when utilized for forestry purposes than when utilized for any other purpose. 

From a commercial standpoint the income must be measured in net profits to the owner, while from a governmental viewpoint it may be measured upon a basis of the welfare accruing to the people. 

Certain uses of the forest, such as recreation, are extremely important to the welfare of the people but are usually valueless from the viewpoint of profit to the private owner. In some 

European countries this difference between public and privato interests has resulted in special regulation of certain forest lands by the government. 

The selection of the specific areas of land to be utilized for forest production is very difficult and cannot be made with any degree of accuracy by the information now at hand. 

It is commonly thought that forest land is any land which cannot be successfully farmed for agricultural crops. 

The poorer and rougher lands are, therefore, forest lands. On this basis

it is proposed to classify the lands of the nation into forest and non-forest lands. Such a classification, even if accurately made, would in all probability be very erroneous as it eliminates entirely many economic considerations which very materially affect the classification of lands. 

Land may be termed the nation's basic resource. The use to which the lands of a country are put determines the economic goods produced by that nation. To classify lands upon a basis of mere physical characteristics, therefore, practically eliminates from consideration the desires of the people for given products. 

Economic goods are analogous to the ingredients of a stew. The more goods the more stew available for distribution to the consumer. The exact flavor of the economic stew depends, of course, upon the relative proportions of the different economic goods produced. On this basis, the American people should utilize only such lands for wheat as will adequately satisfy their appetite for wheat. It would be folly to utilize a large percentage of the land area for purposes of transportation, yet adequate transportation is essential to the economic welfare of the country. In forestry, as in all other activities, the exact quantity and placement of forest land should depend upon the desire of the people for the products of the forest. 

The problem of land classification is not solved by assigning agriculture or any other use prior rights to all the good land and giving to the other economic goods only such crumbs as may fall from the table. True land classification endeavors

to assign the available land so that the total land area will supply the consumers with the desired quantities of each economic good at the cheapest price. On this basis, forestry merely is one means of using land and the particular areas assigned for its use depend upon the amount of forest products desired by the consumer in reference to other products and to the ability of the various areas to produce the desired economic goods. The proper balance between land use and the amount of each economic good consumed, therefore, becomes the only basis upon which a true classification of lands can be made. 

Under the laissez-faire theory, the desire of the consumer for any particular economic good is left to the individual to decide. It is thought that enlightened selfish interest will cause each individual to use only that which is for his best interest. As a consequence, the demand for any given good depends upon the ability of that good to satisfy economic wants and the balance between supply and demand as represented in the market price is the device which causes the proper amounts of all goods to be produced. On this basis, any area of land may be used to produce any or all of the economic goods desired. It is recognized that differences between location and physical properties of the land will give some areas advantages over others in the production to a certain economic good. As a consequence, lands vary in value.

According to the law of differential rent, the value of any particular area of land depends upon the difference between the cost of production on that area and the cost of pro-

duction on the marginal land. By applying this law to each land use, it is found that the value of any tract for wheat is equal to the difference between the cost of growing wheat on the tract and on the marginal wheat acre. In a like manner, its value for corn, oats, roads, factories, forests, or what not, is determined. 

It also follows that es each economic good is in competition with every other economic good, the value of the land may be used as the basis of economic classification. 

Forest land, following the law of differential rent, is that land whose value is greatest when computed upon a basis of forest production. that land whose value is greatest when computed on a basis of its production of Douglas fir. The law of differential rent, therefore, explains the classification of lands into forest lands and the division of the forest lands among the various species. Likewise, marginal land is marginal for one particular use only. Marginal forest lands are composed of marginal Douglas fir, cedar, hemlock, oak, etc., land. It also follows that submarginal wheat land might be above marginal forest land and that below marginal pine land might be super marginal fir land. 

Unfortunately, economic values are extremely difficult In a like manner, Douglas fir land is to ascertain and are in a constant state of change. As a result, the lands of a nation are seldom accurately classified especially when the use of a given tract depends upon the judgment of the owner--a judgment often based more on whims and fancies than upon knowledge and reason. Likewise, land use is slow to change with the shift in economic conditions. Thus, the

changing American diet and the replacement of animals by the gas engine should cause a rapid realignment in the use of land; such, however, is not the case as a part of the land use is firmly planted in custom, accentuated by ignorance. 

Theoretically all shifts in land use should take place on the marginal acres. The curtailment of the production of hay necessitated by the introduction of the gas engine should result in the elimination of the marginal hay acres. In the readjustment these marginal acres might prove to be above marginal for some other use, thereby forcing a shift in the marginal acreage elsewhere.

These economic changes are slow but almost certain to occur unless prevented by governmental action. 

The marginal forest acre will probably react less favorably to any change in use than will other marginal acres. 

The average rotation for forest crops may be measured in decades. 

A shift of land use which is not carried out over the rotational period will necessitate a sacrifice of values in the growing stock already produced. In the many aged type of stand such a shift will be very difficult to make. Changing of forest land to other uses also necessitates the removal of stumps and other growth, thereby making the shift more difficult. It is extremely likely, therefore, that sub-marginal forest land will be liquidated over a period of time, thereby reducing the profits on all forest land during the period of adjustment. 

From a production standpoint, marginal and submarginal lands are a liability. It is also likely that the consumer would benefit by a more orderly shift. Controlled production would, of course, eliminate most of the losses of the

changes made necessary by a shift in the demand for economic goods. 

Controlled production can be brought about by private or governmental means. Private control of production invariably results in higher prices to the consumer without a corresponding increase in utility. Governmental control is of doubtful value due to the tendency of a republican government to react to pressure exerted by organized minorities. 

Controlled consumption is likewise undesirable. If in the form of laws, it represents an undue curtailment of private liberty on the part of the government. If in the form of custom, it represents an undue stifling of personal freedom and of progress.

In the long run, the only feasible solution of the problem of land classification is based upon scientific information. Under presnet conditions, production is determined by the accident of the presence of capital funds and not necessarily by the demand. Likewise, consumption is not based on utility value in the main, but on emotion and custom. In formation which allows the consumer to buy on a basis of utility value or allows the producer to determine accurately the needs of the consumer will do much to ameliorate the difficulties encountered by marginal concerns. The marginal forest land situation, like other marginal units, is likely to be a permanent cancer on the economic structure. 

UNSTABLE FOREST LAND OWNERSHIP. The lands ordinarily con sidered as forest lands are those now devoted to that purpose plus those which have been logged but which have not been utilized

for purposes other than forestry. The richer and better located of these lands are held by private individuals. 

By reason of their area, location, and quality these lands will be the ones to produce the major portion of any forests which are economically grown in the United States. 

Of the 127 million acres of forest land in farm woodlots very few are adequately managed. The woodlot is a secondary unit of the general farm. As such, it receives only such care as is available after the more important phases of the farm are handled. Technical information of woodlot management is often lacking on the part of the farm owner. This results in an inferior wood production rate and in poor marketing of the woodlot's products. The productivity of the farm woodlots of the country is, therefore, below par. The solution of the situation necessitates the education of the many woodlot owners to the point where efficient silviculture and marketing is practiced. 

The commercial or industrial forest area of 242 million acres is mainly in large private holdings. These lands have been consistently handled on a basis of timber mining with the result that ownership of cut-over lands is very unstable. Tax delinquency on such lands is extremely high. About a third of the cut-over land has had its economic value for forest purposes completely destroyed by recurring fires. Little attention is given to the cut-over lands by the owners. 

FOREST DEVASTATION. 

Forest devastation has followed the trek of the "timber miner" across the contine it. Whenever cut-over land was converted into farm land, the policy of timber mining resulted in the economic development of the country. In other

cases, it resulted in waste and devastation. 

Forest devastation destroys not only the tax and wealth production of the forest land but its other effects are far reaching. Towns whose sole support rests on lumbering become mere ghost towns when the mill cuts its last log. Businesses and professional practices are destroyed. Property values melt. 

Civic improvements become worthless and the bonds used to defray the cost of their construction become valueless. The citizens of the town must make the costly readjustments necessary to find economic employment elsewhere. Nearby farmers, due to the loss of their market, become sub-marginal and are forced to move. Taxes drop and the relative costs of government rise. 

The mill owner loses the difference between the wrecked and going value of his concern. The whole process is an economic loss and usually an unnecessary one. 

The causes of forest devastation and unstable cutover land ownership are poor forest protection, unfair taxation, and custom.

Poor forest protection, especially from fire, is responsible for repeated burning of cut-over areas thereby preventing natural reproduction from becoming permanently established. 

Poor fire protection is also the cause of the almost complete lack of timberland insurance. As a result the timber grower cannot easily hedge his possible fire losses. Due to lack of adequate fire records a statistical measurement of risk is not available. Estimates of risk by individuals are naturally tempered with their observations. 

Since the extraordinary rather

than the average is usually observed, fire risk is apt to be over estimated unless based upon actual records of loss. 

Unfair taxation through the operation of the general property tax, places a prohibitory tax upon the timber grower Insofar as both the land and the crop are taxed under such a levy, the taxing body takes a good share of the value of the crop. 

Under such a condition the profitableness of forest growing becomes doubtful.

Custom is merely repeating the acts of our forebears. 

It is an enemy to progress; yet, it is wise business practice to follow the customary procedure until some other action is proven better. 

It is not to be expected, therefore, that timberland owners will suddenly desert the established order of timber mining and invest heavily in timber growing. Changes in procedure are considered visionary when first suggested; however, progress demands that new ideas be given a thorough consideration by the leaders. Likewise, the American consumer has every right to expect the timberland holder to investigate, in a businesslike manner, the possibilities of sustained yield for his particular tract. 

It is estimated that about 4% of the large private forest holdings are in the hands of concerns practicing forestry. 

A large share of this acreage, however, has practically no real silviculture. At the best, only a little regulation and rudimentary silviculture are practiced, Since this progress toward scientific forestry has largely occurred after 1920, it indicates a change from the old viewpoint on the part of these

particular timberland owners.

The timberland owners of the older school still persist in their belief that sustained yield never pays. Their desire is to be left to finish. the timber mining of their lands as of yore. 

Since timber mining is against the economic welfare of the country and since it invariably leaves the cut-over lands in such a condition that silvicultural practice is extremely costly, it would appear that some action is necessary to stabilize the ownership of cut-over lands. 

THE PUBLIC'S INTEREST IN PRIVATE FOREST LAND. 

According to the Malthusian doctrine of population, the maximum number of people in any country is limited to the amount of food which may be produced to feed them. With the development of industrialism and the resultant higher standards of living, it is apparent that the welfare of the population of a progressive country demands that the population be limited much more than that indicated by the food supply theory of Malthus. It is true that the essentials of life such as food, must receive prior considerations provided there is a shortage of land. However, once the requirements for bare necessities of life are met, more production of these items merely results in waste. The utility of food and most bare necessities rapidly decreases if more than an adequate supply is available. It follows, therefore, that once the necessities are produced, added effort should be placed in the production of other types of economic goods. The more of these kinds of goods produced the higher the standard of living. 

Since land is the fundamental resource, any nation which

allows its lands to lie idle or to be wasted in curtailing the possible prosperity of its people. 

Forest devastation and unstable forest land ownership is therefore detrimental to the best interests of the people as a whole. Good government requires that such action be stopped. The longer timber mining is practiced, the more the idle and wasted land accruing to hinder the future prosperity of the country.

CHAPTER SEVEN THE WOOD PRODUCTS PROBLEM 

The wood products problem is that which is concerned. with supplying the American consumer with adequate wood products. At the present time, the nation is the largest consumer of forest products in the world. On a per capita basis, however, Finland and Canada use a greater amount than does the United States. The per capita consumption for the United States is well over eight times as large as that of the countries of central Europe.

THE AMOUNT OF WOOD PRODUCTS NEEDED The amount of wood pro ducts needed in the United States cannot be ascertained by comparison with that of any other country. The welfare of a people depends upon the amount of goods used. The more goods consumed the higher the standard of living maintained. Permanent welfare demands, of course, that production be so organized as to maintain the necessary rate of production perpetually. 

The quantity desired of any particular product must depend upon the ability of that product to satisfy human wants. 

Thus, a quantity of food above that necessary for the proper feeding of the people has a very low utility. Wood, on the other hand, is that type of product which has no particular limit to the amount desired. In short, the more wood used higher the standard of living. the It must not be supposed, however, that wood should be used whenever possible. Economic goods are many and varied; consequently, the standard of living depends upon the total amount

of all economic goods used. Likewise, there is one particular proportion which will give the greatest satisfaction of human wants at the least cost. The amount of wood used must depend upon its ability to satisfy human wants in comparison with other goods. The amount of wood needed is set, therefore, by the operation of the principle of substitution. 

The principle of substitution does not work with equal force in all the fields of wood use. Those fields in which the properties of wood make it the most superior material available indicate but a slight operation of the principle whereas it operates widely whenever the wood product poorly fits its economic use.

For Due to the fact that wood is readily shaped and fastened into a rigid form, it is extremely important for temporary uses of all sorts. Pioneer communities, therefore, use more wood relatively than do those which have been well established. this reason, the presence of a plentiful supply of wood is greatly desired in a rapidly growing country. As the nation becomes better established, its dependence on wood becomes less due to the fact that more time is available to fashion goods from the materials more difficult to use, of man.

In many cases, this appeal results in the widespread use of economic goods that actually have a negative value. The true value is, therefore, secondary to the

Since the demand for most economic goods is one inflated by custom or other emotional appeal, the demand for wood, as well as for its competitors, may be increased almost without limit. The ultimate utility of wood consequently depends, in the main, upon the ever-changing viewpoint of the consumer. Re ligious and other social customs therefore become the keystones to much of the utility value of wood products. 

Since the desire for wood depends upon the thought of the consumer, it follows that it can be increased by proper and adroit means. In the same way the desire for other economic goods can be increased. In this manner, wood is in constant competition with all other economic goods except those necessary to sustain life.

The demand for wood depends upon the amount of wealth available to satisfy that desire. The mere desire for wood does not result in its use by the consumer until money is available to pay for the cost of securing it. Naturally the more accumulated wealth of a nation, the more the amount which is available for the use of wood products. The actual amount of the available funds which may be used for wood products depends upon the strength of desire for the various products. If the apparent utility of wood is high, a larger share of the available funds will be diverted to wood products than in cases where the utility value of wood is low.

Since the desires of the consumer are always greater than his ability to buy, each consumer can satisfy only a portion

of his desires. His objective is to get the greatest satisfaction with the available funds. Low prices of wood therefore increase the number of possible buyers. In this manner, the quantity of wood used is dependent upon the price in reference to the apparent utility. 

THE PRICE OF WOOD PRODUCTS The values imputed to wood as well as to other products, vary from day to day. For any particular transaction, the price depends upon the relative bargaining position of the buyer and seller and may have little connection with the cost of production. For the long-time normal price, however, there is a strong tendency for it to equal the cost of production of the marginal concern. 

The costs of production are those that result from the use of labor and knowledge, capital, and land. Wages, or the returns to labor, are set at a point which gives the capitalist the greatest return when virtually all the labor is given employment. The wood products must, therefore, pay a wage in keeping with the general wage scale of the nation. The returns to labor must be real rather than visionary as they are used for actual living expenses. In a like manner, the returns to capital must be somewhat in keeping with the returns to capital throughout the world.

Certain forms of capital are very mobile and will will not be invested in wood products production unless adequate rewards are expected. The returns to capital, however, are often visionary rather than real. It is the expectation of profit, rather than the actual profit earned, which decides its placement. Theoretically, the return to land is equal to the difference between the cost of production on the particular tract

and the cost of production on the marginal tract. In actual practice, the returns to land, capital, and labor depend upon the relative bargaining power of the individuals concerned. 

A superficial analysis of the costs of production would indicate that the whole question was tied up with the general economic status of the nation. Such a condition is true only insofar as the mobility of labor, capital, and use of land influence the situation. On the other hand, the quality of management has a decided effect upon the costs of production. Efficient production and consumption are the keys to lower costs and therefore represent the means whereby the use of wood products may be increased or decreased.

EFFICIENT CONSUMPTION The low efficiency in the consumption of wood products is indicated by the fact that only about a quarter of the material on the acreage logged is utilized by the consumer. To this waste must be added that caused by unwise consumption and loss by fire, insects, or disease.

The wastes occur in every step of the lumbering process. A large amount of material is left in the woods by the logger. Wastes of manufacture and storage increase the amount. 

Wastes caused by ignorance or carelessness of the ultimate consumer added to the losses of fire and other destructive agencies complete the picture. At the present time, it is not feasible to eliminate all waste due to the fact that the cost of placing much of the material on the market is greater than its apparent utility value. However, it is estimated by experts that two-ninths of the annual drain on the forest could be saved by methods now known to be feasible and that this saving could be doubled through research and experimentation. 

Waste lowers the utility value of the forest and adds to the per unit costs of production. In every respect it is an economic loss. The causes of waste in forest industries may be summed up as follows: 

1. Forced liquidation. Forced liquidation tends to lower the market price, thereby making it profitable only to log the better logs and trees. The amount of material which is un-

merchantable is, therefore, increased.

The principle causes of forced liquidation are found in the increasing costs for taxes and protection and in poor financing on the part of certain individual owners.

Taxation, in America, is habitually based on the general property tax. As the costs of government increase, the taxes necessarily rise. Likewise, any material and sudden reduction of taxes on virgin timberlands would, in all probability result in chaos for the local governments which have planned their financial program upon the present basis of taxation, Insofar as the timberland holder is a speculator rendering the economic utility of paying the annual costs of carrying the property, there is no justification for a reduction of timberland taxes. In fact, liquidation of timber holdings is allow ing the owner to escape paying a portion of the tax on his holdings which the governing bodies have a right to expect. Liquidation is, therefore, subservient to public welfare in that it tends to increase waste and to lower the ultimate amount of taxes paid by the timberland holder.

Certain phases of timberland taxation are manifestly unfair to the owner, The practice of assessing timberlands at a higher ratio than other property unfairly increases the share paid by the timberland owner. Lack of accurate cost records and of precise technical

knowledge has resulted in many costly mistakes of production. 

The cost data and the necessary technical knowledge are seldom available to allow most concerns to know and to recognize the material which can be profitably used. The whole process of production is based upon judgment, often with no better tools than a keen brain and superficial information. 

For this reason, much merchantable material is wasted because it cannot be recognized. 

As in other businesses, there are progressive and unprogressive units. However, there is one outstanding viewpoint which is common throughout the woods industries and the consumers of wood products, namely a belief that wood is an inferior material. This erroneous viewpoint is responsible for a good deal of useless waste throughout the industry. Frugality should require that the value of individual pieces of wood be placed on a utility basis and not be unduly influenced by an inferiority complex.

THE SOLUTION OF THE FOREST PRODUCTS PROBLEM The solution of the forest products problem requires the development of the apparent utility of wood products and the decreasing of the costs of production. An increase in the apparent utility of wood results only from the education of the consumer by advertising and other means. A decrease in the cost of production results from better management, better training of the workers, better technique based on exact knowledge, and better marketing. 

Better marketing results not only from a reduction of distribution costs but from the standardization of the products and the development of grading rules and products which allow the consumer to get a product which fits his needs.

CHAPTER EIGHT THE FOREST RECREATION PROBLEM 

The forest recreation problem is closely connected with the general outdoor recreational problem. Hunting, fishing, camping, and hiking are often directly allied with a forest environment. Scenic attractions to the tourist include tree covered mountains and forest lined highways. Likewise, nature enthusiasts invariably find the forest excellent for their wants. 

Forest recreation is of an exceptionally high quality. 

It is healthful from the standpoint of physical exercise and pure air. It is clean morally. It emphasizes the fundamentals of life and does not tend to create radical ideas. Finally, its effects tend to extend over a long period of time as the interests aroused occupy the mind long after the actual trip in the woods.

Unfortunately, forest recreation is limited to those who have the time and money to enable them to take their recreation in fairly large doses. The distance of the forest from the centers of population necessitates a considerable travel expense for the average recreationist. Likewise, it cannot be taken whenever desired, for a short period of time, or for the price of the average moving picture show. The privilege of a lark in the woods is only for those who have the ability to plan and save for such recreation. 

The amount of forest recreation is enormous. The national parks are visited by millions every season. The 

National Forests have experienced a rapid and constent increase in the number of people which use the National Forest lands for

recreational purposes. In addition, private timberland holdings have been used consistently for recreational purposes by large numbers of individuals. 

The development of the automobile and of good roads has increased not only the tourist traffic in forested states but has presented the opportunity of forest recreation to a multitude of individuals. Active agitation has developed for state parks and for the plan of leaving strips of trees along the roadsides. Forest recreation has emerged from the stage of individual desire to a stage of economic importance to the forested states. 

FORESTRY AND FOREST RECREATION To the forester, recreation is one of the important products of the forest. Its value to the nation is large; yet, it is difficult to express that value in dollars as the economic advantages do not accrue to the forest owner, in the main, but to those who furnish the forest recreationalist with supplies. 

At times, recreation seriously conflicts with other uses of the forest. Hikers dislike the dirt, noise, and smell of grazing. Herbivorous animals, particularly deer, use forage which might otherwise be utilized by sheep or cattle. 

The interest of the fisherman is often adverse to hydro-electric development or the use of the water for irrigation. Likewise, the lumberman, by cutting the forest, invariably destroys its recreational value. 

Recreation is responsible for a large increase in the cost of fire protection. The recreationalist is probably responsible for about a third of the fires started. In general,

recreationalist fires cause a damage below the average; consequently, they are not as important as the number would in- Certain types of recreational fires, as camper's dicate. fires, are highly concentrated thereby decreasing the cost of protection. On the other hand, some recreationalist fires, as hunter's fires, are widely scattered with a resultant increase in the costs.

The recreationalist is rapidly gaining a vested right in American timberland. Due to the large area and difficulty of patrol it is too costly for the owner of a large block of timberland to bring the trespass laws to his assistance. The fish and game laws definitely limit the timberland owners control of his property. Finally, the nature of forest recreation makes it difficult for the land owner to realize in a financial way or to secure public backing for any measure not in keeping with the welfare of forest recreation. In short, the timberland owner is taxed to build roads which are useless to him but which add to his fire risk and losses. In addition, pressure is exerted to force the owner to leave strips of trees along the roads thereby increasing the logging costs of his tract. 

Although forest recreation is not in keeping with the financial interests of the private owner, its value to the people of the nation makes it desirable that it be adequately developed. Since private profits are not, as a rule, obtainable, forest recreation must be developed by governmental agencies. Public parks and other recreational features will do much to alleviate the losses of the timberland owner. Educational measures to teach the public the proper use of the

forest are always in order. Likewise, it might prove fair to divert a portion of the funds derived from hunting and fishing licenses to fire protection, particularly as forest fires tend to destroy the game and the fish.

CHAPTER NINE THE PROTECTIONAL FOREST PROBLEM 

The use of the forest for protectional purposes is not uncommon in Europe. In the United States little has been accomplished along this line. The prevention of shifting sand and erosion together with the maintenance of a uniform run-off of water is highly desirable. Watershed protection for the purposes of flood prevention, hydro-electric developments, irrigation, or city water supply is of growing importance. Like forest recreation, the use of the forest for protectional purposes has large values to the public at large but little value to the individual forest owner. 

The policy of timber mining is in direct conflict with the use of the forest for protection. In fact, any lands necessary for protectional purposes must be lumbered under a system of silviculture which is more costly than ordinary clear cutting. 

Since the proper silvicultural technique is paramount on such areas, certain European countries have found it advisable to designate areas as protection forests and to require the cutting on such areas to be in accordance with the protectional use of the forest. Other conflicts of interest arise from the effect of grazing and recreation upon the purity of water for consumption purposes, and from grazing animals interfering with the best operation of ditches.

Since the private owner cannot secure incomes from his protectional resource, action deemed advisable must be taken by governmental agencies. 

The location of the National Forests in the mountains in the West is extremely fortunate from a protectional standpoint.

On such lands the proper protection of watersheds for hydroelectric development, irrigation, and city watersheds is assured. 

In the Appalachians, the gradual purchase of lands for National forests may eventually solve the general problem in that region. 

However, there are some areas, some of which are quite extensive, which are now in urgent need of protection. It is these areas which need immediate attention because the longer they are left without the protective covering of forests, the faster and greater the damage occurs.

The experience of France in its reboisment work in the mountains and in preventing shifting sands along the coast is sufficient argument for the feasibility of such work on similar areas in the United States. The work ahead is obvious. Areas needing protection should be determined upon a basis of value and the silvicultural requirements complied with. The exact governmental agency to finance the work must be determined in the field of politics.

CHAPTER TEN HISTORICAL THE PRESENT FOREST POLICY 

The actual beginnings of a definite national forest policy cannot be placed before the formation of the Division of Forestry in the Department of Agriculture in 1885. Little was done by this bureau except the work of educating the public in forestry. In 1891, a law repealing the pre-emption and timber culture laws had a rider attached giving the President power to set aside and reserve suitable forestry lands. Under this law, the Western National Forest lands were set aside mainly by 

Presidents Cleveland and Roosevelt.

The new reserves were placed under the administration of the Department of Interior and administered under a policy which did not permit use of the areas. In 1905, the forest reserves were placed under the jurisdiction of the Bureau of Forestry in the Department of Agriculture and their names changed to National Forests. The reasons given for the transfer were: 

1. The scientific forestry work was done in the Department of Agriculture. 

2. The auxiliary sciences, as plant and animal industry, were in that department. 

3. The Department of Agriculture understands the science of crop production. Forests, being a crop, belong in that department.

STATE FORESTRY The activities of the various states differ considerably. Most states having considerable forest land within their borders, have a state forestry organization of some sort. 

The most common type of activity has to do with fire protectional work on the private lands. Funds received under the Clark-McNary law result in a certain amount of federal regualtion of state activity in this field.

Forestry practice is promoted through the passage of special forest land tax laws and the distribution of planting stock and advice.

Some states, particularly New York and Pennsylvania, have developed rather extensive state forests and state forest

The activities of the states in forestry is constantly being increased. In the main, such activities are hampered by lack of adequate appropriations. Likewise, they are greatly influenced by the appropriation of federal money with the proviso that the states match the funds and expend the total for the purposes indicated.

SUMMARY The two major objectives to be accomplished by the American forester are the stabilization of forest land ownership and the securing of closer utilization of forest products. The means of accomplishing these objectives are debatable, particularly as the advantages of public and private forestry are largely unknown.

CHAPTER ELEVEN A POLICY OF PRIVATE PROPERTY 

The policy of private property is the fundamental one of the United States. Under such a policy the private owner is given a maximum of control over his property on the theory that the best interests of the country as a whole are in keeping with the best interests of its individual members. Since each individual is largely actuated by selfish interest, governmental interference can do no good and may result in harm. Each individual should, therefore, be allowed to do as he pleases with his property as long as he does not materially injure his neighbors. 

It is claimed that the theory does not work perfectly due to a lack of complete knowledge on the part of each individual as to his particular self-interest. Governmental activity is necessary, therefore, to educate the ignorant and to protect the weak from the aggrandisement of the s trong. The functions of government are, therefore, limited to the protection of the public welfare, the protection of the individual and his property, and the promotion of beneficial activity through indirect and static means. 

Under the private property theory, the private timberland owner will do that which is most profitable for him, As not pay. a consequence, sustained yield is not practiced because it does Likewise, the wastefulness of the timber industry is caused by the fact that the unused material has no positive value. According to this theory, the solution of the forestry problem will occur whenever conditions are such as to "make forestry pay." The desirable action, therefore, lies in measures

designed to reduce costs of production or to increase market values. Profit becomes the keystone of the entire problem. Private The fallacy of the entire theory lies in the assumption that the public interest never conflicts with private interest and that the American lumberman is a super-man in his knowledge of the wisest action to pursue. Nevertheless, the half-truths presented in behalf of private property appear sound on the surface. It should be remembered, however, that the public interest lies in keeping all its lands productive and in securing the maximum of forest products at minimum of costs. profits are justifiable only because they result in the most efficient production. In short, it may be that the most efficient way to secure the proper handling of the nation's woodpile is to pay adequately for the work. Judging from the average rate of profits in all businesses in the United States, it is not the actual profits earned but the expectation of earning profits that actuates the American business man. Profits in forestry need only be in keeping with profits in other lines of business of similar risks.

The measures proposed for the solution of the forestry problem under the private property theory are designed to increase the private welfare of the private investor in forest The more common measures advocated have been subproperties. sidies in forest protection, taxation reform, limitation of the importation of lumber, and control of the amount of lumber produced.

PROTECTION Certain authorities have stated that protection is

seventy-five per cent of the forestry problem. Although such a statement is undoubtedly fallacious, protection is important as it reduces the risks of the forestry enterprise and the losses sustained. Efficient protection, therefore, tends to reduce materially the costs of production as it reduces the costs of risk and losses due to fire, insects, and disease. No quarrel can be found with the objectives of protection. The main questions concern the source of the funds and the control of the expenditure thereof.

Morally the The protection bill must be paid by the timberland owner, either in the form of losses or expenditures, unless the governmental agencies choose to contribute. timberman may expect a certain amount of aid from the public because the public insists on using the forests for certain purposes and in carelessly starting fires on private timberlands. However, the amount of public funds allotted to forest protection does not depend upon a moral obligation as much as upon the amount of political pressure available to secure them. 

Since public funds are being allotted to forest protection, the public has a right to expect a corresponding return in value. Public interest lies largely in the protection of cut-over areas, as such protection tends to prevent the devastation of such lands and to insure a future forest supply. 

On the other hand, the private timberland owner is mainly interested in protecting the virgin timber as such areas represent his greatest values. Because of this situation, the expenditure of public funds for fire protection should be under the con-

trol of a public agency not susceptible to the political pressure of the timbermen.

TAXATION REFORM The general property tax, under which the timberland is habitually taxed, operates upon the principle of a uniform rate for all property upon a basis of value. 

The value of each piece of property is set by a local assessor. If the assessing of all land is accurate, each tract will be taxed in accordance with its value. The correctness of the assessment therefore determines the fairness of the tax.

Reforms are necessary because, in operation, the tax is unfair to the timberland owner. In virgin timber, the local road and school districts have been gerrymandered to the detriment of the timberland owner. In a like manner, the assessments on timber properties are apt to be relatively higher than for other types, particularly if the timberland owner is not a resident of the local community. In addition, tax comes annually whereas the timberland holder does not ordinarily have an annual income from which to pay the tax. 

The owner of virgin timberland would prefer to pay an income or yield tax at the time of cutting as such a tax would come at a time of ready money for him. Likewise, it would tend to prevent forced liquidation and shift some of the risks of holding the stumpage onto the governmental agencies. 

On the other hand, a yield tax on virgin timber would wreck the financial basis for the local political units and would be unfair to the other taxpayers who are not given the privilege

of shifting the risks of poor crops or low incomes from their properties. 

General reforms in the taxation of virgin timberland are hardly to be expected. The timberland holder, as a speculator, serves society by paying the taxes and other annual carrying charges. No reform in taxes is, therefore, desirable unless the timberland owner is excessively taxed in proportion to other taxpayers. The matter of over-assessment and gerrymandering road and school districts is a local problem and must be handled as such. Railroads have solved the same situation by employing special tax agents. Perhaps the timberland owners may well emulate their example. To advocate a reform in virgin timberland taxation as a means of preventing forced liquidation is using taxation for non-revenue producing reasons. Such a course is poor taxation and usually foolhardy economics. 

Cut-over and growing forests, however, are on a different basis than are virgin timberlands. The owner of such lands is not a speculator holding the stumpage for a future increase in value but, on the other hand, he is growing a crop of trees. Unlike the farmer, the forester does not produce an annual crop nor does he always have an annual income with which to pay the tax. As the general property tax now operates the land and the crop are assessed resulting in a constantly increasing tax until the crop is cut when the tax drops to the bare land value. The situation is analogous to the taxing of colts and heifers before they are old enough to produce.

Obviously, the major reform necessary is the assessing of the land on a basis of bare land only. Since timberland owners have been none too fairly treated by local assessors in the past, they prefer to have cut-over lands valued by a state assessor. 

Naturally, such a division of the assessing function is fraught with trouble from an administrative and political standpoint. 

The second controversial point deals with the time of paying the tax. It is apparent than an annual tax requires the payment at a time when most forest growers have no income from which to pay the tax. A yield tax at the time of cutting is, therefore, highly desirable from their standpoint. Such a tax, however, shifts a certain amount of the risk of growing a crop to the political units and consequently is somewhat unfair to the other taxpayers. Likewise, it tends to produce an intermittent income to the governmental units, thereby disrupting their fiscal arrangements. In the final analysis, the real need for a yield tax is limited to the first rotation. 

After the first rotation the forest should be a sufficiently normal one that annual income is available to pay the tax. 

As a consequence, it would seem to be the wiser action to tax the forest land annually and thereby keep the taxation system uniform for all classes of property. 

Taxation reforms for cut-over lands have followed the 

In Oregon, political expedient of getting what is available. for instance, a careful survey of the tax problem was made by the U. S. Forest Service. Before any results or recommendations were available, a reforestation tax law was passed.

Under such conditions the tax reforms are mere political compromises, often giving the timberland owner an unfair advantage over the other taxpayers. 

The most recent tendency in forest tax laws for cutover lands consists of a low annual tax and a yield tax at the time of cutting. In order to assure permanency, such taxes necessitate a contract between the owner and the state and as such are of doubtful constitutionality. 

In addition, such a tax must favor the forest owners or they will decline to accept the system. As a consequence, the state may give away a valuable privilege to a special interest without an adequate public return.

Reforms in taxes for cut-over lands are fairly easy to make due to the high percentage of tax delinquency for these lands. Local taxing bodies are, therefore, inclined to take something rather than receive nothing. Changes in the tax structure, however, should be carefully made as any readjustment later will be costly and difficult. 

LIMITATION OF IMPORTATION OF LUMBER The obvious effect of artificially limiting the importation of lumber is to raise the price to the consumer, thereby changing the position of the marginal unit and increasing the profits to the lumberman. 

In all likelihood, it would also increase the production of American lumber, thereby increasing the work available in the lumber sections. Undoubtedly, profits in timberland holding and in lumbering would increase. 

It is very doubtful if such action is for the best

interest of the country as a whole. The American consumer would be forced to pay more for his product and thereby cause the principle of substitution to operate. It is very doubtful if wages to the laborer would be increased as the extra work in the lumber industry would result in placing the labor in less efficient production from a utility standpoint. In short, the country would enjoy more utility from its labor by using the same labor in producing goods to which it is best adapted and trading these goods for the foreign lumber. 

Local communities would eventually suffer due to the effect of sudden diminution of taxable wealth with the cutting out of the virgin stands. The slower the rate of cutting of virgin timberland, the greater the tax collected from the timberland holder. In fact, the best interests of local com munities lie in curtailing production to a point where lumbering will be permanent. To allow rapid timber mining is to trade valuable permanent values for the glitter of temporary wealth, Little can be said for a protective tariff on lumber except that it enhances the profits of the investors in that field. This is a matter of no particular concern to anyone except those individuals concerned. Laws to prevent the dumping of timber have the same effect as the tariff in that thay benefit the lumberman at the expense of the consumer. 

Their tendency to stabilize lumber production, however, makes them somewhat more desirable than a protective tariff. 

CONTROL OF AMOUNT OF LUMBER PRODUCED Efforts to stabilize the lumber industry by curtailing the cut has apparently resulted

in a gentleman's agreement among certain mills to operate on a reduced schedule of production. Such agreements are usually readily broken; consequently, the plan seldom has a lasting influence. If the Sherman Anti-trust law were repealed, such agreements could be made in the form of an enforceable contract. 

Proposals are being made by certain lumbermen for governmental control of production, a proposal, incidentally, not intended to result in forced closer utilization or silvicultural practices. The advantages given for controlled production are: 

1. It stabilizes the market and eliminates the speculative profits due to price fluctuations. 

2. It tends to promote closer utilization and thus allow a large amount of material now wasted to become useful. 

3. It stabilizes the lumber industry and therefore prevents a seasonal employment of wage earners. 

In the final analysis, the advantages of controlled production, which in turn is price control by indirect means, are largely fictitious. It is probably true that a stabilized market tends to eliminate speculative profits. However, whether those profits are passed on to the consumer is another question. 

Since the actual price on any sale depends upon the relative bargaining power of the buyer and the scller, it is extremely likely that the shrewd buyer now pays little of the speculator's profit. For the same reason, he is apt to be able to continue to get rock bottom prices under a condition of price control. 

In other words, the inefficient buyer will continue to pay for the gains of the shrewd buyer. The only possible advantage of

price control would be to lessen the rewards of shrewd buying. 

The surest way to protect the average consumer is to train him in the art of bargaining and to supply him with correct market information concerning the produce. If, in addition, a lumber exchange could be maintained at one of the principal trading centers, intelligent traders could hedge their transactions and thereby shift the risks to the professional speculator. 

At the same time, the exchange would tend to stabilize the market. 

The only manner in which controlled production, as suggested, can prevent waste is to result in higher returns to the millman. If the lumberman receives more for his product someone else must receive less. If the return is the result of the elimination of speculative profits due to price fluctuations and does not represent an increased price to the consumer, the argument may be sound. However, it must be remembered that economic tendencies fail to operate perfectly. As a consequence, little in the way of prevention of waste can be expected unless a decided increase in return to the lumberman is made due to the fact that few operators know their costs precisely enough to allow them to isolate accurately the merchantable from the unmerchantable material.

Nevertheless, year-long employment of woods labor should have a salutary effect upon the morals and citizenship of the men. For this reason, seasonal employment in the lumber industry should be averted if possible. However, other means than controlled production could be used to bring the matter about. 

In all probability production control would result in an increased price to the consumer If private concerns are allowed to control the situation, a strong tendency toward monopolistic price schedules is almost sure to follow. Control by a governmental commission, especially one on which the lumbermen are represented, is likely to result in the same situation if governmental control of public utilities is to be taken as a criterion. 

Competition between the lumber industry and its competitors is not enough to protect the consumer from a monopolistic price schedule. Likewise, controlled production tends to stifle individual initiative and development as it tends to shelter the weak and inefficient at the expense of the strong. 

CONCLUSION In the conflict of public and private interests, measures advanced by interested groups are very likely to be designed for the welfare of the group rather than the public at large. On the other hand, the reduction of the costs of operation and the extension of the markets, in the main, result in mutual advantages to both the operators and the public. In this connection, the wood products industries are no different

than other industries. As in other businesses, cut-throat competition, and the dissemination of erroneous information, and other practices not in keeping with public interest are to be expected and guarded against. 

If the forestry problem is to be solved upon a basis of private property, it will be necessary for the individuals concerned to work the matter out. Governmental subsidies may result in the desired action. The better way, however, results in the practice of sustained yield because the individuals concerned believe such action is to their best interests. The change from timber mining to timber farming will not be made by men who see each obstacle as insurmountable. Progressive and forceful leadership on the part of timberland owners is absolutely essential if the public is to be justified in allow➡ ing them the privilege of solving the forestry problem. ·

CHAPTER TWELVE A POLICY OF GOVERNMENTAL REGULATION 

The advocates of governmental regulation believe that the lumbermen should be required by law to practice sustained yield. It is pointed out that the present policy of timber mining on the part of the lumber operator is not in keeping with the public welfare. Insofar as the people as a whole are the real owners of the forests, it is their privilege and their duty to see that the forests are properly utilized. Since the private timberland owner is not practicing sustained yield, it is necessary to force him to so by law. 

THE NECESSARY REQUIREMENTS 

The first requirement for governmental regulation is the passage of the necessary law. The second and most essential requirement is the enforcement of the law.

Vigorous law enforcement is enhanced by creating a special organization charged with the enforcement of the act. 

In this manner, a shifting of responsibility for failure becomes difficult. Likewise, a force is developed which understands the peculiar problems involved. At the same time, a particular lobby to fight the resultant political battles can be maintained. 

The difficulty of law enforcement is lessened by a law which forces the initiative for its abrogation upon the timber operator. The proposal to require each lumberman to deposit a reasonable sum for each thousand board feet cut with the proviso that is shall be returned less a nominal administrative charge upon the compliance with the law is an excellent provision

from the standpoint of law enforcement. 

Under such a law, the enforcing agency has the funds available for placing each acre of cut-over land upon the proper silvicultural basis. Any operator who is dissatisfied with the treatment accorded him has recourse only through court or political action. In either case he must be the aggressor.

SECURING FORESTRY PRACTICE BY LAW ENFORCEMENT 

The crux of the successful solution of the forestry problem by governmental regulation lies in securing proper law enforcement. Friction is almost certain to develop whenever the interests of the operator and the public do not coincide. The operators may seek relief in either the legislative halls or the courts. 

Legislation favorable or adverse to the timber operator is secured by exerting sufficient pressure upon the individual members. Political contests between organized groups are likely to proceed upon a basis of securing the desired legislation by any means at hand. Political capital 

All in all, vigorous law is made of trivial incidents which have a strong emotional ap… peal. Prejudices and customs can be used to advantage. Finally, the legislator himself is apt to place his personal interest above that of the government. enforcement can proceed only when backed by an active lobby and by a constant educational campaign to keep public opinion behind the movement. A failure to provide these political factors is almost certain to result in the negation of the law due to a failure to secure sufficient funds for the enforcement work or to a passage of adverse legislation designed to cur-

tail materially the activities of the law enforcing agencies. 

The courts present a very formidable barrier to law enforcement. In the first place, the laws in the United States are basically in favor of capital. Any law for the curtailment and direction of its use is almost certain to be counter to the basic laws of the land. The general background of court decisions is, therefore, apt to react unfavorably to a law designed upon the basis of making the security of capital secondary to public welfare and consumer interest. 

In the second place, the judges themselves are materially influenced in their thinking by their environment and past training. Furthermore, they are given wide latitude in the penalties pronounced once a conviction is secured. The viewpoint of the judge, therefore, becomes a powerful factor in law enforcement. Since most judges may be expected to reflect the capitalistic views of the nation, it is extremely likely that they will prove to be a serious handicap to vigorous law enforcement when that law enforcement tends to force private capital to make certain sacrifices. 

In the third place, the various prosecuting attorneys can negate any law enforcement by merely failing to make a vigorous attempt to secure convictions. Court cases cannot necessarily be won by the most astute of lawyers but they are readily lost whenever the lawyer fails to build his case carefully. 

Finally, the jury itself is notorious for its failure to enforce strictly the laws. The average juror has little knowledge of the law or its intent. During the trial he is

confronted by a mass of conflicting testimony often accentuated with perjury instigated by the lawyers concerned. Prejudices often cause individual jurors to act counter to the intent of the law. Under the present jury system and bar practice, justice is indeed often blind.

The long length of time which it took Sweden actually to secure forestry by law enforcement, coupled with the record of the United States in the control of public utilities indicate the difficulties which confront the American forester if the choice is made to solve the forestry problem by law. 

In the final analysis, successful law enforcement rests squarely upon a vigorous favorable public opinion. Should it be desirable or necessary to invoke the aid of the law in forcing the timberland owner to handle properly his timber, a continuous campaign of public education is the first essential.

Such boards could be given whatever authority thought to be desirable. It is claimed that such boards would allow the lumbermen to cooperate in the bringing about of forestry practice. 

Such cooperation, it may be pointed out, is a matter of horse-trading.

The group with the superior bargaining power Cooperativè is almost certain to obtain the maximum share. boards will, therefore, be effective in forcing correct forestry practice only insofar as they can convince the timber owner

that such practice is to his best interests or insofar as they have the backing of the law to enforce their demands. In the final analysis, it would seem that cooperative boards are merely a sugar coating either to law enforcement or to unregulated private ownership.

SECURING FORESTRY PRACTICE BY ARBITRATION Boards of arbitration are different than boards of cooperation in that they have authority to make binding decisions. The principle of their operation is simple. Any timberland owner who may be dissatisfied with the enforcement of the forestry regulations could appeal to the board of arbitration. 

The board would act as judge and render a binding decision. In effect, such boards are specialized courts which need not be hampered by the customs and traditions of the regular courts. 

The effectiveness of boards of arbitration would depend, of course, upon their personnel and their powers. Both these matters would have to be settled in the field of politics. 

It is likely, however, that the public interest would be best protected by granting such boards broad powers and to limit the personnel to men well qualified by experience and desire to respect the public interest. 

GOVERNMENTAL REGULATION AND PRIVATE PROPERTY 

Governmental regulation of forest properties cannot succeed unless the owners of those properties believe the forestry use to be profitable. 

Without profitable forestry, the owners would refuse to own the land or would use it for other purposes than that of growing trees. For this reason, the solution of the forestry problem

by either governmental regulation or by uncontrolled private ownership demands the elimination of economic and legal handicaps to forest growing and forest products as well as the extension of such other measures as may be deemed advisable for the increasing of the net return to the forest grower. 

Governmental regulation differs from unregulated private ownership in the method of focusing the action of the forest land owner on sustained yield. The advocates of unregulated private property believe the owners of the land will practice sustained yield whenever they can do so profitably. 

The advocates of governmental regulation, on the other hand, point out that all business, and particularly new ones, are speculative in nature. Many acres of timberland are held by those who have no desire to burden themselves with the risks of perpetual forestry. Since only a few owners are interested in sustained yield, it is necessary to protect the public interests in forest lands by forcing the practice of proper forestry measures by legal means. Just how much real forestry will be practiced by either means is entirely problematical.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN A POLICY OF GOVERNMENTAL OWNERSHIP 

3 Although governmental ownership of business is not very common in the United States, the theory underlying its operation is fairly well known. Advocates of governmental ownership, in the main, believe that such a course is the only one which will permit the stopping of forest devastation and waste. The arguments given for this belief are two: the quasipublic nature of forestry and the purported fact that private capital will not be drawn into forestry because of the length of time between investment and income. 

STEPS NECESSARY FOR GOVERNMENTAL OWNERSHIP OF FORESTS Govern mental ownership presents a simple solution of the forestry problem. It is merely necessary for the government to obtain title to the necessary forest lands and to develop the necessary personnel to handle the lands properly. Most advocates of this plan do not suspect it will be necessary for the government to do more than grow the trees. Private enterprise, it is thought, will log and manufacture the material, once it is produced.

The first step in obtaining title to all the forest lands in the nation necessitates their selection. Theoretically, this selection would be upon the basis of expert judgment unsavored with political pressure. In any event, the particular areas to be used for forest purposes must be isolated and title secured for them.

The easiest method for the government to obtain title is by the process of confiscation. 

At the present time, direct

confiscation of forest lands is impossible on account of the constitutional and other legal protection given private property. Indirect confiscation via the tax route, however, is not only possible but very likely unless the tax reforms occur to prevent it. Unfortunately, revested delinquent tax lands are invariably in very poor shape from a forestry standpoint. 

Since it often costs more to place such lands in shape than their value for forest production warrants, such a policy of confiscation may prove unwise. 

As the outright purchase of lands seems the only feasible method for securing the title, the purchase price would have to be set by expert appraisers. Since the government would be in a monopolistic position in the matter, it could easily manipulate the purchase price so as to entice the private owner to leave the lands in the condition desired from a forestry standpoint. 

The speed at which the forest lands could be attained would depend entirely upon the political pressure obtainable to secure the appropriations for the work. For this reason, it might prove more expedient from a political standpoint to raise the necessary funds by the sale of bonds rather than from immediate taxation. In any event, a program of purchase of forest lands is certain to increase the public debt, taxes, or both.

The development of the necessary administrative force would merely require a rapid expansion of existing forces. 

Because of a dearth of experienced men and lack of time to train properly those already employed, rapid expansion of any force always results in a certain amount of initial in

efficiency. However, such inefficiency should be of a temporary nature. 

ADVANTAGES OF PUBLIC OWNERSHIP 

Theoretically, governmental ownership is exceptionally efficient. The trouble resulting from different ownerships would be eliminated in the work of protecting, logging, and administering the tracts. 

Losses due to forced liquidation, inadequate financing, a nd ignorant managing would be averted. The fullest use of the forest in all respects could be assured. Finally, the consumer would not need to pay large profits to the private timberland owner 

THE DISADVANTAGES OF PUBLIC OWNERSHIP 

Public businesses,and forestry is unlikely to be an exception, are seldom as efficient as they could be. The board of directors is not composed of experts vitally interested in the business but politicians whose knowledge of the requirements of the business is often slight and whose personal interests are often at variance with the public interest. For this reason, a certain amount of the income of a governmental business is likely to be placed in fighting political battles of all sorts.. Public business lacks the upgo to be efficient due to the fact that losses are covered by appropriations. The test of the correctness of decisions is shifted from the economic to the political field. Expert opinions are apt to be based upon the possibility of securing the needed appropriations rather than upon the probability of profit. Progress is apt to be premised upon the ability to get larger and larger appropriations rather than the ability to increase profits through the development of efficient methods of production and better markets. In short, the consumer is

C not protected by a ruthless competition between producers but by vacillitating and often haphazard political action.

LOCAL AUTONOMY 

Extensive governmental forests have a decided Such effect upon the freedom of action of the nearby citizens. forests are controlled by distant officers who react but imperfectly to local political pressure. Local citizens are often not free to barter for economic opportunities which they may believe to exist on such lands. In the same way, industrial development on such forests is carefully controlled by individuals who have no special local interest. 

Governmental forest ownership is almost sure to affect materially the tax situation. Since governmental lands are free from taxes, the tax burden must be carried by the remaining tax payers. Any payments made in lieu of taxes will fail to be completely satisfactory as such payments do not give to local tax officials the same authority which they enjoy with other taxes. Likewise, there is very apt to be a decided difference of opinion in regard to the amount due in lieu of taxes.

ADMINISTRATIVE CONSIDERATIONS 

Large businesses, such as extensive governmental forests would be, may become too large for efficient handling. According to the law of diminishing returns, after a business reaches a certain size the net returns per unit tend to decrease. The exact size of a forestry organization which produces the maximum net returns per unit is very problematical.

In a measure, a system of decentralized control helps to offset the operation of the law of diminishing returns.

In the field of politics, the larger the organization the more interests involved and the greater the difficulty of a raid by private interests. From this standpoint, the national government is in a better position to furnish consistent and adequate administration of the forest lands. National forest ownership would also allow the consumer states to have a voice in the matter whereas, under a system of state ownership, the only protection afforded the consumer states would be the competition between the various states--a rather peculiar situation.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN PROGRESS TOWARD A NATIONAL FOREST POLICY 

The ultimate forest policy of the United States will undoubtedly result from the activity of the leaders in the various movements. Private timberland owners now have the opportunity to tako whatever steps they deem advisable. If they can curtail adverse discussion or can make a showing sufficient to convince the majority of their intent and ability to solve the problem, little change will be made in the present policy. On the other hand, active and insistent activity on the part of the advocates of governmental control or ownership will undoubtedly bring about a decided change in the present forest policy. 

No particular forest policy will be an unmixed blessing. Likewise, the success or failure of any measure adopted will largely depend upon the character and ability of the men who direct the affiars. A policy based on private ownership of forest lands is desirable only insofar as the private and public walfare coordinate. Whenever private enterprise seriously injures public well-being, governmental action is highly desirable. 

The solution of the forestry problem calls for enlightened action in the woods. The continuance of forest devastation is an economic cancer on the life of the American people and the best method of solving the problem is highly problematical. The opinions of men must be relied upon to determine what will happen under any particular course of action. The more opinions expressed and the freer the discussion. the better the chances of arriving at the best solution. The

theater of public opinion thus becomes the best place to solve the major problems. 

The desirability of action designed to decrease the costs of production or to increase the utility of the forest products, meets with the approval of all. However, the questions of who is to pay the cost and who is to reap the rewards of such improvement are debatable. Subsidies to private enterprise often have the effect of transferring public wealth to private hands. In any event, most subsidies are seldom necessary and often unfairly affect the distribution of wealth. 

The specific action desirable depends, in a large measure, not only upon the physical situation but upon the character and ability of the leaders and the forces necessary to motivate them into action. For this reason, a discussion of action thought to be desirable by any particular individual is not within the province of this book.
