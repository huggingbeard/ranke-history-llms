THE CORPSE WITH KNEE ACTION 

What was such a good-looking girl doing in the dead of night along the waterfront? What about the two men who jumped out of a black sedan and overpowered her, until Bill King of the San 

Francisco Times came to the rescue? Was the lady's name really Stella Holmes, and was she engaged to the shipping magnate Harold Landsbury as she claimed?

Bill accompanied her to Landsbury's boat to find out. Instead he was confronted with the gruesome spectacle of the tycoon weltering in his own blood in a cabin. Curiously, an artery behind his knee had been severed. It was a strange method of murder, but Bill King recalled that someone had used it before.

There was no answer. Instead, the rear door was opened and a smartly dressed young woman stepped out. She gave him his fare and, without a word, walked briskly toward the pier.

A gust of wind enveloped her, and she drew her coat more closely about her. The driver, watching, noted her slender, shapely legs. He studied them a moment,

then yelled after her, "Can I be of any help?"

She gave no sign of having heard him, continuing to walk in the same direction until her figure was lost in the thick mist.

He did not know whether the horns, whose croaking seemed to cut through the fog, had drowned his voice, or whether she had deliberately ignored him. What was a beautiful dame like that doing on such a night in the most deserted part of the Embarcadero, anyway? 

He buttoned up his coat, puckered his lips, and thrust the car into gear. All dames were screwy! You never could tell what was on their minds.

As he swung the cab around the headlights picked up her retreating figure approaching the pier-shed, a diminishing black speck in the murky grayness. His foot moved involuntarily to the brake pedal, started to press down, then returned emphatically to the accelerator. 

Dim lights began to penetrate the fog. Then the ghostly outlines of a ship loomed out of the darkness, and a sharply inclined gangway. The girl's ungloved hand clung to the wet rope at the side as she went up. Ears strained, she heard only the creaking of the boat and the slapping of the unseen water far below.

The gangway shuddered as someone above her stepped upon it and started rapidly down. She shrank back against the rope while a husky, well-built man, his hat pulled down over his eyes, passed her with no recognition of her presence and turned toward the street.

Once aboard, she moved toward midships as if familiar with the vessel. Her steps did not falter until she stopped abruptly to light a cigarette. As she turned aside to shield the flickering flame of her lighter two men, their faces hidden by upturned overcoat collars and hats pulled low, walked past her so quietly that, straightening up and seeing them, she was startled. For a moment they seemed to hesitate, then moved on. 

She watched them until they vanished, then looked about her. Only portions of the rocking, creaking vessel were visible; the lights on the masts seemed suspended in mid-air. She stiffened at a long, drawn-out groan from a nearby fog horn. Lingering no longer, she began to walk rapidly toward a cabin about ten feet ahead through whose window came an edge of light. 

A black sedan was parked at the curb not far from the pier entrance. Inside two men were talking in low tones. One was big and bulged with fat. The other was a shrimp whose bass voice contrasted sharply with his companion's high soprano.
