RECENT EVENTS Franklin D. Roosevelt's Re-election. President Roosevelt's second term began with his inauguration on January 20, 1937, the change of date from March 4 having been made by the Twentieth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States. He could look with satisfaction on the recent election and feel that he and his program had the support of a large majority of the American people. He had received 27,000,000 votes, while his Republican opponent, Alfred M. Landon, had 16,000,000. In the electoral college the President had received 523 electoral votes against 8 for his opponent. 

This was one of the largest majorities, both popular and electoral, that a President had ever received. In the newly elected Congress, also, the President's party had an overwhelming majority. In addition to the Democratic 

President and the Democratic majorities in Congress, most of the states had elected Democratic governors, so that the victory was almost complete. 

The Seventy-fifth Congress. The new Congress, which began its first session on January 3, 1937, had 76 Democratic senators against 16 Republicans and 4 Independents, while in the lower house there were 328 Democrats against 90 Republicans, 13 Independents, and 4 vacant seats. With this overwhelming majority President Roosevelt felt confident that he was in a position to proceed with his New Deal program. In fact, before the election, in a campaign speech at Madison Square Garden in New York, he pointed out that "we have just begun to fight." And he referred to certain of his opponents as "economic royalists."

A Reform Program. President Roosevelt, therefore, looked forward to permanent reforms in the government. During his first term, many of the new laws were emergency measures, passed at a time when the country was in the midst of a depression. These measures were mostly for relief and recovery. Now, however, the President felt the time had come for reform. 

He expressed this idea in his inaugural address when he said: "Our progress out of the depression is obvious. But that is not all that you and I mean by the new order of things. Our pledge was not merely to do a patchwork job with secondhand materials. By using the new materials of social justice we have undertaken to erect on the old foundations a more enduring structure for the better use of future generations." Again, he said: 3

"I see a great nation, upon a great continent, blessed with a great wealth of natural resources. I see a United States which can demonstrate that, under democratic methods of government, national wealth can be translated into a spreading volume of human comforts hitherto unknown, and the lowest standard of living can be raised far above the level of mere subsistence." He pointed out that one third of our nation is "ill-housed, illclad, ill-nourished,” and that “The test of our progress is not whether we add more to the abundance of those who have much; it is whether we provide enough for those who have too little." 

These words represented the philosophy on which the reform program was based: a program which was begun in the first term as relief and recovery, would now, in accordance with the same ideals, be transformed into a program of social reforms. 

One of the measures which the President suggested was a government reorganization bill which would give him power to reorganize the executive branch of the government. This bill, in 1938, passed the Senate by the close margin of forty-nine to forty-two, while in the House it was defeated. It thus became evident that, while the President was elected by a large majority, he did not have the complete support of Congress. Many of the 

Democrats who had readily voted for emergency relief and recovery measures held back when some of the permanent reform measures were under consideration.

The Fight over the Supreme Court. Another serious setback to President 

Roosevelt's program was the defeat of his attempt to add new members to the Supreme Court. Some of the New Deal measures passed during Roosevelt's first administration had been held unconstitutional by the Supreme Court. Among these measures was the National Recovery Act (NRA), which was declared unconstitutional in May, 1935; the Agricultural Adjustment Act, declared unconstitutional in January, 1936; and the Guffey- 

Snyder Coal Act, declared unconstitutional in May, 1936. 

Following the NRA decision, the President maintained that this action of the Court threw the country back into the horse-and-buggy age. Later he praised President Jackson for his attitude in defying the Supreme Court and spoke approvingly of President Jefferson's assuming responsibility for the purchase of Louisiana without authority from the Constitution.* It so happened that in 1937 five of the justices of the Supreme Court were past seventy-four years of age, and that since 1897 only three appointments to the Supreme Court had been made by a Democratic President, although * For the acts of Presidents Jefferson and Jackson here referred to, see, if desired, pages

the Democrats had been in power for twelve of the twenty-four years since 1913. President Roosevelt, therefore, maintained that the Supreme Court was out of sympathy with the social and economic philosophy of the recent period and proposed that a change must be made in the membership of the Court. In his message on February 5, 1937, he spoke of infusing new blood into the Court in order to facilitate procedure and bring the judiciary abreast of the times. He asked Congress to pass a bill empowering the President to appoint a new justice to the Supreme Court in addition to each member of the Court who had passed the age of seventy and continued to sit on the bench-except that the total number of justices (normally nine) at any time should never be made to exceed fifteen. 

This proposal of the President at once drew fire from his opponents and also aroused much opposition within the ranks of the Democratic party, thereby bringing on one of the most sensational political and legislative battles which the country has witnessed in recent years. The controversy raged for over six months, with prolonged arguments pro and con. On 

March 10, 1937, President Roosevelt in a radio address, called a “fireside chat," brought the question before the American people and referred to the American form of government as a "three-horse team." He pointed out that the people in the driver's seat were expecting the third horse, namely, the Supreme Court, to pull in unison with the other two - the legislative and the executive. During the controversy the President's opponents charged that he was seeking to pack the Court so as to make it possible for him and his party to become firmly entrenched in the government. To this the President replied, however, that he was not seeking to pack the Court but that he was merely trying to unpack it, because it had been packed by the appointments which had been made prior to his election. 

While the controversy was raging, several important decisions were handed down by the Supreme Court. On March 29, 1937, the Court upheld the constitutionality of the Washington State Minimum Wage Law by a five to four decision; and the national Frazier-Lemke Act, which aids farmers in danger of losing their farms by the foreclosure of mortgages, was upheld by a unanimous decision. On April 12, 1937, the Wagner-Connery

Labor Relations Act was upheld. In another important decision, May 24, 1937, the Social Security Act with the unemployment and old-age provisions was approved. Thus it seemed that the Court, or at least a majority of its members, was falling into line with the social and economic philosophy of the New Deal, and the opponents of the President's Supreme Court bill pointed out that there was no longer a need for the appointment of new justices.

The Senate Committee on the Judiciary reported adversely on the bill by a vote of eight to ten, and the fight for the addition of new justices of the Court was lost. However, since the New Deal measures were now being upheld by the Court, President Roosevelt felt that even though his Supreme Court measure was defeated, he had really won, for the goal for which he fought was attained. He stated on June 24 that "the attitude of the Supreme Court toward constitutional questions is entirely changed." He also pointed out that "its recent decisions are eloquent testimony to its willingness to collaborate with the two other branches of the government to make democracy work."

New Appointments to the Supreme Court. In May, 1937, Justice Willis 

Van Devanter, seventy-eight years of age, one of the conservative justices, resigned. He was replaced by Justice Hugo L. Black, of Alabama, on 

August 17, 1937. Justice George Sutherland, seventy-six years of age, informed the President on January 5 that he would retire from the Court on 

January 18, 1938. In January, 1938, President Roosevelt named Stanley 

Forman Reed, Solicitor General of the United States since 1935, to succeed Justice Sutherland. Thus two conservative justices were replaced by justices who were in accord with Roosevelt's philosophy of government. 

The death of Justice Benjamin N. Cardozo, one of the liberal justices, created another vacancy and gave President Roosevelt an opportunity to appoint a third justice. To this vacancy he appointed, in January, 1939, Professor Felix Frankfurter, of the Harvard Law School. Professor Frank furter ranks among the nation's outstanding liberal teachers of law and has for a long time been a New Deal adviser. Another of the liberal justices, Louis D. Brandeis, resigned his position February 13, 1939; and President 

Roosevelt filled that vacancy by the appointment of William O. Douglas, who in 1929-1934 had been a professor of law at Yale University. 

The Recession of 1937. In the fall of 1937 President Roosevelt's reform program was hampered by a business recession. Big business criticized the 

New Deal legislation, asserting that it was hamstringing business. The administration, on the other hand, criticized big business, and William O. 

Douglas, then the Chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, pointed out that the New York Stock Exchange must reorganize or else be regulated more strictly by the government. The Secretary of the Interior, Harold L. Ickes, blamed the recession on "the economic royalists" and charged that big business had gone on a "sit-down strike." In his Jackson 

Day speech of January 8, 1938, the President himself entered the conflict by saying that there would be no compromise with economic autocrats. He stated further that he was working "to restore and to uphold the integrity

LABOR PROBLEMS 

The National Labor Relations Board. It will be recalled that in July, 1935, Congress passed the Wagner-Connery Bill, also known as the National 

Labor Relations Act. This act created the National Labor Relations Board. 

The Board's powers were upheld by decisions of the Supreme Court in 1937 and 1938. The main purpose of the National Labor Relations Board is to investigate the issues, facts, practices, and activities of the employers or employees in labor controversies; to see that the employees have the right of self-organization, the right to form, join, or assist labor organizations and to bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing; and to prevent any person from engaging in any unfair labor practice affecting commerce. The National Labor Relations Act also provides that nothing in the act shall interfere in any way with the right to strike. In August, 1937, the Board reported that it had handled 6,479 disputes, involving 1,944,088 workers, and that three fifths of the cases had been closed; that of these cases 2,231, involving nearly half a million employees, had ended in agreement of both parties. In January, 1938, the National Labor Relations 

Board in its report pointed out that forty-one cases, involving substantial disagreements between the American Federation of Labor and the Committee for Industrial Organization during the fiscal year which ended June 30, had been handled. Of these the Board had decided twenty-one in favor of the A. F. of L., sixteen for the C.I.O., while four were withdrawn. It further reported that of the 8,851 cases, covering 1,845,818 workers, closed during the year, 95 per cent were settled by dismissal or withdrawal of union complaints or by agreement between workers and employers without hearing.

President Roosevelt, however, was not satisfied with the progress which was being made in the field of labor, and insisted on further legislation. 

During 1938 the National Labor Relations Board was under heavy fire from both capital and labor. The American Federation of Labor found fault with the Board for its alleged favoritism to the C.I.O.; and the employers opposed it because of alleged unfairness and prejudice. These

C.I.O. and Sit-Down Strikes. In 1935, the Committee for Industrial 

Organization (C.I.O.), headed by John L. Lewis, had begun a movement for industrial labor unions, each of which would include all the workers in an industry instead of the members of a particular craft, which had been the basis for most of the unions in the American Federation of Labor. Ten unions of the American Federation of Labor were suspended because they supported the C.I.O. This resulted in splitting organized labor into two groups: one, the A. F. of L., headed by William Green, and the other, the 

C.I.O., under the leadership of John L. Lewis. The great gains which organized labor had made during the last half century were largely the result of craft unions or skilled labor unions affiliated with the American Federation of Labor. Under the leadership of John L. Lewis, however, a new policy was initiated: that of organizing the workers of more and more industries into large industrial unions which made no distinction between skilled and unskilled labor. Among the unions affiliated with the C.I.O. were the United Mine Workers, the United Textile Workers, the Amalgamated Clothing Workers, the United Automobile Workers, and others. 

Soon after this organization got under way, strikes occurred which took a new form and a new name. They were known as "sit-down" strikes. In 

1937, the United Automobile Workers Union began such a strike in some of the plants of the General Motors Corporation. The strike was called for the purpose of forcing the General Motors Corporation to recognize the United Automobile Workers Union as the only official representative of the General Motors workers. The strikers sat down on the job, refused to work, and also refused to leave the plant for fear that others might take their places. Such a strike was apparently considered illegal, and the General Motors Corporation obtained an order from the court directing the sit-down strikers to vacate, but no attempt was made to carry out this order of the court. With organized labor thus divided into two camps, it was difficult in many cases for the employer to decide which was the regularly constituted agency for collective bargaining. Following the strike in 

General Motors plants, many other sit-down strikes occurred in different parts of the country. As the epidemic of sit-down strikes spread throughout the country public opinion turned against this form of strike, which some of the courts declared to be unlawful seizures of property. William 

Green, President of the American Federation of Labor, condemned these strikes as illegal.

Following the organization of automobile workers, the C.I.O. next proceeded to organize the workers in the steel industry. The United States 

Steel Corporation accepted the C.I.O.'s terms, granted a 10 per cent increase in pay, and recognized the C.I.O. Some of the smaller companies resisted, and a series of strikes followed. The N.L.R.B. intervened, in some cases finding the employers guilty of violating the provisions of the Wagner-Connery Act, and ordering them to reinstate men who had taken part in the strike and had been discharged. 

Under the Wagner-Connery Act relations between employer and employee were far from peaceful; for in the year 1937 there occurred 4,470 strikes, the largest number in the history of organized labor. However, the number declined in 1938. The U. S. Department of Labor reported 2,049 strikes in the first ten months of that year as compared with 4,347 for the same period in 1937. In some of these strikes force was used by the state. governments to remove sit-down strikers from the employers' property. 

In November, 1938, at its meeting in Pittsburgh, the Committee for

Industrial Organization adopted a constitution, set up a national organization, and changed its name to the Congress of Industrial Organizations. 

The constitution adopted at this meeting, in providing for a national organization, brought about the complete separation of the C.I.O. from the 

American Federation of Labor. The roster of the C.I.O. early in 1939 included 35 affiliated unions, 675 local industrial unions, and 8 organizing committees. It had a membership of nearly 4,000,000. The American 

Federation of Labor in August, 1938, reported 32,631 unions affiliated with 102 national and international unions, 1,517 local trade and federal unions directly affiliated with the Federation, 48 state bodies, and 792 city central bodies. The membership was listed as 3,623,087. 

In February, 1939, President Roosevelt pointed out that this division of labor into rival groups was detrimental to the best interests of both labor and industry. He therefore asked both John L. Lewis and William 

Green to appoint members of a committee to meet at the White House on March 7, 1939, for the purpose of settling their differences. At this conference, Lewis proposed to "supersede and embrace" the A. F. of L. and the 

C.I.O., a new American Congress of Labor in which neither Lewis nor 

Green should be allowed to hold office. The proposal was not accepted by the A. F. of L.

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938. The Seventy-fifth Congress passed the Federal Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, most of which was to go into effect October 24, 1938. The purpose of this act is to protect workers' health, efficiency, and general well-being. However, many businesses are not affected by the law, because they are not engaged in interstate commerce. The Constitution of the United States limits the power of 

Congress, concerning commerce, to the regulation of foreign commerce and of commerce between the states. It was chiefly for this reason that the 

NRA was declared unconstitutional. The new labor law of 1938 made it illegal for workers employed in interstate commerce to receive less than twenty-five cents an hour, and provided that the work week should be no longer than forty-four hours. It was not illegal to work for a longer week, but more expensive for the employer, since he would be required to pay one and one-half times the regular rate for overtime. As years go on the standards set up by this law are to grow stricter. Beginning October 24, 1939, the minimum rate will be thirty cents an hour and the number of hours in the work week forty-two; in October, 1940, the number of hours in the work week will be reduced to forty; and in October, 1945, the minimum rate will be raised to forty cents an hour. This Act of 1938 has been described as an attempt of the New Deal to put a "floor under wages and a ceiling over hours." Besides the regulation of wages and hours, however, it also imposed Federal prohibition of labor by children under sixteen in interstate commerce industries, to go into effect immediately.

Congress. The emergency Relief Act of 1935 extended it until June 30, 1937. The life of the Public Works Administration was later extended to 

July 1, 1939, by the Public Works Administration Extension Act of 1937, and to June 30, 1941, by the Public Works Administration Appropriation Act of 1938.

The Public Works Administration made grants of money to public bodies not exceeding 45 per cent of the cost of the projects involved. Loans as well as grants were made. Money was advanced to "states, municipalities, and other public bodies for construction or repair of waterworks, sewer systems, electric-light plants, streets and highways, bridges, schools, hospitals, and recreational facilities." Projects were to be started not later than January 1, 1939, and completed by July 1, 1941, under the provisions of the Act of 1938.

By October 1, 1938, the PWA had allotted funds for projects in 3,068 of the 3,071 counties in the United States. The total amount of money allotted them was $5,831,832,200 for the construction of 33,136 projects. 

The opponents of the public works program point out that while billions of dollars have been spent, unemployment has not been greatly reduced; and that, on the other hand, the number on relief rolls has steadily increased.

The WPA. Not to be confused with the PWA is the WPA- the Works 

Progress Administration — for several years headed by Harry L. Hopkins. 

In 1939 Hopkins became Secretary of Commerce in the President's cabinet, and Lieutenant-Colonel F. C. Harrington succeeded him as Works 

Progress Administrator.

The Works Progress Administration was set up by executive order under the authority of the emergency Relief Appropriation Act of 1935. 

A later act extended its life to June 30, 1939. In January, 1939, President 

Roosevelt asked for an additional appropriation of $875,000,000 for it, but Congress, after long hesitation, appropriated only $725,000,000; and later, after a second request by the President for $150,000,000, it appropriated only $100,000,000.

The purpose of the WPA is to provide relief and relief work, and to increase employment by providing useful projects. Like the PWA, the 

WPA makes funds available for the construction of highways and streets, public buildings, parks, public utilities, water systems, etc. It differs from the PWA in that at least 95 per cent of the persons employed upon projects undertaken by the WPA must be certified as in need of relief.

Thus the aggregate expenditure was $5,099,000,000. The NYA. Appropriations amounting to more than $150,000,000 have been used in conducting the program of the National Youth Administration, a branch of the Works Progress Administration which has helped young persons to continue their work in high school and in college. The 

NYA program was under the supervision of Executive Director Aubrey Williams. In May, 1938, there were nearly 327,000 young people, half of whom were men and half women, receiving assistance from the NYA program. About 225,000 were in high school; about 99,000 were doing undergraduate work in college; and about 3,000 were doing graduate work. 

The Social Security Act. One of the most significant reforms of the New 

Deal was the Social Security Act, approved August 14, 1935. The act is administered by the Social Security Board composed of three members appointed by the President. The act provides for three major phases of work: old-age benefits, unemployment compensation, and public assistance.

The United States Supreme Court ruled on May 24, 1937, that the taxes levied under the act were valid. The Court also upheld the state unemployment compensation laws and the provisions for the payment of oldage insurance. These rulings served to dispel doubts as to the constitutionality of the act.

The old-age insurance program is the only feature of the act to be directly administered by the Federal government. By June, 1938, 39,500,000 applications had been received from workers throughout the nation. Federal old-age benefits are of three types: (1) monthly installment benefits payable after age sixty-five, (2) single cash payments to persons at sixtyfive, and (3) payments upon death. The principal type of benefit provided by this program is a life income payable in monthly installments to individuals who have become sixty-five years of age and who have withdrawn from regular employment. The amount of revenue for old-age insurance was provided for by a system of gradually increasing taxes on the worker's wages, starting at 1 per cent in 1937 and reaching 3 per cent in and after 1949, such taxes to be levied both on the employer and on the employee.

The amount of the final pension will be determined by the worker's annual salary and the number of years he has worked. 

Another feature of the act provides for unemployment compensation. 

This feature calls for the co-operation of the Federal and state governments, the work of the Federal government being to provide an incentive for state action and to establish plans for the framework and administration of such a program. If a state law is in accordance with the Federal act, the 

Federal government will collect a special pay-roll tax from employers in the state, 90 per cent of which is credited to the state. No payment is required of the employee. In the first five months of 1938 over $139,000,000 was paid the unemployed workers in the twenty-five states then paying benefits. By the end of July, 1938, unemployment benefits were made available in twenty-eight states.

In providing public assistance to needy persons, the Federal Social Security Act recognized the joint responsibility of the Federal government, the state, and local communities for the protection of certain groups from the extremes of destitution. For aid to the needy aged and the needy blind, the Federal government pays one half of the total expenditures up to a combined maximum of $30 per month to each individual. Every state in the Union now provides old-age assistance, and 1,721,000 persons were being aided by September, 1938. About 39,000 blind persons were receiving aid in June, 1938; by that time grants of $5,164,000 had been certified for their use. Toward providing aid to dependent children the Federal government pays one third of the total cost up to a maximum of $18 per month for the first dependent child and $12 per month for each additional child in the same home. By June, 1938, Federal grants of $25,080,900 had been certified, providing help for more than 600,000 dependent children.

The CCC. The Civilian Conservation Corps was re-established by an act of Congress approved June 28, 1937. This organization took the place of the agency which was first set up under the Act of March 31, 1933. The purpose of the Civilian Conservation Corps is to provide employment and offer vocational training for young citizens of the United States. Many of these had been moving about in search of employment. Failure to find work, it was feared, might lead many to a life of crime. In the 2,600 camps throughout the country employment is provided for them through the performance of useful public works in connection with the conservation and development of the natural resources of the United States. Some of the types of work in which these boys are employed consist of “works of public interest or utility for the protection, restoration, regeneration, improvement, development, utilization, maintenance, or enjoyment of the natural resources of lands and waters, and the products thereof, including forests, fish, and wildlife on lands or interest in lands. . . belonging to, or under the jurisdiction and control of, the United States, its Territories, and insular possessions, and the several States." The work includes also the prevention and control of forest fires, forest tree pests and diseases, soil erosion, and floods. The director of the CCC may enroll not more than 

300,000 men at any one time, of whom not more than 30,000 may be war veterans, exempt from age limits, and the others, with very few exceptions, are unmarried male citizens between the ages of seventeen and twentythree years, who are unemployed and in need of employment. Enrollments are for a period of not less than six months, and re-enrollment must not exceed a total of two years.

More Adequate Housing. In his second inaugural address President 

Roosevelt pointed out that one third of the nation's people were ill-housed. 

This was not an entirely new idea, for efforts to promote home ownership and better housing were begun under the Hoover administration with the enactment of the Federal Home Loan Bank Act, which was approved

June 13, 1933, and the National Housing Act of June 27, 1934, and by amending acts in 1934 and 1935. The Federal Home Loan Bank Board was originally created to supervise the operation of the Federal Home Loan Banks, now one in each of twelve districts; and it also administers the 

Home Owners' Loan Corporation and the Federal Savings and Loan Insur ance Corporation. The Federal Home Loan Bank System has become the largest mortgage reserve institution in the world. It had a membership on

Home Owners' Loan Corporation. The Home Owners' Loan Corpora tion was created by the Act of June 13, 1933, as an emergency relief agency. 

Its purpose was to make long-term mortgage loans at low interest rates to those who were in need of funds for the protection, preservation, or recovery of their homes and who were unable to procure such financing through regular channels. Most of these loans were granted so that home owners might refinance their homes and save them from foreclosures, which were, in March, 1933, taking place at a rapid rate so that thousands of families were losing their homes. To cope with it, President Roosevelt in his message to Congress on January 5, 1939, asked for an appropriation of $968,158,851 for the activities of the Department of Agriculture, including more than $500,000,000 for subsidies to farmers for the fiscal year. An important new feature of the act is a provision for Federal crop insurance for wheat, beginning with the 1939 crop. 

The amended Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act empowered the Secretary of Agriculture to encourage farm practices which will conserve and improve the soil by devoting a larger portion of the land to soil-conserving crops to take the place of soil-depleting crops. When supplies of a commodity glut the market and greatly reduce prices, the market for that particular crop may be apportioned among producers by setting quotas. However, such a quota can be in effect only when two thirds of the farmers agree to it by voting in favor of it. Loans are made available to enable producers, without financial hardship, to carry over supplies from years of unusually large production to be marketed in years of crop shortage, thus providing for an ever-normal granary. Such loans are provided through the Commodity Credit Corporation which was established for this purpose.

The agricultural program has been criticized on the ground that it is based on an economy of scarcity rather than an economy of abundance. 

The critics maintain that in order that we may have more goods to consume we must produce more goods.

The TVA. The Tennessee Valley Authority is a corporation created by act of Congress May 18, 1933. This act provides that the Authority take control of the Wilson Dam and the munitions plant at Muscle Shoals, which had been constructed earlier. The powers of the Tennessee Valley Authority are vested in a board of three directors, appointed by the President with the approval of the Senate. The Government vs. Private Industry. With the development of the 

TVA program, strenuous objection was voiced by some of the leaders in private industry, especially by the utilities which were most directly affected by the competition from the TVA. The utilities represent an investment of about $12,500,000,000. Those in control of these huge enterprises feared that the value of their investments would decline because of government competition, and that the loss would ultimately lead to financial ruin; hence they opposed the government's entry into a field which they maintained should be reserved for private enterprise. The representatives of these companies pointed out that TVA is merely an "entering wedge” and that the country is confronting the problem of government ownership of many other industries.

Those supporting the TVA, however, pointed out that the production of electricity is a matter of vital concern to the general welfare of our country; that while the capital invested in the production of electricity in twenty years has grown from less than $5,000,000,000 to more than $12,000,000,000, the benefits of this increase were not always shared by the consumers in the form of cheaper rates, but were at times absorbed in huge profits which were enjoyed by a small group of utility magnates. 

They further pointed out that in some cases abuses had developed in the utilities. Giant utility holding companies had developed which engaged in questionable financial practices which in some cases caused investors to suffer heavy losses. The TVA supporters also claimed that the rates charged for electricity by the private utilities have been too high. The opponents, on the other hand, say that lower rates will mean higher taxes.

INTERNATIONAL RELATIONS Foreign Relations. In the years following the World War the United 

States worked steadily toward world peace. Even though our country did not join the League of Nations, we were nevertheless interested in promoting peace. We took part in disarmament conferences, and finally our Secretary of State, Frank B. Kellogg, took an active part in the formulation of the Kellogg-Briand Pact which condemned recourse to war for the solution of international controversies. This principle was accepted by most of the nations of the civilized world.

RECENT EVENTS 

In 1932 and 1933 the United States took part in the World Economic Conference to consider questions of stabilization of currency and the removal of trade barriers. We were thus interested in promoting better international relations. However, it was soon evident that peace would not reign throughout the world. In 1935 war was begun by the attack of 

Italy on Ethiopia. Later Japan began her attack on China, and still later (1936) civil war broke out in Spain. In an attempt to keep America from becoming embroiled, President Roosevelt issued a neutrality proclamation after he was authorized to do so by a joint resolution of Congress. 

As time went on, the dictator Mussolini in Italy, the dictator Hitler in Germany, and the war lords of Japan became more aggressive. In international controversies also the Fascist dictators claimed that they were acting to save Europe from Communism, while the Japanese claimed that they were saving China from a similar fate. It became quite evident that there was little hope for collective security, that peace was not soon to reign throughout the world, and that the American ideal of democracy was not looked upon with favor by European dictators. 

President Roosevelt called attention to the European political situation in his speech in Chicago in October, 1937. He referred to a quarantine of dictators and said: "The present reign of terror and international lawlessness began a few years ago. It began through unjustified interference in the internal affairs of other nations or the invasion of alien territory in violation of treaties and has now reached a stage where the very foundations of civilization are seriously threatened. . . . "The peace-loving nations must make a concerted effort in opposition to those violations of treaties and those ignorings of humane instincts which today are creating a state of international anarchy and instability from which there is no escape through mere isolation or neutrality... "When an epidemic of physical disease starts to spread, the community approves and joins in a quarantine of the patient in order to protect the health of the community against the spread of the disease." 

From this speech it became clear that the President was out of sympathy with the European dictators; but it left a doubt as to what the foreign policy of the United States would be. In March, 1938, Hitler created a crisis by seizing Austria and making it a part of Germany. Europe was tense, and many Americans feared for the peace of the world. On April 14, 1938, in an address in Washington before the Governing Board of the Pan-American Union, President Roosevelt again expressed his opinion on world affairs when he said:

He said: "Democracy has disappeared in several other great nations, not because the people of those nations disliked democracy, but because they had grown tired of unemployment and insecurity, of seeing their children hungry while they sat helpless in the face of government confusion, government weakness through lack of leadership in government. . . . History proves that dictatorships do not grow out of strong and successful governments but out of weak and helpless ones. If by democratic methods people get a government strong enough to protect them from fear and starvation, there democracy grows; but, if they do not, they grow impatient. Therefore, the only sure bulwark of continuing liberty is a government strong enough to protect the interests of the people and a people strong enough and well enough informed to maintain its sovereign control over its government."

The Lima Conference. In December, 1938, the United States took part in the Pan-American Conference held at Lima, Peru. The American delegation was headed by Secretary of State Cordell Hull. In view of the developments in European countries, this conference approved a resolution unanimously condemning "all persecutions from racial or religious motives which place a number of human beings in the impossibility of obtaining a livelihood." The Conference adopted the Declaration of Lima which stated that the American states "reaffirm their continental solidarity and their purpose to collaborate in the maintenance of the principles upon which solidarity is based"; that they "reaffirm their decision to maintain and defend them against all foreign intervention." Thus, as European prospects for peace diminish, the United States turns toward the Western Hemisphere in an attempt to obtain collective security and peace in this part of the world.

The Congressional Election of 1938. As the time approached for the primary Congressional elections of 1938, President Roosevelt assumed an active part and used his influence to secure the selection of candidates who were favorable to the New Deal. In a number of the states where senatorial contests were under way he expressed his preference for those who favored his program and urged the defeat of conservative Democratic senators who had opposed his program. In nearly all those cases, however, the Democratic senators whom he designated as undesirable were returned to office. 

In the November election it soon became evident that there was considerable reaction against New Deal policies. In the House of Representatives the Republicans gained 79 members, while in the Senate the Republicans gained 8. Of the Democrats elected to Congress a number were opposed to parts of President Roosevelt's program. Thus the new Senate contained 22 independent Democrats and 23 Republicans against 47 New Deal Democrats, while the new House of Representatives had 170 Republicans and 71 independent Democrats against 191 New Deal Democrats. Therefore, the 

Seventy-sixth Congress, which opened January 3, 1939, was not nearly so strong for the New Deal program as the Seventy-fifth Congress had been.
