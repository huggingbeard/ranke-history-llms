Foreword 

I wrote these little character studies of our faithful old servants, who have contributed so largely to the comfort and happiness of my life, because I love them and want my children, nieces and nephews to know them as I did. 

I hadn't the remotest idea they would go out of the family circle, but my husband thought our friends also might be interested in them and persuaded me to have this little booklet printed - which I offer with apologies. 

December, 1944. 

- Mabel Shands Saunders.

"AUNT EMMERLINE"

"Aunt Emmerline Tidwell"

My first menage consisted of a cook, Aunt Emmerline Tidwell by name, one of the "fore de war, black mammies," famous in Southern history, and as picturesque a character was she as was ever portrayed in history or fiction — and a part-time, man-of-all-works, Charley Webb. I will tell you more about him later. 

Aunt Emmerline was our "mainstay." We had a great affection for her and she for us, but she had very little respect for my knowledge about housekeeping. If I attempted to tell her how I wanted anything cooked, she would say: "Git out uv my kitchen, Chile, I done made dat fore you was borned." And as what she made was always good, I got out.

Her waffles were famous among some of our friends and she gloried in showing off her wares. Her hospitality was so great that she used to go out in our side yard, that bordered on the drive around the University Campus, in the afternoons and if she saw any of our close friends she would hail them and say: "Light and come in and have supper with us," and offered, as inducement, to give them "waffles and all due respect."

I wish it were possible for me to portray her to you now as I saw her then. After her supper was cooked and served she would take her stand by the dining room door, tray under arm, and wait for applause, which was always forthcoming. And she was as famous for her ghost stories as for her waffles, and someone would be sure to say: "Aunt Emmerline, won't you tell us about that time the 'hant' nearly caught you down by the graveyard?" or some 3

other of her hair-raising adventures. She would launch forth and was as dramatic as Sara Bernhardt in her palmiest days.

The poor old soul was a prey to all the superstitions of her race and times. She believed in ghosts, hoo-doos, and all other signs and symbols. The house in which we lived was one of the University Professors' homes and had been the scene of a great tragedy many years before we inhabited it. Some new members of the Board of Trustees decided they needed "new blood" in the faculty and several of the dear old men had been ruthlessly discharged. Of course, they had no savings on which to live. A college professor then got barely enough salary on which to live and support his family. The poor old man living in our house, seeing no decent future for himself, just committed suicide. So the colored folks regarded our place as being "hanted."

Some mornings when I asked Aunt Emmerline how she felt she would say: "Poly, Chile, I ain't slep a wink. Old man Squinch was a-walkin' agin las night." I said: "Aunt 

Emmerline, you know that isn't true." "Oh, yes, it is, Honey! I smelt him. Ain't you ever smelt a hant?" "No, how do they smell?" "Kiner sweetish like sorter like a rattlesnake." I sincerely hoped it was a ghost and not a rattlesnake she smelled in my yard. 

She also believed strongly in "hoo-doos" and Charley

Webb, being quite intelligent, and with some education, did not believe in any such foolishness, and just delighted in scaring her. We had to threaten to discharge him if he did not stop teasing her.

One morning we came down to find we had no breakfast. Aunt Emmerline had found a "conjur bag" in front of her kitchen door and no power could have induced her to cross that threshhold. We had to find Charley Webb and make him confess he had put it there, and to fully convince her, he had to tell exactly what was in the bag before he opened it in front of our eyes.

Another time she came in with an empty milk bucket. 

I asked her what was the matter. She said: "Dat ole cow jist w-a-l-l-i-e-d her eyes at me and I knowed Rackamore done sot a spell on her." Charley had to milk for several days till the cow seemed to return to normalcy. 

Aunt Emmerline, like all of her race and generation, was very religious, and above all other rites enjoyed a funeral. She belonged to a "Buryin' Society" and donned the costume on all possible occasions. It was a royal purple basque over a black calico skirt and a bright yellow sash around the waist. She came home from a funeral one day greatly troubled in spirit. I asked her what was the matter. 

She said she "jes knowed dat preacher done tacked some years on Sister Isabel Shegog. I sho don't want him to be tackin' no years on me when I dies." 

She seemed to me far beyond the age when a few years more or less mattered - whether dead or alive, but she said: "Honey, I wants you to sit down and write up to

Tennessee to Marse Joe to look up in de garrit and git my age and send it to me. I know jus zackly where it is—in a little buro drawer in de cornder." But as she had no more accurate address for "Marse Joe" I could not write to him. But I tried to help her figure out her age as nearly as possible. The nearest fixed date she had was "when de stars fell." She said: "I was a little gal sleepin' on a pallet by my ole missus' bed. She was sick and in de night she said to me 'Emmerline, take de piggin and go down to de spring and fetch me some cool water. I got an innard scotching fever.' I got de piggin and went out in de yard, and, Lordy-mussy! Glory-Halleluya!! All de stars was a fallin out of de Heavens. I run back in de house and me and Ole Miss fell down on our knees and spent de night a-prayin.'' The stars fell in 1833, so I figured out about how old she was from that. All her dates were from "since der srender" or "fore de war" and according to her ideas but few things had been so good since then. 

One day she spoke of "fore de war" and just to tease

her someone asked: "Before what war, Aunt Emmerline?" 

She replied, with great disgust: "Fore what war? Why fore de war 'tween de white folks and de Yankees." 

After our commencement was over in June, and the noisy students had gone home, the quiet of the nights on the campus was disturbed only by the croaking of the frogs in the lotus ponds and the singing of the katydids in the grand old trees. But our restful peace was not for long, for in a week or so the summer normal school began. One morning Aunt Emmerline came in and announced: "De

Normans done come agin. It's jist like when de Yankees come down South," and she resented the invasion just as much.

Aunt Emmerline was not only a fine cook, but wonderful doctor with home remedies. Once she came to my room and found me "croaking" from a heavy bronchial cold. She said: "Chile, I sees you got a misery in de chest. I'm gwineter get some tuppin-tine, kerosene and mutton suet and rub it fer you. De Lamb was de ceptable offerin' to de Lord and his fat has got de healin' power." And I tell you now, when applied by her powerful hands her remedy penetrates to the marrow of one's bones.

Aunt Emmerline was our Guardian Angel till we went to Germany, where we were going to be for fifteen months while my husband was studying for his Doctor of Philosophy Degree. While we were away she passed on to the everlasting life promised to all true believers, but I doubt if she finds anything in Heaven she enjoys more than she did her "Buryin' Society" down here in this "vale of tears."

Charley Webb 

Charley Webb was the "handyman" around "Ole Miss"

University Campus. In fact, I think he was the handiest man I ever saw anywhere.

It was amazing the number of different things he could do for different people in one day. He began his day about 

5 A. M. by building fires for the boys in dozens of dormitory rooms. (There was no central heating at "Ole Miss" till after 1900). Then he would black their shoes and when they went to breakfast he would clean their rooms. Then he would run any errands they wished - such as carrying notes to their girls to make dates to call, or play tennis, or go buggy riding — or maybe to a dance. There were no telephones then either. If there was to be a dance, all the dress suits had to be pressed. All of this before his real day's work began.

He performed all these services for my husband when a student and living in the "Bachelor Professors' House."

When we were married, we came into possession of the last unoccupied Professor's house. And as that was between the two dormitories and right on his way, Charley took us on too. But we had many more chores for him to do than just fires, shoes, etc. He had to plant and work our garden; feed and curry our horse, and saddle or hitch him up to the buggy any time we wanted him. When we had a dinner party he had to wait on the table. 

Our fine Jersey cow which was given to us as a wedding present had a very delicate digestion and Charley frequently had to tie her up to the garden fence and "drench" her.

CHARLEY WEBB AND "MR. PAUL"

In short, he was indispensable to us. But he managed to take care of all our needs and those of the students also. 

One of his services for the students was strictly sub-rosa. 

It was considered quite a “lark” for the students to go out some dark of the moon to a nearby chicken yard and steal a turkey. Charley would cook it for them and they would have a midnight feast in their dormitory room. 999 Charley came very nearly knowing about all the campus escapades and he very frequently received subpoenas to appear as witness before the faculty or in court. He said one day: “I don't know whut makes 'em keep on summaging me - I never knows nothin.' That trait is characteristic of most of his race not one of them will know where anybody else lives- their last name - what they have been doing, or anything else about them, until they learn it is to the advantage of the one sought to have that information given. That is their idea of loyalty to a friend, and I guess it is a pretty good one. The students were distinctly Charley's friends. 

One time one of these dear boys was caught in the act and was brought up before the faculty for trial. One of the pious, pompous old professors said the boy should be expelled, for a boy who would steal a turkey when a student, would steal a horse when a man. My husband, who had first been a student, then Fellow, then Assistant Professor, then full Professor, had to stand up before that august assembly and confess he had stolen a turkey when a student, but did not believe he would steal a horse now, and quoted from one of our beloved Southern poets that "The sinfulness of sin depends on the spirit you goes and does it in." Well, that saved the boy. He was just given a bunch of demerits and told to go and sin no more. 

The news of this sinless sinning reached Charley, took root in his mind and bore fruit very promptly.

We used to buy a whole coop of chickens at a time from country farmers, who brought them to the campus, and then just turn them loose in our chicken yards or hen

houses. In one lot I got one of the most peculiar looking chickens I ever beheld. All its feathers were turned backwards and it had no tail feathers at all. It would be unmistakable anywhere. Occasionally, I bought chickens from Charley too. In one lot from him the "Unmistakable 

One" reappeared. It had evidently been the dark of the moon when Charley visited my chicken yard last. I said: "Charley, you should have known you could not sell me that chicken of mine again." But you couldn't outsmart him he said: "Fine, Miss Mabel, I just wanted to see if you was a careful wife for Mr. Paul."

Our life at the University was very sweet and pleasant in many ways. We had delightful companions in the families of the faculty. We were surrounded by bright, happy boys, and their merry spirits were infectious. We had many very dear friends in Oxford. Everything was fine except the salary, and that was sufficient until our son was born. But after doctors' bills, nursing and insurance had to be paid it was hard to make ends meet, and impossible to save anything for our old age and a "rainy day." 

Our dearest man friend in Oxford was a banker and I heard him tell my husband it was a sin for a man with his business ability to teach Greek for $2,000.00 a year for the rest of his life. So, when he was offered a position in a bank shortly after that, I persuaded him to accept it. He resigned his position of Professor of Greek on June 10th, and became President of a "wobbly" little bank in Southern Mississippi the next day, June 11th. 

And Charley Webb left home, wife and children and went with us. 

There he did double duty again. He was janitor in the bank, and man of all work for us even to being nurse for our four year old son occasionally. He used to put him up on our very high old horse and hold him there and ride around the block for hours at a time, or do anything else the boy wanted done. He said: "I take care of you now, boy, and you must take care of me when I am old," which 10

the boy promised to do. And don't you believe Charley lets us forget that promise now.

Charley carried with him to South Mississippi his small vices and greater virtues. Mississippi was a prohibition state, but now and then my husband would bring me a much treasured bottle of whiskey from New Orleans. I found, however, that after the first using this whiskey was disappearing, but the level was kept up by adding water. 

I showed the bottle to Charley and said: "Charley, what about this?" He said: "Miss Mabel, you know I just can't resist likker. You ought to put it where I can't find it." 

I agreed - but there was no such place in my home. 

His honesty in important matters was illustrated, however, in quite a substantial way. In my husband's small bank (which was supposed to close at 3 o'clock in the afternoon) they kept an "after-hours" box in which depositors could put deposits after 3 P. M. When the bank finally closed this was to be put into the safe, and entered on the books the next morning.

One afternoon one of the Assistant Cashiers hurried off to see a baseball game and forgot to put the "after-hours" box in the safe. He remembered it later in the evening and rushed back to the bank, but found it gone. About daybreak he called my husband up, actually weeping, and told him of the loss, and said he would work the rest of his life to pay it back. My husband comforted him and said it would no doubt turn up, and they could fix it all right he was sure.

A little later, when Charley came in to make the fire, he put the "after-hours" box on my husband's bed, and said: "Mr. Paul, you ought to get after that boy for being so careless. He left this box out and I took it home, and 

I ain't slept all night because I was afraid somebody might get that money from under my mattress."

A natural feeling about certain things - what was ours was his — was an inheritance from the good old days typified by the saying: "Massa's chicken, Massa's nigger.” 11

After six years in South Mississippi we moved to New Orleans, and, of course, Charley wanted to go with us. 

But in the "Dryest," strictest prohibition state in the Union he could manage to get "white lightning," or some other kind of whiskey - -or Jamaica ginger, or something else to get drunk on all too often for safety. And we knew in a "wide open" place like New Orleans he would never be sober.

I had often suffered mental anguish as I watched him go weaving towards the butler pantry door, carrying my best dishes, and on one occasion hit the wall instead. So we had a sad parting from our dear servant and friend.

But we have kept in close touch with him through all these years. For some reason or other he wants to "touch" us quite often. I had a funny letter from him once - he wrote: "Dear Miss Mabel - I have been elected delegate to a Methodist Conference in St. Louis, Mo. All I need to put Mississippi on the map is the proper garments." He had been wearing my husband's old clothes for thirty years, but I felt that this occasion demanded something better, so I sent him a cut-a-way coat, lined with taffeta silk that rustled beautifully when he walked, and the striped trousers to go with it. I had put that suit in and out of moth balls every spring and fall for several years without its being used once. Needless to say I had hardly given it away when some occasion demanding such an outfit arose, and I had to confess that I had given it to Charley to help put Mississippi on the map. 

Charley had been a Prophet not without honor in his own land for many long years. He was always the orator on all big occasions of his church. Some of the students usually wrote his speeches. He especially liked the ones my brother wrote for him that never used a word of less than four or five syllables if one that large could be fitted in. He was also a great debater and some of his extemporaneous retorts were wonderful. Once he floored his opponent by saying: "Wylie Hope, you are too slow for these 12

times. Some day your head is going to be knocked off by a passing event."

Many years after we had moved to New Orleans, when my son was going to be married, we sent Charley an invitation to the wedding - which he accepted. When he arrived all the family and six house guests were having breakfast. 

He came in beaming and walked around the table greeting each of us as long lost friends. When he came to my husband's place he put his hand on his shoulder and said: "Mr. Paul, Miss Mabel sent me an invitation to come down to my boy's wedding, but she was so busy she forgot to send me the money to buy the railroad ticket. But I just told my wife I would advance it to you, and you could reimburse me when I started home." A chorus went up from around the table: "Of course he will, Charley." And, of course, he did. He must have made the loan with which to "advance the money to Mr. Paul" from some mutual friend in Oxford who would have joined in that chorus if he had been with us.

The prize letter we received from Charley came a few years ago. It ran: "Dear Mr. Paul: I seat myself with pen in hand to let you know how I am gittin along. These are hard times, Mr. Paul, to git along in. They is hard times, Mr. Paul, for young men to git along in and you knows I ain't young any more now. You have always been mighty good to me, Mr. Paul. You done helped me out wid de funeral expenses when Alice died. You done helped me out wid my mortgages, and every time I ever bin in trouble you helped me out. But I am telling you one thing,

Mr. Paul, past favors ain't doin' me no good now-present help is what I needs. Let me hear from you soon. I hope you and Miss Mabel keeps well and enjoys God's blessins. 

Your old servant & friend - Charley Webb Saunders." (The Saunders added for effect.)

Charley's daughter, Pauline (named for Paul) wrote him recently and asked him to send the money to her the next time he was going to send it to Charley for he wasted it. 

I know very well it is "wasted" on the "Oh, be joyful" and 13

I continue to send it to him direct for he is eighty 

years old, or over, and if he can occasionally forget the hard times he complains of, and the infirmities of his age, I, for one, am all for aiding and abetting the "waste.” 

Last Christmas my husband was at the University for a day. As soon as Charley heard the news he came posthaste to see him-clad in rags and tatters. Paul said: "You old rascal, you know you just wore those old clothes to impress me. You know you have better ones." Charley laughed at being understood so perfectly at any rate he was promised a new suit and a bottle of whiskey. I sent him one of the quite good suits very promptly, but no whiskey. A month later this letter followed: 

"Dear Mr. Paul: Have you forgot your promise to send me a suit of clothes and some whiskey? Miss Mabel done sent me a suit, but you aint. The whiskey now is so pore 

I done stopped botherin about it. If you would drink enough to get drunk it would kill you. Goodby and God bless you - Your friend Charley Webb." 

The latest letter of March 2nd, 1944, read: "Dear Mr. Paul: A few lines to inform you I am still living and feeling very well. I bin sick with the flu but am up now. I wrote you about four weeks ago and thanked you for the last check and asked you to send me a couple of pairs of cotton flannel drawers. I wear them all summer. Son got me some shirts but they didn't have any drawers. But I don't think the fellow mailed that letter now I hope you will attend to it at once as I am in need and you told me you was going to see that I would have all I needed while I live.

I could write more but I am gittin weak and nervous. 

Give my love to Miss Mabel & the children. I hope to see you all once more in life. Write to me soon. 

Your old servant & friend - Charley Webb."

This order I promptly filled and hope to fill many more before he is finally called. 14

SOPHY 

Sophy and Sonny-Boy

Sophy was my mother's cook, and when I say cook I mean just that, for Sophy could do it. The modern term "help" could convey no slight idea of Sophy. Even now when I think of her light, fluffy biscuits, feather-weight waffles, salt-rising bread, fried chicken, barbecued spring lamb, and many other "delectables" I get so hungry I can hardly stand it. 

And a cook had to be a cook at our house for it was a full time job with three big, square meals a day, every day of the year. Besides my father, mother, grandmother, four brothers and myself there were nearly always one or two relatives visiting us or living with us. It was just generally understood that when a boy in the family was ready for college he would go to stay with "Uncle Shands and Aunt Mollie" and go through the University. So we always had nine or ten places at the table - which was long enough to add one or two more in case a friend dropped in at the last moment. 15

My father bought flour, sugar and Louisiana open-kettle sugar house molasses by the barrel, rice by the hundred pound sack, hams by the dozen, and a whole cheese, as large as a barrel head, at a time. He said his garden was his golf links and he, with old Rodgers' help to do the plowing and heavy work, had the best one in town. 

In this connection I am going to digress a bit and tell you of an amusing incident that occurred to us: My husband was the Professor on the Faculty who had charge of the program for the "Lecture Bureau." He had invited 

William Jennings Bryan (still famous for his "Cross of Gold and Crown of Thorns" speech) to address the students of the University. We thought it fitting to have the distinguished citizen entertained by the Dean of the Law School (my father) while there. He was expected to arrive on a train at 6 P. M. We had invited twelve other distinguished guests for dinner at 7, but to our dismay he arrived on a noon train, just before we were going to have a simple vegetable lunch, giving time to prepare an elaborate dinner for the evening. We were going to "put the big pot in the little one." For lunch Sophy had just cooked a big pot of tender young turnip greens, made corn pones to eat with them, gathered a big lot of little onions, radishes and young lettuce leaves for which she fixed her famous hardboiled egg salad dressing. She had fried some ham, to make gravy for the rice, which we always had three times a day and that was all. So when the unexpected guest arrived we sent an S. O. S. to a dear friend of ours who "fared sumptuously" every day. She snatched two broiled chickens and a cherry pie from her stove and sent them to us.

With that addition to our menu we felt satisfied to invite our guest in. When he set his eyes on the turnip greens and corn pone and tender young vegetables he smacked his lips and "fell to." He ate practically all of them. Never have I seen a man enjoy food as he did that. 

Well, the family enjoyed the borrowed company food which he did not touch.

-- Now back to Sophy — As I said before, she had plenty to do and loved doing it. She was one of the most powerful creatures I ever saw a regular "Amazon." She was about six feet tall, with a large frame, strong muscles, but not a pound of superfluous flesh. I don't think she ever missed a day from illness in the whole twenty-five years she worked for us with one notable exception. 

One morning my mother came down to breakfast to find 

Sophy had gone - "lock, stock and barrel." Her clothes and bedding had been taken from her house. She had just vanished. We knew not how, where nor why. But we knew she had not been kidnapped or violently abducted for no human being was powerful enough for that. There had been a big Baptist protracted camp meeting for the colored brethren in Oxford that summer and Sophy admired and liked the visiting revivalist very much indeed. 

So we thought that, in her religious fervor, she might have followed him to other fields. So we had to get another cook to fill her place- or try to. Her place was hard to fill. We had several to make the experiment without success. But exactly a year from the day on which she disappeared she walked in and said: "Miss Mollie, here I is." 

The cute little "Pickaninny" in her arms was explanation enough. Never a question was asked. Well, the little boy, called Sonny-boy, was raised in our back yard and he was one of the greatest freaks ever born — half moron, half genius. Our home at the University was near a deep railway cut and that little fellow sat by it for hours at a time waiting and watching for trains, and without a pencil, a drawing or chart of any kind he could take a pair of scissors and an old newspaper and cut out freehanded a train perfect in every detail.

And he had one of the very rare mathematical minds that could calculate dates years, ages ago, without even stopping to think. Like "What day of the week was May 

10th, 1742?" Answer "Tuesday," or whatever happened to be correct. His mathematical calculations were as accurate 17

as a Burroughs Machine. No one has ever been able to explain the method. 

I don't think Sonny-boy ever learned his letters. Sophy could read and write but had but little time, or taste, for such. She kept busy feeding chickens, setting hens, or patching quilts made of scraps of our dresses. 

She gave 

me a quilt as a wedding present to start housekeepin' on she said. 

Even when "Sonny-boy" was very young, Sophy realized he was not quite normal and she kept salt rubbed in his little kinky head to drive the evil spirits away.

When my son was a little fellow his greatest delight was to be allowed to play with "Sonny-boy." One day I had him all dressed up, spic and span, to see a friend of ours from a distant town. After being on exhibition awhile he said to me: "Movver, may I get filfy dirdy?" Our friend said: "What did he say?" I explained that he wanted to be allowed to play with Sonny-boy when he always got "filthy dirty." 

A few years later my father left "Ole Miss" and went to teach law at Tulane. They boarded in New Orleans instead of keeping house as all the children had finished school, married and located in different places. So Sophy went back to the little town from which she came. 

Shortly after that the one policeman the town afforded was baffled by a series of mystifying burglaries. Stores would be broken into but nothing would be missing, cash registers never disturbed. 

But one night he saw a big colored boy go to a store and break a window with his bare fist and go in. He hid outside and watched to see what the thief would do when inside. Well, he hunted all over the store carefully, putting some things in his pockets. The policeman then followed him to a loft in a barn and there found a great heap of sardines and “juice-harps” — things the store proprietors had not looked for. After that the boy was made to sleep in the jail, but he was allowed to wander at large by day for he was harmless. So Sophy 18

made herself useful around the jail and stayed there too. 

But after a little while they wandered off to a nearby farm. They could always get a job for both were good workers. They would stay there a while till they decided they wanted to go somewhere else. So we could not keep track of them. 

Another span of years passed without our seeing Sophy or even hearing from her. But the day before my son was to be married - in New Orleans - our doorbell rang and our polite butler, "Eddie," answered and came to the kitchen, where I was very busy, and said: "Mrs. Saunders, there is a lady at the door wants to see you." But the "lady" needed nothing but for the door to be opened. There she stood by Eddie's side, shoes in hand. She had walked the five miles from the station to our house and her feet hurt her. After she had finished embracing me and I had caught my breath sufficiently to speak, I said: "Sophy, where did you come from?" She sa: "Honey, I been hearin' voices - they wouldn't let me sleep - they said 'Go to Miss Mollie, go to Miss Mollie,'— so I jes got on the train and come. And here I find my boy is gittin' married and you ain't even tole me." I tried to make her realize I didn't know where to find her. 

After a little visit to us she vanished from our lives again only reappearing about ten years later when she heard that my precious little mother- her beloved "Miss

Mollie" was to be buried in our old home town, where she had first served us. The voices in her faithful heart called again and she came to mingle her tears with ours.

After eight years of silence I thought our good, old Sophy must have gone on to join our host of friends in the "Great Beyond." But a month ago we had another of her surprise visits. 

She looked so fine, well and strong, I said: "Sophy, you don't look a day older than you did ten years ago. How do you do it?" She said: "I 'spect the Lord jis does it for me." 

I am sure He does for she is one of His own lambs. She 19

said: "I wish you could see how much cotton I picked last fall." I told her if she could stoop over and pick cotton I wished I could swap backs with her. She laughed and said: "I can hoe and plow too." 

I asked if Sonny-boy was working on the farm too. She said: "No-m. He won't hang around me much. He says

I ain't never told him he is a man - and try to boss him too much. He ‘keeps bach' in Oxford. And he says he ain't never goin' to git married. He had a little house by hisself.

He works awhile and gits some money and goes and buys a lot of food and cooks it up and invites a lot of folks to come in and help him eat it up - I guess he took that from you, Miss Mabel."

Well she stayed a month with us but the spring weather was getting in her blood and her old fingers began itching to get to digging and planting again. She has pulled up every weed and stray blade of grass in my small yard. 

So she left us yesterday. I told her I had loved her visit and would miss her. She said: "I will miss you too, and 

I sho' hate to leave you and Mr. Paul looking so feeble. 

Now you must write me and let me know when you get broken-down and I will come and take keer of you."

Every time we part we expect our next meeting to be in Heaven. 20

Eddie When little Eddie was born into the Jackson family, he being the fourteenth child, his pious father said: "De good

Lord done been too good to me. He done sent me more chillun dan I kin feed and take keer of. I'm gwine ter ask de good sisters at Saint Adolfus orfin house if dey won't take dis chile off my hans. Couse he ain't zackly no orfin but he mout as well be fer all I kin do fer him." 

And as there is always room for one more with the good Sisters, they took him and raised him to be as near a saint himself as ever a little colored boy could be.

During the war I felt it my patriotic duty to let my man of all work go to serve his country in some essential way. 

I did my own driving and hired a girl to do the butler's work. But when the war was over and men were back hunting jobs I could have one with a clear conscience. So

When I returned in the fall I learned, to my horror, that my $400.00 had been used as a down-payment on a house valued at $6,000.00 by the Homestead Association. Eddie had decided it would be better business to build a double duplex so that the tenant on the other side of the house could pay the interest on the whole investment. 

Then our trouble began in earnest. I say our trouble because he was too tender hearted to make his tenant pay his rent and when his insurance or interest fell due he never had the money with which to pay and I had to put it up or he would lose all we had invested. 

Now, also, this fine house had to have fine furniture. 

So he bought a bedroom suite that cost $450.00, and put a 23

second mortgage on his house as security. He let payments on that pass by until the interest compounded (and confounded) brought the cost up to about $600.00. Then the company was about to take the house and he had to come to me for further help. I went to see the furniture dealer to see if I could persuade him to take the furniture back as part payment. He refused to do so and I lost my temper so completely that I told the man I thought that Shylock was a Christian gentleman in comparison to a man who would sell a poor negro, with a wife and three children such an expensive set of furniture and then try to take his house for payment. Needless to say that did not help a bit.

Eddie had always told me he was going to pay me back when he got his bonus from service in the war. The bonus came just at this time and went to pay for the furniture. 

One morning Eddie said: "A friend of mine certainly did have good luck last night. His house burned down and he had good insurance on it. Do you think it would be a sin for me to pray for my house to burn too?" My husband replied: "It would be no sin if you did not try to help the Lord out." 

Well, this thing ran on until I had "advanced" more money than I was willing to confess to my family. So I let the Homestead Association take the house back.

After Eddie had moved into another house he came in beaming, and said: "It certainly is nice not to have anything but the rent to worry about." I, too, felt greatly relieved and just decided that all the money I had lost might be counted up as my treasure laid up in Heaven, and I hoped the Lord would forgive me my trespasses as 

I forgave Eddie his debts.

Eddie's hope and faith were great, but far greater than these were his charity. For quite a while my husband was greatly afflicted from arthritis. He said he felt as if a giant had hold of his legs and was trying to break them. 

He was confined to bed several months and had day and night nurses to try to ease his pain. The day nurse had help from members of the family which was quite satisfactory all around. But we could find no night nurse who would massage his legs or apply the infra-red lamp all night long. So I had to let him have the never-tiring, always willing Eddie for night nurse and get me another helper in his place for the day.

At last the various doctors treating him agreed that an operation was necessary. Naturally, I was frantically anxious about it. Eddie said: "Don't you worry, Mrs. 

Saunders. Doctor is going to be all right. I have made nine novenas for him." And he was all right.

Eddie was one person who verified the statement that "Whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth." He had many trials and tribulations. He had music in his soul and was the composer of many "Blues" - "Pretty Audrey," "Red 

Onion Blues," and several others. And he trained several of his friends, forming an orchestra to play for fraternity dances. Some of the musicians thought it would be fine to have phonograph records made of them. So Eddie got 25

the Victor Company to come down from Chicago and they made the records. When all expenses were paid, each member of the orchestra getting $50.00, there was exactly $11.00 left for Eddie, the composer, director and manager.

But a far sadder tragedy befell him. The priest in his diocese wanted to give a benefit performance for some worthy purpose and asked Eddie to play for him. Of course, he did. When he went in to supper he laid on the piano his clarinet (which was given to him by Secretary Baker for distinguished services to the sick and wounded in the hospitals and on the ambulances during the war). When he returned from supper the clarinet was gone. He was heartbroken for it was his greatest pride and joy. I tried to comfort him and said: "Eddie, the priest may get it back for you. Someone may confess he stole it." He replied: "Oh, Mrs. Saunders, it may have been a Baptist who took it." 

Before moving away from New Orleans, I secured a good position for Eddie with a firm with which my husband was connected, and I felt quite satisfied about him. 

But several weeks later I learned he had left that job and had taken one at the Post Office for he said there was more chance for promotion there - he could work up to $2,200 a year. His ambition and conscientious service far exceeded his strength. To begin his day's work there he had to take the air mail out to the airport for the five o'clock plane. That meant he had to leave home about four o'clock. He went without breakfast, and his work seemed to come at two hour intervals, giving him no time for real rest, and he ate only "snatches" between times.

He had always been a very small eater and I had forced him to drink milk and urged him to eat more when he worked for me. Without this proper care he developed tuberculosis. 

When I returned home I heard he was quite ill. I rushed out to see him and found he had temperature 105°. 

It was Sunday afternoon and my family physician was out 26

of town, so I called a good friend to go with me to get him into Flint Goodridge Hospital before night. He was kind enough to leave a party announcing his engagement to be married to go with me. I guess that gave his fiancee her first idea of what a doctor's wife could expect.

After two weeks the acute attack was over and they allowed me to take him home - which he begged me to do. I then arranged to put him into a Tuberculosis Hospital a beautiful place with everything to make him comfortable and get him well. I had hardly left town again before he left the hospital and went back home. 

Two weeks later I heard, to my great grief, that Eddie had died quite suddenly. But I am sure he now has a golden harp to replace the lost clarinet. 27

PART II Nurses 

My conscience will not let me rest because I did not even mention, in my little sketchbook, the sweet old nurses my children had. I feel I have been disloyal to some of the best friends I ever had. 

They were tried and true 

and were with me not only in my happy hours, when the babies were well, but in the agonizing ones when they were ill and I didn't know what to do. 28

In the little college town where we lived there was no pediatrician. Just the dear old "Family Doctor" who gave the baby a powder and trusted the mother and nurse for the rest of the cure.

Aunt Sallie 

Aunt Sallie was my first nurse and such a wonderful one she was! She was a semi-professional nurse and charged more than we could afford to pay for many weeks. 

But my baby was very delicate and we had to keep her for several months. 

After she gave him his morning bath she would say: "Now lay down on Mammie's lap you pore little skint rabbit, and let her git some fat on them little bones of yours." And she would anoint him with lanolin and cocoa butter from head to toe. While gently massaging him she would sweetly croon a lullaby and soon he would be off to happy dreamland. Her treatment was so successful that before a year we got a less expensive nurse and let Aunt Sallie go help out some other, poor, ignorant young mother. Sallie

Sallie was such a good name for a nurse that the next one we employed was named Sallie too. But quite a different Sallie was she. She was a handsome, buxom young wench. The kind that frequently causes trouble - and she did. This is how it happened: 

My mother had a two seated surrey — and it really did have "a fringe on top" and to it was hitched a pair of dapper bay ponies. And it was driven by Jim, a good looking young "Buck." Every day my mother came over and took us for a drive, Sallie and the baby riding on the front heat with Jim. Her enjoyment of this was so evident it was beyond the endurance of her husband, who worked around the campus of "Ole Miss" where we always drove. 

One day when I went over to my mother's house I found

Jim the most forlorn picture I ever beheld. Both eyes 29

were nearly closed, his nose was out of joint, and over the cuts in his face the salve was held in place with pieces of white cotton which made quite a striking color contrast. 

He really was a picture of woe. His beauty was completely spoiled.

After this was accomplished, Sallie's husband said: "I done had my saterfaction! He can have her now." Poor, innocent, forsaken Sallie! She was afraid to stay out in her house alone, so she soon left us and followed her husband, and I trust they had a happy reunion. 

Aunt Sallie 

As strange as it may seem, my next nurse was named Sallie too but she was "middle-aged" so was in the "Aunt" class. I would not have thought of calling her just "Sallie."

When she came to us the hot summer weather had set in and my little boy began to wilt - just as the flowers did in the scorching sun. So I had to take him to the mountains for two or three months. And I needed a great deal of help and comfort, all of which Aunt Sallie would give me.

In those good old days, there were lovely little mountain resorts in North Carolina which even a college professor could afford. So off to Arden Park Lodge went

Aunt Sallie, my sick baby and I. But let me add (confidentially) that my husband had to teach a term in Summer 

School at Knoxville, Tenn., to finance the outing for us.

However, he spent one month with us in the mountains and had a fine time.

It was a delightful place — always filled with charming people, mostly from Charleston and other parts of the "low country" of South Carolina. It would have been just lovely if Aunt Sallie hadn't gotten homesick. But it is almost impossible to transplant an elderly negro even for a summer. So Aunt Sallie began worrying about things 30

at home. Especially did she agonize over David. She said: "David is the only bad chile I ever did have. But he just keeps on sowin' them wild oats and I can't do nothin' to stop him. I done whupped him, I done prayed over him, I done smoked him with chicken feathers, and nothin' ain't done him no good." I asked her just how she had smoked him with chicken feathers. She said she stood him up in the chimney and set the feathers afire in a pot at his feet. 

I could never have kept her through the summer if it had not been for another nice old nurse up there with whom she formed a close friendship. They were “birds of a feather" who just naturally "flocked together."

And to her I owe an irrepayable debt of gratitude. I think she saved my baby's life. 

She told Aunt Sallie, "That chile is just starvin' to death." Aunt Sallie answered: "Whut you goin' to do with a chile whut won't eat eggs nor drink milk?" The reply was "Give him Rackahoo - they makes it at my boss's store in Memphis." And she gave my baby some of her babies' food which he ate with delight and he began to mend from the first mouthful.

This was a baby food containing farina of wheat, arrow root, and cocoa.

Aunt Annie

She was no larger than the little girl I had let go, but she had been nursing for fifty years, and was a darling. 31

She had the brightest, merriest little face I ever saw and always came in beaming like the morning sun. Her greeting was always the same: "Good mornin', Miss Mable. It's a nice day for the washerwoman" or "a bad day for the washerwoman" as the case might be.

She was indignant to find that so large a child could not talk better. She said: "How that young gal 'speck a chile to know how to talk if she don't never talk to him none?" And she jabbered like a magpie to him all day long and soon he was jabbering too.

Poor little Aunt Aunnie had matrimonial troubles too. 

She had been with us nearly two years when she astounded us with the announcement that she was going to get married and settle down. We hated to lose her but if she would go, we would send her off in good style. My mother, who was a little woman too gave her a dress - a gray satin one for the wedding garment. The poor little soul was as happy a bride as ever lived. 

But her felicity was short lived. Four days later she returned a betrayed, deserted woman. The young scoundrel to whom she had entrusted her life had persuaded her to entrust him with her fortune also, and with all her life's savings he had skipped the country to parts unknown.

My baby girl was six years younger than her brother, and when she was born we were living in a newer part of the state, in the timberlands more recently settled, where there were but few "old-timers." But I was lucky enough to get a darling old "Black Mammy" to nurse her, Callie by name. I missed a wonderful opportunity by not taking down every word Callie uttered. Her expressions were priceless. But I was too busy and too distraught by the care of a very delicate baby to think of anything else then, and now I have forgotten nearly all of them. 

My daughter's first letter to Santa Claus asked for "a live dog, a live lion, and a gun," a rather difficult order for him to fill. But he did get a lovely shaggy silk lion which she adored. Shortly after Christmas she was taken 33

with pneumonia and developed empyema and was desperately ill for months. She kept the lion in her arms day and night and would run her little fingers through his silky mane all the time. After several weeks we thought he must be too full of germs to be sanitary, and gave her a silk shawl with a long fringe on it as a substitute for the lion's mane. After a while that too had to be taken away to be washed. Then she began twisting her little fingers through her hair. Callie said: "Yes! They jes took away your silk lion and your silk shawl but they can't take away your pretty little silk head." She was as inordinately proud of her pretty red curls as was her fond mamma.

From the red head she began calling her "Little Red 

Riding Hood," and then abbreviated it to "Little Hood" nickname she bears to this day with some of her family. 

When she had almost recovered from pneumonia she developed colitis and we had to be very strict with her diet and the poor little thing was almost starved. The white of a raw egg was one thing allowed her. One day

Callie and the baby were out in the back yard sitting in the sun. Callie was so engaged in conversation with the cook she didn't hear the hen cackle or notice the baby toddle into the chicken yard, and take the egg and crush it in her hands and begin eating it—white, yolk, shell and all, 'till the cook said: "Lord, God, look whut that baby's doin'." Callie ran and picked her up and said: "Come here, you little egg-sucking pup, you!" and ran and grabbed her up in her arms and gave her a loving little pat.

The next day I learned what was the absorbing subject under discussion then. Callie said: "Miss Mabel, Celie is goin' to quit." I asked her why and said I thought she was quite satisfied with her job. She said: "Yes-m, she wus, but she ain't now." I asked what was wrong now. 

She said: "Celie says you wus the fust white folks she e-v-e-r has worked fer whut n-e-v-e-r does buy a watermelon." I urged her to persuade Celie not to quit, for she was an excellent cook, and promised to keep her in water- 34

The next summer we spent at Mont Eagle, Tennessee, which was a Chatauqua. One of their annual events was a baby show. Both Callie and I were certain our baby would take first prize. Surely no other could be as beautiful as she was, with her head full of bright red curls, and the lovely white skin that goes with red hair, and those big, brown eyes.

We went to no end of trouble to decorate the carriage with delicate pink paper chrysanthemums and it was lovely, and she in it, too. Callie was very temperamental but in her heart deeply religious. When she came in one morning I said: "How are you today, Callie?" and she said: "Miss Mabel, when

I think that deep feeling in the hearts of colored people, without adequate words to express it, is the origin of their "Spirituals" which are often just the repetition of one line over and over again. "Swing low, sweet chariot, comin' for to carry me home, Swing low, sweet chariot, comin' for to carry me home" over and over.

When my daughter was two and a half years old, we moved to New Orleans and, while looking for a house to buy, we boarded with a private family who had no place for Callie to stay. So we had to leave her in Mississippi.

And, anyway, we had learned that elderly colored people cannot be happily transferred.

So our next and last nurse was a very intelligent white girl. She was one of eleven children of a preacher, in a small Mississippi town. There were so many mouths to feed in the parsonage she decided to get out and earn her own living and help feed the others. And we were lucky enough to get her.
