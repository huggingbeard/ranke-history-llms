HONEYMOON BRIDGE 

HE two players sit opposite each other at the table. The players should provide themselves with two packs of cards with different backs which are easily distinguishable, and with Bridge score and pencil.

To begin the game one of the players spreads a pack face down on the table and both players draw a card from this one pack. 

The player drawing the higher card wins. He has the choice of cards and seats and has the first deal. The Ace is high and the suits rank in their regular order as in Bridge: spades-heartsdiamonds-clubs.

The one cutting the lower card shuffles the pack of cards chosen by dealer and cuts them towards the dealer. The dealer has the privilege of shuffling just before the cut, if he so desires. 

The dealer then gives the first card (face down) to his opponent and the next one to himself until thirteen have been distributed to each player. The remaining twenty-six cards are placed in a pile face down on the table a little to the right of the center. 

While the dealer is distributing the cards, the other player is shuffling the second pack for the next deal. Thus using two packs of cards serves two purposes. It saves time between the hands and secondly, it indicates who is to play the next hand, as the cards after being shuffled are placed at the shuffler's own right hand, ready to be passed to the opponent to be cut the moment the hand is finished.

The non-dealer is the first to play. He leads one of his thirteen cards and his opponent must follow if he holds one of the suit; if he does not hold one of the suit, he must discard. Whoever wins the first trick gathers it up and puts it at the side face down. The winner of the trick draws the top card from the pile and the other player takes the next card. The winner then leads to the next

HONEYMOON BRIDGE 

trick and each time the winning player draws the top card and the other player the next until the cards are all drawn. When the pack has thus been exhausted, it will be noted that each player holds just thirteen cards. Each player will hold thirteen cards throughout the drawing, as a card is replaced from the pile after each trick is played.

The player who wins the last trick begins the bidding. The bidding can be either Contract or Auction Bridge, whichever the players decide. The two players bid against each other as in the four-handed game and are permitted to double and redouble. 

When the bidding is completed, that is, after one player has passed, the latter player to bid becomes the Declarer with the amount of his contract to make. The opponent of the Declarer has the next lead. The play now goes on, following Bridge principles and these tricks are kept in order as in the regular game of Bridge. 

When the Declarer has won six tricks he has made up his book and all further tricks are those counted in scoring. He follows exactly the laws for scoring whichever game is being played, taking the rewards and penalties as they occur. Games and rubbers are scored exactly as in the four-handed games. If but one player is keeping the score, he should make his figures large enough to be easily seen by the other player.

As the twenty-six cards are played while the drawing is going on, a player can observe them and can mentally record each one of them. He can see his own thirteen cards which he has collected in his own hand. By elimination he can know the thirteen cards his opponent is holding. It can be seen that the game is a real memory test and can be played seriously and scientifically enough to suit any player.

From the very beginning of play a contestant can gather an idea of the suit which his opponent is collecting and this will guide him in determining the most advantageous suit to collect for himself. A player must always realize that the spade suit will outbid other suits which are able to win the same number of tricks and is, therefore, the most important suit. The suits follow their

HONEYMOON BRIDGE 

3 own ranking in importance. First the spade, then the heart, then the diamond, and the club suit comes last of all. 

In building up a hand, a player should, as the game advances, decide whether it is likely that he, or his opponent is making up the better hand. If it seems probable that his opponent is having better success with his drawings, it will then be wise to build up as strong a defense as possible, retaining cards of the suit which his opponent is obviously saving and which will be the trump; thereby giving up all hope of an offensive hand. Overbidding to prevent the opponent from making games and slams is an important feature of the game, and scientifically handled will mean the saving of many hundreds of points. 

The game holds ample opportunity for clever finesse. Playing one's own suit during the drawing stage when one can well afford to do so will often deceive the opponent and throw him off the track completely. The higher the card a player can afford to sacrifice for this purpose, the greater the chance he will have of accomplishing the desired results. A hand usually increases in interest as the playing and drawing progresses and often works up to a beautiful climax, particularly when both players are working for big hands, in which case there is much excitement in the final drawings.

HONEYMOON BRIDGE NO. 2 

There is another game of Bridge for just two players, which is not as interesting or as scientific as the one described above, but is sufficiently entertaining to appeal to many players and seems worthy of mention. We shall call it Honeymoon Bridge No. 2.

The two players draw for deal, cards and position as in Honeymoon Bridge No. 1. The dealer's opponent sits at the left of the dealer and not opposite. After the cards are cut by the opponent, the dealer distributes the cards as if there were four players in a regular game; that is, thirteen cards to each of the four hands. 

However, the cards dealt to the hands opposite both dealer and opponent are not placed in a pile, but are arranged face down on

HONEYMOON BRIDGE 

the table in a line side by side. When six cards have been dealt in this fashion to each hand opposite the players, the seventh card dealt each of these hands is placed face up on the first card; the eighth card is placed on the second and so on until all six are covered. The thirteenth card is also dealt face up. 

The two players' hands are dealt in the ordinary way with all thirteen cards face down in a pile in front of them. The two hands dealt to the vacant places are the players' dummy hands. Each player's dummy hand is directly opposite and each player plays the cards from his own dummy. 

The opponent of the dealer leads to the first trick and the play follows as in the regular game of Bridge; that is, a player must follow suit if he is able or if not he has the option of trumping or discarding. When an exposed card is played from a Dummy hand the face-down card underneath is turned over and left in the hand exposed until a player uses it. 

The tricks are collected and computed exactly as in Auction or Contract Bridge and the score is kept on the customary Bridge score card. Rubbers are played and the bonuses for honors, slams, and so forth are taken as the score card directs. 

Although there is a large amount of luck involved in this form of Honeymoon Bridge, there is, after all, considerable judgment needed in deciding whether to play a card from dummy so that another card will be immediately uncovered or whether to keep a card concealed for a longer time.

The dealer has the privilege of bidding first. He studies his own hand and his dummy hand and makes whatever bid he feels is justified with the combined hands. After the dealer declares or passes it is then the other player's turn to bid and the bidding continues between the two players until one player passes. In case there is no bid made by either player, the deal is thrown out and the other player deals.

After a bid by either player, the next player can then overbid, pass or double and the Auction thus continues until one player passes. The higher bidder then becomes the declarer. After the bidding has ended the declarer takes the four cards, which we have called the "widow," from the center of the table, and looking at them, puts one into his dummy rack, gives one to his opponent for his closed hand, one for his opponent's dummy rack and one for his own closed hand. The declarer does not show his opponent the two cards which he retains for himself and for his dummy. 

The two players now each have a complete hand of thirteen cards and a dummy hand of thirteen as well. 

The "Widow"

After the cards in the "widow" have been distributed the declarer now directs the adversary to lead either from his own hand or the dummy hand and he must lead from whichever one of the two hands the declarer designates. After the opening lead the hand at the left of the leading hand plays next, the adversary's hand which did not lead plays the third card and the fourth is played from the declarer's hand which has not played. This is the exact order of play which we follow in the four-handed game. 

Four cards constitute a trick and the play goes round the table "clockwise." The hand which wins a trick must lead to the next trick. Each player plays from his own dummy rack as well as

from his own closed hand, when it becomes his turn to play. A player can see his own dummy hand throughout the play, while both of the opponent's hands are concealed from him. 

In leading or playing either from a closed hand or from a dummy hand a card is quitted when the player's hand no longer touches it. After a card has been quitted it cannot be recalled; but until it has been quitted the player may withdraw it and substitute another without penalty. 

Tricks, honors, games, rubber premiums, penalties, etc., are scored as in regular Bridge, except that a revoke made by a dummy hand is penalized exactly as one made by the closed hand, and there is no penalty for cards "exposed" by any hand. 

The two players should play for rubbers and the score pads designed for the regular rubber game can be used in the twohanded game. In this two-handed game of bridge each player knows exactly what cards are against him, although he is, of course, unaware of the distribution. In bidding he is able to estimate fairly accurately the number of tricks his combined hands should win. He must not overlook the four cards in the "widow" which he will have the privilege of distributing if he becomes the declarer. The advantage of the "widow" should be worth two tricks in the play of the hand and a player is fully justified in adding two tricks to the number of probable tricks which a hand is worth. Not only may the player expect at least one taking card from the four in the "widow," but the fact that he can control two of his adversary's cards should give him the advantage of another trick. An honor which might be in the "widow" and which might prove troublesome if its location were unknown to the declarer can be safely tucked into whichever one of the adversary's hands will enable the declarer to finesse against it. Positive knowledge of two of the adversary's cards should be of value in the play of the hand.

DOUBLE DUMMY BRIDGE 

Contrary to the general belief straight Double Dummy is an excellent game for two people. Two players bent on improving their Bridge game can find no better way of practicing. It may not seem to be much fun as a game, for there are fewer thrills than in other two-handed Bridge games and no real suspense in the play.

The cards are dealt in four piles of thirteen cards each, exactly as in the regular Bridge game. The players can decide on the bidding to suit themselves. They can each bid before they have seen their dummy hands or they can bid each hand exactly as they would normally if they had seen just one hand. In any case, a player should not see the dummy hand of his opponent until after the bidding has ended. The hands can be bid as Auction or 

Contract. Then, after the opponent of the declarer leads to the first trick, the dummy hands are both placed face up on the table in plain sight of both players. Both players know where all the cards lie, and the practice of making a perfect picture of a closed hand and playing with that picture always in mind, is an invaluable lesson to those eager to learn how to handle the cards so that the greatest number of tricks can be wrung from them. In this way the entire situation is known to a player and he has the problem of how best to combat it. Finessing, ducking, holding up, overtaking, unblocking can all be experimented with and mastered by this perfectly simple method. In this game situations for end plays can be created and a player can familiarize himself with the various combinations until good sound play will become a habit.

CASSINO 

'N NEARLY all card-playing families the children are taught Cas sino and it is frequently the first card game a child attempts to play. The rudiments of Cassino are easily learned, as it mainly requires matching cards and combining numbers. This is, in reality, a lesson in arithmetic served in an attractive, interesting way. Thus addition is learned more rapidly, perhaps, than in the class room, and all the while the youngster is developing concentration and memory, he is enjoying himself to the utmost. 

Except as a stepping stone in a child's card education, Cassino seems to have gone out of fashion. Although we are told that there are today many groups of players who prefer the game to all others, we may not know, personally, a single adult player who devotes time to it. Cassino is, in fact, as old as the hills. Old prints of quaintly attired men and women seated at a table are often found in antique shops, entitled "Cassino."

As revivals are quite the fad, let us engage in a few games of Cassino just to see how much fun it really is. We will be almost sure to add it to our repertoire of games for two players. If we find the simple Cassino game somewhat tame we can adopt Royal Cassino, which is scientific enough to satisfy the experienced gamester.

Rules of the Game 

An entire pack of fifty-two cards is used. 

The two contestants draw for deal. The player drawing the lower card is the first dealer. The Ace is the lowest card. The four suits have no ranking value; hence if two cards of the same numerical value are drawn, the players must continue to draw until the cards are of different value. 

The non-dealer may shuffle the cards, but the dealer has the privilege of shuffling the cards last. The pack must be cut by the ΙΟ

non-dealer, who must cut more than four cards and leave more than four at the bottom of the pack.

The dealer now distributes one card to his opponent, one card face up in the center of the table, one card to himself. He continues thus until the two players and the table each has four cards. 

The remainder of the pack is left face down on the table. The non-dealer has the first turn to play.

The object of the game is to secure as many cards as possible by pairing and combining the cards in the hand with those exposed on the table. If the player has a card of the same numerical value as one on the table he can place his own card upon it and take them both. If there are two or more cards of the same denomination he may place them in a pile and, by adding his own card to them, he can take them all. If he can combine any two or more cards from the table so that they will equal the number of a card in his own hand, he can take them all. For example: Assuming that there are exposed on the table a seven, a four, a two and a one. 

A player holding a seven in his own hand can combine the four, two and one, which makes seven. He can pile with these three cards the seven which is also on the table, playing on the whole pile the seven from his own hand. Again: The table contains two fours, a three and a one. A four from the player's hand will take all four cards. The cards which he obtains in this fashion are placed face down in a pile in front of him. 

A player can also build up combinations to be taken later in the hand. For example: A player holds a ten and a two, and there is an eight on the table. The player can place the eight from his own hand on the two, announcing "ten." These cards are then left in a pile on the table until a later round when the player can place his ten on the other two cards, taking them all. 

A player is permitted to play but one card at a time from his own hand. If, in the above examples, the opponent holds a seven in the first case and a ten in the second, he can play the lucky card from his hand and capture the pile left by his opponent. 

An opponent can also build on a pile left by the first player.

Assume that a player has built himself a six, with a four and a two. If the opponent holds an eight and a two, he can place the two on the six pile announcing "eight" and this eight pile he can take on a later round.

A player must be sure that he holds a card of the denomination which he announces. He can, however, increase his own build, if he has the proper cards and the opportunity. For example: A player can place a two on a two, announcing four, and then on the next round he can place his four on the other two making the total eight, all of which he can take with his eight on a later round. 

A double build cannot be increased. For example: Suppose a player holds two sevens. On the table is a five and a two. He plays from his own hand one of his sevens on the five and two, announcing "two sevens." This pile cannot now be added to. Only a seven can capture it.

When a player has no play to make he places one of his cards face up on the table. A player is not obliged to make any play unless he desires to do so except when he has made a build. In case of a build he must play when his next turn comes. He is not, however, forced to take in the cards he has built. He can make any other play, such as forming other pairs, making another build, or taking in the opponent's build. 

After the first four cards in each player's hand have been played, the dealer distributes four more alternately to each player, one at a time, none being placed on the table. When the pack is finally exhausted the player who makes the last play takes to himself all the remaining cards on the table. 

At any time a player succeeds in taking every card exposed on the table, he is said to have made a "sweep" and he is rewarded by scoring a point. So that he will not forget the point it is customary to leave one of his cards face up. The winner of the last trick counts a sweep only if he successfully combine all the cards then on the table.

A player should always take up the card laid down by his opponent in preference to any other play.

When Big or Little Cassino has not been played, a player should be cautious about playing a ten or a two.

ROYAL CASSINO 

Although Cassino is an excellent game for the young or for the player who desires merely a pleasant pastime, Royal Cassino is played by the most experienced gamester, who delights in vying with his opponent in a keen, exhilarating mental combat, which taxes his ingenuity and memory to the utmost. This is all made possible owing to the fact that in Royal Cassino the cards run in order from one to fourteen, instead of from one to ten as in the regular Cassino. The face cards take on a numerical value and are not merely matched as in the simpler game. The Jack, following the Ten, is valued as `eleven, the Queen as twelve and the King thirteen. The Ace is both one and fourteen at the option of the holder although an Ace on the table must always be reckoned as one.

Bringing the face cards into action so that higher and more numerous combinations can be formed, greatly increases the scientific possibilities. Combine with Royal Cassino the twenty-one point game, with its "claiming out" feature, and we have a card game for two of the very first water.

The counting and scoring in Royal Cassino follows the regular game and all penalties are similarly enforced.

Players sometimes record their scores on a cribbage board and score the points as they are made. In this event the game is usually increased to sixty-one points, once up and down the board, then pegging home. The score for cards is all that remains to be scored after the hand is played. Strategy of Royal Cassino 

Expert Royal Cassino players are rare and they are reticent in divulging their methods of play.

It is noted, however, that they are exceedingly cautious in handling their point cards, that is, both Big and Little Cassino and the four Aces. They plot to test the opponent's holding by deliberately playing a card on the table as a sort of bait, with a certain definite combination in mind. When it is apparent that the coast is clear the point card can then be used to build the desired combination which it is most unlikely the opponent will capture. 

The successful Cassino player schools himself to keep a mental account of the cards as they are played. This is much more difficult than in Bridge, owing to the fact that they do not fall in suits but are played irregularly with little relation to each other. When the home stretch hovers in sight, the better player of the two contestants has a great advantage. On the next to the last deal only eight cards are left to distribute and only four concealed in the opponent's hand. The expert has a chance of judging the next round which is the "finals" and develop his end play accordingly. 

This is of immense value when there are point cards still unplayed or when the number of cards each player holds is nearly even. At such a time woe to the weaker opponent!

Making a build in error: If a player makes a build which he has not the card to take in, the build must be separated, and the opponent who has played after the false build can take back his cards and amend his play.

Cards once taken in cannot be examined, except the last combination won, nor points nor cards counted until all the cards have been played. A mistake cannot be corrected after another combination has been taken in.

CRIBBAGE 

RIBBAGE is generally conceded to be the best of the twohanded card games. It is primarily a game for two players as it was originated for that number and no more. It was not made over, or merely adapted from a game for four players, such as the various forms of Honeymoon Bridge.

The scoring of Cribbage is done on a board designed expressly for the game. The scoring board contains one hundred and twenty small holes, sixty on each side, and small pegs of a size to fit the holes are used for scoring purposes. There are at least four extra holes on every board. These holes are arranged at one end of the board and are used to hold the pegs before they are thrust into action.

There are many Cribbage boards on the market, many of them very elaborate and costly. The board found to be the most convenient for the home is, happily, the least expensive. It is a large one made of wood, unadorned except for a coating of yellow shellac, and has with it four large-sized pegs. To avoid confusion two of these pegs are usually red, and two white. They may all be of the same color, however, as the players' pegs are on opposite sides of the board and there is no danger of mixing them. 

This particular board is about sixteen inches in length from one end to the other, four and a half inches wide and a little less than an inch in thickness. 

Then there is the other extreme, the tiny board, perfectly made, which can be easily carried in a man's pocket, ready for a game wherever the owner may chance to find himself.

The Pegging or Scoring 

The Cribbage board should be placed horizontally between the two players a little to the right or left of the center of the table, and each should start at the same end pegging parallel down the outside edge and up the inside back to the starting place, or home. 

The four pegs, usually two of the same color for each player, are used for pegging. The first points made by each side are scored by taking one peg from a hole outside the board, which is called the starting point, and placing it in its proper hole. When the second count is made, the player does not use the first peg for scoring but leaves it in the hole, using the second peg. When a third score must be marked, the back peg is taken out and the points reckoned from the foremost peg, which must not be disturbed during the progress of the game. Thus the last count is clearly marked by the distance between the two pegs. By this means of scoring it is easy for the players to detect the amount pegged and easily check each other's scores. 

The players decide whether their game will be sixty-one or 121; that is, if they will go once up the board and return, or up and down twice. The latter score of 121 is the more popular and makes a more scientific game. In either case, to win the game the player must peg off the board into the extra hole from which he started. 

Merely completing the tour and arriving at the sixtieth or 120th hole is not sufficient. The moment a player pegs off the board the game is ended, whether or not the other player has finished his play or the hands have been computed.

THE STRATEGIES OF CRIBBAGE The Discard 

Perhaps the first point for us to consider is which two of the six cards dealt to us should be discarded into the crib. This is, plainly, one of the most important and scientific points of the game. It is obvious that the discard usually depends upon whether or not a player is to have the crib. If the crib is to belong to the opponent the discard should be two cards which are the least likely to be of advantage to him in the forming of pairs, runs, fifteens and so forth. The most unlikely cards to reckon for points in the crib, and consequently the generally accepted cards for that purpose, are Kings. A sequence must end with a King. It can never go beyond it. Hence the King has less chance of forming a sequence than any other card except an Ace. The Ace is considered to be the next best card to present to an opponent's crib, for this very reason. The Ace, however, is often an excellent playing card, frequently being the means of scoring exactly thirty-one. 

Furthermore, it is often an aid in forming fifteens when combined with sixes, sevens and eights or with fours and ten cards. 

The two cards which should be discarded into the opponent's crib should be as far apart in numerical value as it is possible to have them, so that a sequence of any length would be most unlikely. To discard a pair, with its possibilities of a double run, is obviously unwise.

Avoid discarding a Jack. A card turned of the same suit, as 

Starter, will give the opponent a point. 

On the other hand, when it is the turn of a player to discard into his own crib he should try in every way to "feather his own nest." The most advantageous cards are fives, sevens, eightswhen so held as to form sequences, pairs or flushes. The five is above all cards the most often useful as it will make fifteen with just one of any sixteen cards. A player should discard two cards. of the same suit whenever possible without otherwise interfering with the value of the hand.

The Score 

The score is, after all, the most important element in the game. 

When one player completes his round of the board, or his second round, as most players prefer, and has pegged off, the game is won. After that the most spectacular hand is of no avail. Hence the time element is the deciding factor in the discard and play, and all rules in the game are subordinate to this main principle. 

When well in the lead a player should discard cautiously, giving

CRIBBAGE 

29 to his opponent's crib the cards which are likely to be of the least value. On the other hand, when a player has fallen far behind he discards rashly, taking a long chance of obtaining a Starter which will give him a big hand. The value of a hand depends to a very great extent on the Starter. Therefore a player, before selecting his discard, must consider what his chances are of securing various numbers.

A word of advice to the beginner. Do not hastily make what at first seems the obvious discards. Consider every possible combination.

Surprising are the many revelations. In play never hasten to make a pair or a run without stopping to calculate how much the opponent may score in consequence. 

Although Cribbage is simple enough to be enjoyed by the very young, it has possibilities of strategy which recommend it to the most hardened and experienced gamester.

CONQUIAN 

NONQUIAN, a card game for two players, ranks with the very best. Although it is extremely popular in Mexico and in the southern United States bordering on it, the game seems to be little known outside that part of the world.

Conquian takes its name from the Spanish con quien, meaning "with whom." We sometimes hear it colloquially referred to as "Coon Can."

In many respects Conquian is very like the Chinese game of Mah Jong, played with cards instead of tiles, and adapted for only two players, and its principles are so old that they undoubtedly antedate our own better-known games. 

Conquian is played with the Spanish pack of forty cards instead of the fifty-two in our own pack. The eight, nine and ten of each suit are deleted, which leaves but ten cards in a suit, the Jack following the seven in sequence.

Many American players find it less confusing to remove the Jacks, Queens and Kings from the pack, leaving a straight sequence in each suit from the Ace, which is one, to the ten. Playing 

Conquian with a pack of fifty-two cards makes a very inferior game, as it enables a player to win easily on a sequence of one suit. 

The two players draw for seats and the player drawing the lower card has the choice and becomes the first dealer. Ace is the lowest card and the King in the Spanish pack the highest card. 

In the American game the ten is the highest card. 

The four suits have the same ranking value and when both players draw a card of the same denomination they must draw again until they obtain cards of different denomination. 

Each player should be supplied with ten or a dozen counters to be used in settling the account after each deal. 

The cards are shuffled by the non-dealer, called the pone, and 32

Object of the Game 

The object of the game is to form triplets, fours-of-a-kind and sequences by combining the cards dealt with those which are drawn from the stock. When the player has formed eleven cards in the above fashion and these eleven cards are laid on the table, he has won the game.

The sequences must be of the same suit and no sequence can be shorter than three cards. The Ace is used in the sequence of onetwo-three only. When the cards are dealt each player sorts his cards into sequences and triplets and considers carefully what cards are necessary to complete his hand. The non-dealer or pone now begins the play by drawing the top card from the stock on the table. This card he turns and places face up on the top of the stock. If he can use this card to form a sequence in the same suit of three or more cards, or to form triplets or fours-of-a-kind, he can do so if he chooses. If he is to use the card he removes it from the pack and places it near him on the table at his right. He then removes from his own hand the two or more cards which complete the combination, leaving them all face up on the table. 

These exposed cards still remain a part of his hand. 

If a player accepts any card from the stock in this fashion, he must discard from the concealed cards in his own hand, as it is obvious he would have too many cards. After each play a player must have in hand and exposed on table exactly ten cards, until the eleventh card will complete the hand and win the game.

When a player discards, the card selected is placed face up on the table beside the stock. This card is now at the disposal of the dealer who can use it if he has two or more cards which will give him one of the necessary combinations.

The game is won by the player who is first to expose eleven cards which will give him a complete hand. His hand may be composed of sequences, triplets and fours-of-a-kind. It is a complete hand if it contains only two or more sequences, or triplets and fours-of-a-kind. It does not need all three combinations. The cards exposed during play are a part of the eleven cards. The eleventh card must be drawn to complete the hand, for if a player is forced to discard he will have but ten cards remaining, hence he cannot hold a complete hand.

Each deal is a game in itself, and the player who wins receives one counter from his adversary.

When the last card is drawn from the stock and passed by both players, each player shows his concealed cards and if neither can combine his cards to make a complete hand it is tie. This is called a "tableau."

When there is a tableau each player plays a counter into a pool. 

The winner of the next game takes the pool in addition to another counter which the adversary must pay. Players continue to play one counter into the pool even if there are two or more tableaux in succession.

The deal passes alternately from one player to the other. 

Penalties of Conquian 

Under no conditions can a card once turned down be faced and examined. If a player violates this rule, his adversary can take up and examine the remainder of the stock, but the position of the cards in the stock must not be disturbed nor must a single card from the stock be shown.

If a player exposes any but the top card of the stock the adversary may look at all of them.

If a player draws out of turn his adversary may claim the card.

Suggestions for Play

A player must watch carefully the cards which are passed, making a mental picture of them as far as possible, as it will enable him to form an idea of his opponent's hand. He will, furthermore, know what cards to expect from the stock. If a player finds, toward the end of the stock, that he has little or no chance of getting eleven cards himself, he must make every effort to play for a tableau. This he does by using no cards from the stock which will compel him to discard cards which will enable the opponent to win.

A player cannot block his opponent and at the same time win the game himself, for so long as he refuses to play the card which he knows his adversary desires to win the game, he cannot complete his own hand. It is quite obvious that his only chance to win would be for himself to make use of the card which his opponent needs.

RUM 

R UM is an adaptation of Conquian and has probably been played more generally in recent years than any other card game for two players. It is not as scientific as Conquian, but many have found it entertaining. It is sometimes called "Rummy." 

Rum for two players is played with a full pack of fifty-two cards. The cards are spread and drawn for choice of seats and the first deal. The Ace is the lowest card and the King the highest. 

The suits have no ranking value, therefore if the players draw cards of the same denomination, they must draw until they obtain cards of different denomination.

The dealer's opponent shuffles and cuts the cards for the dealer. 

The dealer then distributes one card at a time alternately to each player until each has ten cards. The dealer then turns the top card of the thirty-two cards remaining in the pack. This card is placed face up near the center of the table. The remainder of the pack is then placed face down in a pile at the left of the exposed card. This pile is called the stock. 

The object of the game is to get rid of the ten cards dealt, together with those which are drawn in turn from the stock, by laying out on the table combinations of sequences, and triplets. The sequences must be of exactly three cards and must be in the same suit. The Ace is in sequence with the two and three (Ace-twothree) and with the King (Queen-King-Ace). The Ace cannot, however, make an "around the corner" sequence as (King-Acetwo). 

The non-dealer has the first play. He begins by deciding whether or not he wants the exposed card. He does not need to make a combination with the card. He may take it if it will benefit his hand, thereby making his prospects brighter. If he does not want the exposed card he takes the top card of the stock and, 38

RUM 

without exposing it, places it in his hand. If this card will form a sequence in the same suit, or three cards of the same denomination, he may declare the combination by placing the three cards face up on the table. These cards are now common property; they no longer belong to that player alone. This feature of the game is unlike Conquian. 

After drawing, the player must discard one card, whether or not he has declared any combination. The discard is placed face up on top of the first card turned and which has been refused, completely covering it. If the first card turned was chosen so that there is no card exposed on the table, the player discards his card face up in the spot where the first card was placed. 

The dealer then has the option of taking the exposed card left by the opponent or drawing the top card of the stock. The dealer cannot take the first card exposed even if the opponent has refused it. 

The players now go on in turn in that order: drawing, declaring, discarding until one player declares all the cards in his hand, except the one last card which he may discard after declaring all the rest of his hand.

A player may add one card to a sequence already exposed on the table, or he can add the fourth one of triplets. He may do thus, playing but one card at a time, and he may do so whether he himself or his opponent declared the combination. 

A player is never obliged to declare any combination. However, as he can declare but one combination in a turn, he must be cautious not to wait too long.

When a player gets rid of his last card, either by declaring it or discarding, he calls out and the game comes to an end. 

When a player has called out and declared the last combination in his hand it is too late for the opponent to declare anything. The opponent, however, shows the cards he has left in his hand, and the winner gets credit for the numerical value of these cards. The 

Ace is one, the Jacks each 11, the Queens each 12 and the Kings each 13.

R UMILY is a development of both Conquian and Rum in its varying forms. It borrows many points from both games, with many unique features of its own. 

Rumily is new. It is guaranteed to be the latest 1931 model and has all the modern improvements, being thoroughly up to date in every detail. It has only just been christened, and this is the first description of the game ever arranged for publication. Those who have been introduced to Rumily are enthusiastic in their praise of it, and it is already quite in vogue. It has several thrilling features which make it much more fun than any other form of Rum, and many go so far as to declare unhesitatingly that they prefer it to all other card games for two.

Slams 

A Grand Slam is made when a player is able to announce "Rumily" without having previously exposed a single combination; and the card enabling him to complete his hand is drawn from the stock.

A Small Slam is made when a player completes his hand by taking an exposed card, when he has not previously declared a single combination.

In both Grand and Small Slams the player is permitted to discard a losing card at the end. 

Suggestion for Play

The conservative player does not win many games of Rumily. 

Scores are made by declaring combinations, and the more cards passing through a player's hand the more combinations he is able to declare to increase his score. The thrill of the game comes from picking up many of the exposed cards and hurrying to declare as many combinations as possible before the opponent calls "Rumily." 

Of course, when a player can get rid of his hand quickly before an opponent has time to declare many of his cards, it is obviously unwise to incur the risk of trying for too large a score, by picking up discards.

When the opponent is far in the lead and the hand seems hopeless, picking up the exposed cards and trying for as many combinations as possible before the opponent can declare Rumily is good play. Early in the game, it is sound play to hold high cards, discarding the low ones as high-card combinations are much more

49 worth while, owing to their numerical value. Three twos, for example, only score 6 points, while three Jacks are 30 points.

Watch the opponent's hand carefully, keeping the number of cards he holds always in mind. His discards will give an idea of the cards which he does not want and, by elimination, we can make a pretty accurate picture of his hand and the cards which will help him.

The number of cards in the opponent's hand should be the player's guide at all times, as indicating the possibility of declaring enough combinations before the opponent calls Rumily, to make the risk worth while.

Suggestions for Play

The most important point in Russian Bank is to try to be rid of the stock pile as soon as possible. The Tableau should be arranged in such a way that spaces can be made and cards played from the stock. It is always better play to take cards from the stock rather than the hand as the stock may block the play and prevent the player's winning.

A King can never be gotten rid of except in a space. Hence it is very important to watch for a possible space for this purpose. 

Spaces enable one to form or move sequences. The number of spaces controls the number of cards that can be moved. Great ingenuity is needed to determine just what cards to give to the opponent's stock or discard pile. Cards that can be immediately returned to their places injure the opponent not at all, however; taking cards from the Tableau to give to the opponent will often produce valuable spaces.

There is probably no game which requires such perfect concentration as Russian Bank. A player must be ever alert for possible plays whether he or his opponent is in action. A moment's inattention may prove fatal, as a single slip may give the opponent the turn to play, thereby losing the game. On the other hand, a quick eye and mind may detect a flaw in the opponent's play, often resulting in a well-deserved game.

The Bézique family is one of the largest known to the card world, and it has one outstanding feature peculiar to itself-the Kings and Queens may enter into the bonds of holy matrimony, provided they can be properly, or, one might say, lawfully mated.

Problems in Play

A player is often obliged to make two declarations at the same time, although under the laws of the game, he is permitted to score but one combination at that time. For example: Assume that a player has announced four Kings and has scored the 80 points allowed him. One of these Kings is the King of spades. On the next turn he announces Bézique—that is, the Jack of diamonds and the Queen of spades. The Queen of spades also forms a marriage, or a Royal Marriage if a spade is the trump. As the marriage and Bézique belong to different classes, they may both be scored although a player, after announcing Bézique, must await his next turn to declare before he can score the points for the marriage. A player would always declare the more valuable combination first, or a Bézique before a plain marriage. It is customary when declaring two scoring combinations to say, as in case of Bézique and plain marriage, "Forty for Bézique and twenty to score." He would continue to repeat at every trick lost, "Still twenty to score."

Such a score does not obligate the player to score it the next time he wins a trick, if he succeeds in securing a combination which will give him a higher score. In this event he could score the higher amount and continue to carry the lower until such time as he would have nothing better to announce.

It may be said to appeal to men rather than to women, and many experienced gamesters prefer it to all other games. The player feels that he has more opportunity to exercise his own mental powers and outwit the enemy than in any other card game for two players. Pinochle will be found in the old textbooks spelled in many ways. Binocle, penuckle, penuchle, penucke and penuckel are given by different writers on the game. Suggestions for Play

It is disadvantageous to lead unless a player has something to declare, or there is a ten (called a brisque) in the trick. Tens are of no value except in the trump suit, as they do not enter in other combinations.

The nines should be gotten rid of early in the hand so that they cannot be easily won by the adversary later. 

The lead is often advantageous toward the end of the hand, to prevent the adversary from declaring. 

It is obviously better to lead an Ace than a King or Queen, as there is less chance of winning 100 Aces than marrying the King or Queen. The Queen of spades should be safeguarded, as it can be used in three different combinations, namely, marriage, pinochle and four queens. If trumps, it may also form a sequence, as well as royal marriage.

The cards must be carefully matched so that a player can form an idea of the most likely cards which he will draw. 

When holding duplicate trump cards, it is better to play the one on the table than the one in the hand.

PIQUET PIQUET 

IQUET was originated in France during the reign of Charles the Seventh, and was mechanically designed for a ballet of living cards which was presented at the Palace of Chinon. Since that time it has never ceased to occupy a high position in the card world, being played especially by the upper classes. 

In our own time Piquet has had considerable vogue in the smart gentlemen's clubs in our larger cities, particularly in New York and Philadelphia. It must be confessed, however, that the season of 1931 finds it temporarily, at least, supplanted by Backgammon. 

This should not alarm the lovers of Piquet, who may rest assured that so standard a game will undoubtedly weather the present tidal wave. 

Piquet is a card game expressly designed for two players. Two packs of cards of different pattern and color should be used from which all the cards below the seven have been deleted. This will leave two packs each of thirty-two cards. These rank as in Whist: 

Ace, King, Queen, Jack, ten, nine, eight and seven.

The two players each draw a card from one of the packs spread face down on the table. The player drawing the lower card becomes the first dealer. Ace is low in drawing. In the event that the players draw cards of the same numerical value, it is a tie and they must draw until they obtain cards of different values. 

The player drawing the higher card is called the pone. He shuffles and cuts the cards for the dealer although the dealer always has the privilege of shuffling after the pone and before the cut. While the pone is shuffling the cards to be used for the first deal, the dealer shuffles the cards to be used by his adversary for the second deal. After this, each player shuffles his own cards, always presenting them to the pone to be cut.

Repic is scored when either player amasses 30 points by successive declaration before the play of the hand commences. For this the player adds 60 to his total, making it 90 instead of 30. 

The important factor in repic is to remember the proper order of the declarations. Equalities do not save repic.

The Discard 

Discarding advantageously is the first problem which confronts us. In discarding, there are two important principles to bear in mind. First, to secure as high a count as possible and, secondly, to arrange the hand so that it will play safely. 

The discard varies according to the player's position, that is, whether he be the dealer or the pone. As the pone is the first to speak we will consider him before the dealer.

The Pone or the Leader Hand 

Before discarding the player should consider carefully the cards which must be in the dealer's hand. If he holds a sufficient number of high cards so that pic or repic would be impossible against him, he should try for the point, that is, to procure as long and strong a suit as possible. This often results in the sequence score as well. 

Instead of each suit containing thirteen cards, which number we have in Bridge, there are but eight cards in each suit, and the player must bear in mind this fact in choosing discards. 

The pone should realize always the importance of playing for "cards," that is, for winning at least seven of the twelve possible tricks. In the long run the player who tries for the card score will find himself well in the lead. For the card count, Aces are, of course, the most valuable as they enable a player to gain the lead to further his own suit. On the other hand, when the pone sees a fair chance for a pic, that is, scoring 30 in the hand and play combined before the adversary scores anything, or repic, which is scoring 30 in successive declarations before the adversary scores at all, he should seize it at once, as such a hand will usually finish the game altogether. The pone does not need to guard against the adversary leading a long suit, as he himself has the lead, and he is permitted to continue to lead as long as he can win tricks. He may, then, unguard Kings and Queens; in fact, discard the cards

81 of a suit altogether, retaining only those cards with which he may retain the lead. A hand composed entirely of two suits is most favorable for repic, for if Kings and Queens of the other two suits have been discarded there is little or no chance of the opponent equaling the hand. 

The Dealer 

The dealer, on the other hand, is more in the position of the defense. He must guard against the possibility of pic or repic or even capot (which is the winning of all the tricks). The dealer must keep his Kings guarded with one card and his Queens with two cards. Thus the dealer has less chance to try for pic and repic, as he is in constant fear of the adversary making a long run and scoring heavily before he can get a chance to win a trick. 

He should, in fact, pay heed to all four suits, realizing the damage which a long suit in the pone's hand could do to him. In discarding it is often important to guard against the embarrassment which comes from being forced to lead away from tenace combinations. 

For this purpose it is often necessary to keep what we call in 

Bridge, "a card of exit." That is, some card that is sure to put the adversary into the lead so that he will be forced to lead up to the tenace combination. Leading

The pone, in his opening lead, usually begins with the point, that is, the longest suit. However, in case he is leading to a tenace combination in the dealer's hand, it is sometimes wise to lead some other suit, for the saving of one trick will often prevent the adversary's score for cards, or even pic. 

With the odd trick in hand, it is always good play to take it at once, since the score for cards is well worth securing. There is little to be gained by trying to win an extra trick or two, since there is only one point given for each card led, provided it is above a nine, and certainly does not warrant taking the risk of losing the ten points won for cards.

Sinking

A player is never obliged to declare the whole value of a combination unless he desires to do so, since there may be an advantage in concealing the hand's real strength. This is called "sinking." 

A player may announce fifty for his point when it is in fact sixty, or he may announce a sequence of five when it is seven, or a trio when he holds a quatorze. Sinking is seldom resorted to, unless the player is convinced that the adversary cannot hold a hand as strong as his own, and a player should use it with discretion, for that part of the combinations which are sunk are lost for scoring.

The Play 

The play proceeds similarly to the no-trump declaration at Bridge. The high cards win the tricks and the leading of a long suit will often force the adversary to unguard other suits. In

NE of the first games we are taught in our early youth is High-Low-Jack. This is the same game as All Fours, which it is called by the writers of the old game books. One of these writers tells us that in 1754 a pamphlet was published which contained the following heading: "Serious Reflections on the dangerous tendency of the common practice of card-playing, espe cially the game of All Fours." 

High-Low-Jack, or All Fours, was widely played in the early American days, being probably the most popular game for some years. 

Today it is played by many of the younger players and is known among them as "Seven Up." The same game is also popular in the South, where it is called "Old Sledge." The High-Low- 

Jack family includes many games. Among them are California 

Jack, Shasta Sam Pitch, Auction Pitch, Smudge, Pedro and Cinch.

High-Low-Jack is played with a full pack of fifty-two cards, ranking as in Whist: Ace, King, Queen, Jack, ten, nine, and so forth. The two players draw both for positions at the table and to determine which one shall be the first dealer. The player drawing the higher card wins. The Ace is high. The players draw again in case of a tie.

The non-dealer, called the pone, now shuffles the cards. The dealer always has the privilege of giving the cards a final shuffle. 

The pone cuts the cards towards the dealer. He must, as in the laws in Bridge, take off at least four and leave at least four in the pack. The dealer, then completing the cut, distributes the three top cards to the pone, then three to himself, then three more to each, so that each player holds six cards. The next card on the top of the pack the dealer turns face up for the trump and leaves 85

it face up on top of the pack or stock. If this card is a Jack the dealer scores one point for it immediately.

After the cards are dealt the pone looks at his cards, the dealer still leaving his cards face down on the table. If the pone is satisfied with the trump turned he says, "I stand," the dealer then picking up his cards, and the play begins at once. If the pone is not satisfied, he says, "I beg." In the latter event the dealer has the choice of giving the pone one point and playing the hand as dealt, or of dealing three more cards to pone and three to himself. The first trump card turned is discarded face up on the table and he then turns the top card of the pack for a new trump. This is called "running the cards." If the dealer decides to give the point he says, "Take it," and the pone scores one point for the gift. 

If the new trump turned is a Jack of another suit the dealer scores one point for it immediately. If a Jack of the first suit is turned no point is scored for it as that suit is decidedly not the trump suit. The Jack thus turned is laid aside and another card turned. When any card of the same suit as the first turned card is turned again, that card is laid aside and three more cards are given to each player. This continues until the dealer turns a card of a different suit. If no card of a different suit is turned before the pack is exhausted, all the cards are thrown down and the same player shuffles and deals again.

When the cards have been "run" and a trump finally determined, it is customary to discard all but six cards, thereby clearing the hand of all superfluous ones. Some players prefer to keep all the cards if but nine have been run, but to discard down to six if more than nine are run.

California Jack

California Jack is the variation of High-Low-Jack which is by far the most popular with many players. In California Jack the draw, shuffle, cut and deal are the same as in the regular game, and the dealer turns the remainder of the pack face up on the center of the table a little to his left. The top card indicates the suit which is to be the trump. Each player picks up the six cards which have been dealt to him, and there is no begging or exchanging.

The opponent of the dealer leads to the first trick, and the winner of that trick takes the top card from the pack and the dealer takes the next card. The winner of each trick leads to the next trick and the winner of each trick takes the top card which is exposed, the opponent taking the card just underneath the top one. Thus each player's cards will be continually restored to six. 

When the stock is exhausted the last six cards are played as in High-Low-Jack.

The game is seven points and these points are scored as in the regular game, except that low may be caught and the player, in order to score the point for low, must have it secure in his own tricks. The two spot is always low, and the Ace high, as all the cards in the pack are in use. The points for game are computed as in the regular game, but inasmuch as all of the cards are included in the hand, these honors will total 80. Therefore a player holding 41 or more will win the point for game. There is then no necessity for both players to count their cards except as a means of checking. The score of California Jack is often kept on a cribbage board, and the players may decide on any number of points for the game.

If, however, there is a single man of the opponent's left anywhere in the winner's home table, it is a "Backgammon" and he scores a triple game.

In the modern game played in the New York clubs and by Society generally, a different method of scoring is sometimes used. 

The winner scores a point for each one of the opponent's men left in the opponent's Home table; two points for each man left in the opponent's Outer table; three points for each one remaining in the winner's Outer table, and four points for each one remaining in the winner's Home table. The winner also scores four points for each man left upon the Bar.

The Doubles 

The above method of scoring is not so much used since the very great popularity of the doubles in Backgammon.

BACKGAMMON Optional Doubles 

At any time during the game either player may double. This double must be announced just before he himself throws the dice. 

The opponent is forced to accept or decline the double. If it is declined the game automatically ceases and the score is made up as the game stands at that moment, the player who declines the double being the loser.

If, however, the opponent accepts the double the game goes on, and the player who doubled throws the dice and plays. When it is the opponent's turn to throw again, this player may double and the first doubler must accept or decline. Thus the doubles may go on indefinitely throughout the game. It is considered a pretty act of courtesy when doubling, for a player to say, "I offer you a double," and the opponent replies, "I accept your offer,” or, “I decline your offer."

So that the players will not lose count of the number of doubles throughout the game, the modern Backgammonists use twoinch cubes which indicate these doubles. The six sides of the cube are marked with numbers-2-4-8-16-32 and 64. The first player to make an optional double turns the cube so that the 2 is uppermost and pushes it across the table in front of the opponent. 

When the opponent makes a further double, he turns the 4 uppermost and pushes the cube back to the first double and so on. 

As six doubles are usually the limit in one game, the cube is considered adequate. The cube is placed at 64 until a player doubles. Automatic Doubles When the two players are each throwing a single die to determine the first move, and they throw the same number, it constitutes an Automatic Double; and the amount of the game is automatically doubled. When the number of checkers, which the loser has left on the board at the end of a game, is counted for the score, each checker is worth two points instead of one. In case

We now offer a few hints to guide the player in his race to his home table, as Backgammon is primarily a race for home and the player proceeds to do this in two ways. First, to hasten with his men as fast as ever he can, and secondly, to retard his opponent's men from progressing. This latter feat is accomplished by hitting a blot made by the opponent, thereby sending back the enemy's man to begin his journey anew, and by endeavoring to occupy advantageous positions which will prevent the opponent from gaining ground.

The enemy has made his blot by leaving a man alone, without another to guard him, thus exposing his single man. It is not obligatory, by any means, for a player to “hit” a man merely because he is exposed. Such a play often results in exposing a player's own man, hence a player should consider carefully before deciding to make the hit. It is considered advantageous to hit a blot when the adversary's man has progressed further than the one thus exposed. The man so exposed may not be hit and even if it is hit in the very next play, the move has merit inasmuch as the adversary's progress has been retarded more than the first player's.

The Laws of Backgammon : 

1. If the men are wrongly set up, the mistake may be remedied if the player in error has not moved a man, otherwise they must stand as set up. 

2. If a player begins with less than the proper number of men, the error cannot be rectified after the player has made a throw for his move. 

3. The players must each cast a single die for the privilege of first move, the higher winning. Ties throw again. 

4. By mutual consent it may be agreed to let the higher throw play the points on his own and his adversary's die for the first move; otherwise he must throw again with two dice. 

5. Each player must throw the dice into the table on his right

105 hand, and if either die jumps into the other table, or off the board, both dice must be taken up and thrown again. 

6. To constitute a fair throw, each die must rest flat upon the board, and if either die is "cocked" against the other, or against the edge of the board or of a man, both dice must be taken up and thrown again. 

7. If a player moves a man a wrong number of points, the adversary must demand that the error be rectified before he throws himself, or the erroneous move stands good. 

8. A man is considered played when the figures have left him. 

9. The dice must remain on the table as they are thrown until the player has made his moves.

Played on a Board 

Camelot is a game of skill. Like checkers or draughts, as the 

English call them, and the immortal game of chess, Camelot is played on a board with pieces of men which are moved and controlled by the player. Again, as in checkers and chess, chance is eliminated entirely, except the possible advantage which the opening move may give to a player. In this respect the game differs radically from the dice or card games. The better player will usually win and the keenest satisfaction comes from success under such conditions.

Owing to the outward similarity, it is but natural to compare the new game with chess and checkers. A leading feature of chess is capture by replacement upon the space from which the enemy is taken, while in checkers the enemy's pieces are jumped and captured. It might be said that Camelot resembles the latter game more than the former, and more, too, than any other game. 

Like Armies on a Battlefield

Camelot presents the unique feature of personal encounter on the battlefield. The opposing forces meet like small medieval armies grouped near the center of the field, so close, in fact, they could "see the whites of each other's eyes."

The arrangement resembles the football formation more than any other game. The field or board of Camelot contains one hundred and sixty squares. Each square is about one inch in size, somewhat smaller than those on the regulation checker board. 

The squares are alternately tinted in two colors. Four of them, two at each end, are matched by stars and are known as fortresses or goals. The other one hundred and fifty-six squares are numbered in order. The numbering is merely for the purpose of recording plays or designating positions in the progress of the game. All of these squares are available for play, and the board

CAMELOT 

is placed on the table so that the starred squares are directly in front of the two players who are to sit opposite each other. This board represents the battlefield on which the two armies are to meet and combat. 

Knights and Men

There are two types of pieces, knights and men, comprising a force of fourteen pieces for each of the two players, or twentyeight in all. The men are of two different colors. One player's pieces are red and the other's yellow. The knights are easily distinguished from the men, as the former are somewhat larger with black tips.

The Start of the Game 

To begin the game the players toss a coin to determine which one shall have the choice of seats and color of men, as well as the first move or play. The pieces are then arranged on the squares as indicated by small white circles on the board. There is a small K within the circle to indicate the square on which the knight takes his stand. When the formation is completed the forces are in double ranks and a knight is stationed at the end of each line of men. 

The illustration shows the correct formation before the contest begins. The yellow forces are now massed against the red awaiting orders to begin the attack.

The Object of the Game 

The object of the game is for a player to succeed in driving two of his pieces away from him into the two starred squares at the farther end of the board, that is, into the squares which are directly in front of the opponent. These two pieces may be two knights, or two men, or a knight and a man. These starred squarts represent the enemy's fortress or castle which are the goals the forces are striving to reach.

To accomplish this the deadly battle begins and the brave soldiers push forward moving adroitly, ruthlessly destroying the enemy's flanks, capturing and holding the defeated victims.

The Moves 

There are four different types of moves and these are easily learned. They are: The plain move; the jump; the canter, and the 

Knight's charge.

The Plain Move. In the plain move, any piece, either knight or man, may be moved one square in any direction, forward or backward, sideways or diagonally to any adjacent unoccupied square of either color. In this respect the move differs from the familiar checker move, where a player must move to a square of the same color. In Camelot, however, the move resembles the King's move in chess.

The Jump. A player may jump his opponent's piece provided there is just beyond an unoccupied square on which the piece can land. When an enemy's piece is jumped that piece is removed as in checkers. Again, like checkers, a player can jump any number of men in one turn or move and a player is compelled to jump if able to do so, although in Camelot more than one opportunity often presents itself, in which event, a player may jump whichever way he chooses. In Camelot, however, the player has even more freedom in jumping than the king in checkers, since he is permitted to jump in any direction, sideways, forwards, backwards, or diagonally. He can also make as many jumps in one play as he is able to, and when jumping more than one piece in a move the direction of the jump may be varied after every leap. 

It will now be clear why the squares on the board alternate in color. The two colors have a definite purpose-namely, to facilitale the movement of the men. 

The Canter. Then there is the canter, or overpass, which is a leap over a friendly piece. Any piece, a knight or a man, may leap in any direction, forward, backward, sideways or diagonally, over any one of his own pieces which happens to occupy an adjacent square, provided there is an unoccupied square immediately be-

IIO CAMELOT 

yond it in any direct line. A man may canter over a knight or a knight over a man. In the move, jump and canter a man and a knight have equal rights. In the canter the piece which is passed over is not removed. A player can canter over any number of friendly pieces in a single turn or move. A player is not compelled to canter, as he is compelled to jump, the canter being entirely optional. The object is to arrive at a desirable point which will threaten the enemy.

In cantering and jumping the alternating tints of the squares are of the greatest assistance to the player. It is impossible for a player to jump or canter from a square of one tint to a square of a different tint. It must always be from one color to the same color. The only utility of the two colors is to guide the player to jump and canter accurately. Carelessly landing on a wrong color never occurs except in a beginner's game.

Knight's Charge. There is but one distinction between the privileges of a knight and a man. A knight can make what is expressively called "A Knight's Charge," that is, he is permitted to canter and jump both in one move. He can canter over one of his own men, and if an enemy piece is on an adjacent square he can continue on jumping the enemy's piece and removing it from the board. He can continue, in the same play, jumping as many opposing pieces as he is able. A knight may canter before a jump and he can make as many canters as he needs before he jumps, he can jump as many pieces as he is able, but after he has finished his jump or jumps, he cannot continue to canter in that move. In other words, he can canter before a jump, but can never canter after a jump.

A knight is never compelled to make a charge merely because he is able to do so, but when he has made a canter so that he is next to an enemy's piece he is obliged to continue and capture it, unless he can by a different route capture another piece. The knight's charge, both the canter and jump, can be made in any direction. Opportunities in planning for a knight's charge give a chance for brilliant play, perhaps unequaled in any modern game.

The knight's charge might be called the "pièce de résistance" of the game, for as he comes snorting down through the ranks he lays waste and causes damage impossible to repair. 

The two players take their turns alternately as in checkers or chess, and by means of the above-mentioned moves, the player who first succeeds in forcing two of his pieces (two knights or two men, or one of each) into the enemy's fortress, which is plainly designated by the two starred squares, wins the game.

At the beginning of a new game, the other player from the one who made the first move, has the opening play.

The players alternate in this way each taking his turn in opening throughout the play.

When an enemy's piece is jumped that piece should be removed immediately even when the player is able to make a further jump. 

If this practice is not carefully followed, some confusion may arise over which piece was the one jumped.

A player is allowed a reasonable length of time to decide on his move, and when the finger has left the piece, that piece is considered played and cannot be taken back. It is often wise, at least for the beginner, to move the piece to the square which he is considering and keep his finger on the top until he convinces himself his move is a wise one. Most chess players have formed this habit.

A player is not permitted to move into his own fortress or starred square. This would be a rather too obvious means of keeping away the enemy. A player can, however, if the occasion arises, jump an enemy piece, landing on his own starred square. He can do this even if he has made a knight's charge to perform the feat. 

When this play occurs one is obliged to move away from the starred square on his very next play. This removing one's own piece from one's own goal takes precedence over all other plays, even an opportunity to jump an enemy's piece.

The simplicity of play must have appealed to the novice in the game, but let him not rest under the delusion that the game has not infinite possibilities for the thorough exercising of his mental powers. New mysteries and delights will be revealed in the repeated games that follow, and with the development of better play will come the thrill which only a game of skill can produce. "Opening Fire"

The two armies lined up so near each other can "open fire" in numerous ways. The usual opening is the forward play, either by canter or plain move in order to afford a better positioning of a Knight in the next move, or with a view to "filling in," with other canters or plain moves, for a reorganization of the line, a strengthening of force upon one or the other flanks, or as a part of a campaign towards a drive at either wing or center of the opponent's army. The capture of an enemy's piece does not ordinarily occur until each side has utilized several moves in positioning for attack or strengthening defense. Positioning is accomplished by a plain move to an adjacent vacant space, or by a canter over a friendly piece or pieces one at a time in order to reach a certain space, which may afford better advantage of location or aid in "filling in." The point of attack selected for this purpose is usually the weaker of the opponent's wings in case one of the wings is rendered less strong than another by the opponent's moves. Such an attack invariably includes either one or two of the player's Knights, either entirely distinct from a wing reserved in solid formation for resistance although for the moment remaining inactive, or with some connection with the inactive wing, permitting cantering support, or the possibilities of a Knight's charge through the lines. 

One illusion which often possesses a player in his first few games is the thought that by gaining the edge of the board he may creep down the side lines towards his opponent's fortress and accomplish the winning of the game by such a simple, obvious, uninteresting method. Such tactics never succeed against a capable opponent unless a considerable portion of the enemy has been captured, or so separated as a result of previous attack that the way is open and clear. The reason that such a method of reaching the goal is not practical is that one or more of the pieces creeping along the edge is susceptible to a compelled jump (by the sacrifice of an enemy piece) into a position where either a plain jump or a Knight's charge will capture it. Furthermore, the process of positioning and creeping down the sides requires so many moves that, in the interim, the opponent has ample time for an undisturbed program of attack, thereby enabling him to damage the main body of one's forces beyond repair. Outflanking the enemy can rarely be accomplished save by a definite attack and at least partial annihilation of the enemy's wing.

Two things in the course of a game should be carefully watched : the point which an enemy's Knights may reach, and the possibility (often by a sacrifice of one or more pieces) of the opportunity to break in with a counter attack which will do severe damage. In fact, before making any move throughout the game, a good player will carefully observe every point which may be

reached by an opposing Knight, in order that he may, if possible, avoid incurring risk from an enemy's Knight's charge.

No beginner can expect, in his first few games, to form more than the briefest conception of the great possibilities for success or hazard which may be incurred. Beginners, in their first few games, are sure to leave numerous "holes" or vacant places, which if once reached by the enemy will permit capturing of several pieces. As the player becomes more expert, he leaves such holes with great discrimination.

Broadly speaking, the game becomes a campaign of various minor engagements, in which a player's definite program is varied and changed by his own strategy, influenced always by the type of attack of his opponent.

DOMINOES 

T IS claimed that dominoes were originated in Italy early in the eighteenth century, and spread from there to France, England and other countries. They have retained their early popularity and are played today in nearly every country in the world. Around 

Cairo the traveler often sees the natives engaged in an earnest game, while America has many loyal devotees.

Dominoes are pieces of ivory or bone about one and a half inches by three-quarters of an inch and three-eighths of an inch in thickness. On the face of each there are two compartments, arranged with dots or a blank in each compartment. The standard set of dominoes contains twenty-eight pieces and terminates at double sixes. Some sets carry the dots to double nines and others to double twelves.

To begin the play for all games, the dominoes are placed face down on the table. The two contestants sit opposite each other at the table and both players shuffle the pieces about, mixing them as thoroughly as possible.

Each player then draws a domino from those which are face down on the table, turning it immediately so that the other player can see it. Some players decide to let the higher domino determine which player shall set the first domino in the line, and others prefer the lower domino for this purpose. The former is somewhat more popular. In this case the higher double would precede all other combinations. That is, the double six would take precedence over all other dominoes, and a double blank would win over the 6-5 domino.

When the leader has been determined by the draw, the dominoes are again shuffled and each player draws seven dominoes. These dominoes are placed in front of the players and are made to stand on their edges with the faces toward the player who drew them and carefully concealed from the opponent. While some players are agile enough to hold six dominoes in the palm of one hand to obviate the possibility of their tipping over, most players find this feat both difficult and awkward.

The dominoes which remain after the players have drawn are pushed at one side, still face down on the table. These are called the stock or boneyard.

All games of Dominoes, except Matadore, are based on the principle of following suit or matching. We shall consider, first of all, the Block Game.

The Block Game 

The player who has the first play lays down on the center of the table any one of the seven dominoes which he holds. His opponent can now add to this domino, provided he can play one of the same suit. The doubles are played with the ends toward the players, while the other dominoes are placed in the other direction.

The object of the game is to block the adversary so that he is unable to play. The two players proceed to lead in turn, provided

DOMINOES 

they are able to play one of the suits which is at either end of the line.

When a player finds that he has no piece which will match either open end he must say "Go," and the other player goes on with the play. The first player continues to play as soon as he is able. 

When neither can play the set is blocked. Both players then expose the dominoes left in their hands, and they count the number of dots on the dominoes each player holds. The lower score is deducted from the higher, and the difference between the two scores is awarded to the player having the lower score. The game is usually placed at one hundred points, although fifty points is sometimes preferred and agreed upon. When both players have the same number of dots on the unplayed dominoes left in hand it is a tie and neither scores. If a player is able to play all of his seven dominoes, while his opponent still has some left unplayed, he announces "domino," and scores the total number of dots on the opponent's dominoes.

The player having the opening set should begin with a domino of his longest suit and work to get the line back to this suit as often as possible. It is not always wise to try to block an opponent so early in the game that a player succeeds in blocking himself. 

Large scores cannot be made by such tactics, unless a player's hand contains unusually low dominoes. 

The Draw Game 

The Draw Game is a variation of the Block Game in which either player has the option of drawing any number of dominoes from the stock or boneyard, except the last two dominoes, which can never be drawn. A player is permitted to draw when able to play on the line or not, at his option, but when he is unable to play he is obliged to draw until he can play, or until all but the last two dominoes are gone. The Draw Game is the most common game of all the varieties of dominoes, and usually the one which we first teach to the beginner.

Matadore Game 

In the Matadore Game, the two players each draw seven dominoes. The player having the highest double sets. In the event that neither player has a doublet, the highest domino sets, beginning with the 6-5, and following the same order of precedence used in drawing for the Block Game.

The object of Matadore is not to follow suit at the ends, but to play a number which will make the end number and the number played equal seven. If the end is a six, an Ace must be played, a two must be played to a five and a three to a four. 

It will be seen that there is no piece capable of making seven with a blank. Hence, to obviate this difficulty there are four trumps or Matadores. These are the double blank and the dominoes which have seven dots on their faces, the 1-6, 2-5, 3-4. The four Matadores can be played anywhere at any time it is the holder's turn to play, but the following player, if he does not also play a trump, must play the complement of seven to whichever end of the Matadore is left exposed. If he cannot play either of these he goes to the boneyard and draws until he can get a seven or a trump. The doublets are not placed crosswise as in the Block Game, and each end is counted singly. A double three cannot be played to an Ace, as the double counts as three, not six. When a trump is played, the player can leave exposed whichever end he chooses, so that the opponent will be obliged to play another trump or make a seven by playing the complement of the exposed end. There should be three dominoes left undrawn in the boneyard, to prevent each player from knowing his opponent's hand.

The player who first rids himself of all his pieces calls "domino," and scores as in the Block Game. If domino is not made before the drawing is ended, and a player cannot play in turn, he must pass and await his next turn to play, but he must always play if he can. Failure to play when able to do so deprives a player of any count which he might make with the hand. 

Blanks are very valuable in the game, the double blank being

the most valuable of all the Matadores. It is impossible to make sevens with a blank, and a player holding blanks can usually succeed in blocking his opponent's game altogether. 

It is unwise to play a blank as the first lead unless a player has a corresponding blank or a Matadore.

The double blank should be held back until the opponent has played all his blanks.

MUGGINS OR ALL FIVES 

At present the most popular of all games played with dominoes is "Muggins," or, as it is sometimes called, "All Fives." This form of dominoes might be called the European game, as it is the standard game played in the Continental cafés, while the contestants sip their native wines.

It is also the variation which is so frequently enjoyed on shipboard.

It might be said that "Muggins" as well as other games played with dominoes appeals particularly to the masculine mind. 

For the game of Muggins the standard set of twenty-eight pieces is used, and the two players seat themselves opposite each other at the table. The dominoes are placed face down on the table, both players shuffling them thoroughly. Each player then draws a domino and turns it face up. This is to determine which one of

DOMINOES 

131 the two players is to be the leader. The one drawing the higher domino wins the draw. Doublets do not take precedence over other pieces. The 3-2 domino would win over the 2-2. In case of a tie in the number of dots on the dominoes, when, for instance, one draws the 6-2 and the other the 5-3, the players must draw again, and continue until they obtain pieces with a different number of dots. 

Players usually have, on the table, some sort of indicator which will designate who is the leader in each hand. This can be a coin, knife, key or any small convenient object. As soon as the leader is decided upon by the draw, this indicator is placed on the table near that player and is moved across the table after each hand, just before the shuffle. After the pieces drawn have been returned, face down, to the others, the opponent reshuffles all the dominoes. 

When this is done the leader then draws seven bones and brings them over in front of him. As a pretty little act of courtesy the player who shuffles never draws a single piece until his opponent has taken all of his. This gives the opponent the whole twentyeight from which to choose and assures him, furthermore, of an honest shuffle.

The shuffler then draws his seven bones and both players stand them on their edges in front of them. They arrange them in suits exactly as in the card games.

The leader now must place the first domino on the table. For this purpose he does not need a doublet. He can lead any one he chooses. If it is doublet, he places it on the center of the table with the ends towards the players; if it is not a doublet he places it cross-wise with the sides towards the players. Suggestion for Play 

Except that the players score the multiples of five as they are made throughout the game, the principles of Muggins are the same as the Block Game. That is, the player's object is to rid himself of his pieces and block his opponent so that the opponent will be left with dominoes which he is unable to play.

To accomplish this result the player leads first from his longest suit so that he can have a good chance of disposing of the entire suit. It is also wise to play, as a spinner, the doublet of a player's longest suit. In this way he can keep the suit pieces in reserve and play on the ends whenever he chooses. When one player has shown his suit by his lead or play, his opponent should try to take the lead away from him by playing on that suit and bringing it back to his own suit. Players must always keep in mind the fact that there are seven pieces in each suit. By elimination the player can form an opinion of what pieces are in the opponent's hand and in the stock. 

A player should not lead the 6-6 or the 6-3 unless he holds both these dominoes. Giving the opponent the opportunity to play the 

6-3 to the 6-6, or similarly the 6-6 to the 6-3, will give him fifteen points. The only excuse for leading one of these pieces, when not holding the other, would be when also holding at least five pieces of the six or three suit. The 4-4 and 4-2 domino should be treated in the same way. 

Leading the 6-4 domino will give the player ten points. As he can never win any more with the piece he may as well lead it unless he has another suit which is very long. 

The 3-2 domino is a safe domino to lead, as it can never be scored on.

The 5-5 domino, as it counts immediately ten points, is a tempting domino to lead. However, this is not wise unless he holds the 

5-0, as this piece would give the opponent ten points. The 5-5 should be led only when strong in the five suit. In this event the 

5-5 domino is the best to lead as it establishes the piece as a spinner.

The five suit should be handled with great care as it gives many opportunities for the opponent to score.

A player must not hasten to play a domino just because it will enable him to score. Careful consideration should be given to such a move to assure the player that the opponent is unlikely to make a still larger score. Refusing to score under such conditions is called a sacrifice, and the player who knows the exact moment to make the sacrifice, qualifies as an expert.

ANAGRAMS 

NE of the most interesting and profitable games for two players is the game of Anagrams or Letters. Because it is played with lettered wooden blocks one must not assume that it is merely a game for children. On the contrary, it is distinctly a "high-brow" game and many consider it superior to all others.

Naturally, being a word game, it has especial appeal to the literati. 

The larger a player's vocabulary the better his chances of success; and, like the cross-word puzzle, the game of Anagrams is also a very definite aid for enlarging the vocabulary. 

Strictly speaking, an anagram is a word formed from another by transposing the letters. As, for example, the word result, the letters of which can be transposed to form six other common words. All these various words are found in the old-time poem:

As soon as a player disposes of the one letter which he has drawn he draws another from his own pile in preparation for his next turn. This letter he keeps concealed standing before him, as explained above, until it is played.

From this point on in the game the anagram principle is applied whenever possible and desirable. If a player sees one or more letters in the center of the table, which he can use to form another word by combination with one of his opponent's words or one of his own, he is at liberty to do so, taking the opponent's word and placing it in front of himself as his own, or revising one of his own words by adding one or more such letter.

For example: Assume one of the players to have taken the word "steam," and his opponent draws an R. He can take the word "steam" from his opponent and combining with it the R make "stream," or if the player who held the word "steam" drew the R, he could change his own word to "stream." If there is no better play to make it is wise to play on one's own words as it thus prevents the opponent from making the play and capturing the word.

To follow on with the play either one drawing an S can make the word masters. The letter T will make mattress or smatters. 

An E gives us teamsters. Then all player needs for remonstrates is an R, an N and an O. It would seem that this would complete the play on this combination of letters. Just the letter D, however, will transform the word to demonstrators. 

The player who acquires ten words, none of which the opponent can take away by drawing one more letter, wins the game. 

When Experts Play 

Many play Anagrams permitting words of two or three letters. 

This makes a rather childish game, especially for two players. On the other hand a few real experts in the game scorn even the fourletter words, stipulating a minimum requirement of five or pos-

sibly six letters. These experts assert that there are so many fourletter words which are practically impossible to change, such as wife, that it rewards luck too generously at the expense of real skill. Furthermore, owing to the ease with which four-letter words can be formed, the game is likely to terminate before a sufficient amount of real action enters into it. Just making words which have the minimum number of letters taken from the center of the table does not give one the satisfaction which comes from capturing one of the opponent's words, and the greater the number of letters added, the greater the thrill the brilliant player experiences. 

Some of the very best players do not begin to exert themselves in the game until the opponent has so many words that there is danger of losing the game altogether, when they proceed to take these words away, adding letters so numerous that they defy further play.

Keep the Dictionary at Hand 

It is very essential, while playing the game, to have a dictionary within easy reach. This dictionary must be the final authority and no word can be taken which does not appear therein. For this purpose a large dictionary like the Century is not recommended as it contains many obsolete and uncommon forms. The greatest satisfaction is given by Funk & Wagnalls' Standard desk size or Webster's Collegiate. Either of these will be found to contain quite enough words and variations to assure a most interesting game. Proper names are not permitted even if they appear in the dictionary and only foreign words which are generally admitted into our language and in common use such as beaux and vive are allowed, provided they are found in the dictionary, which is always the last court of appeal.

The laws of Anagrams are simple. We have covered all of them in the directions for playing the game. To sum up the most important of these: The root of the word must be changed whenever a play is made either on a player's own word or his opponent's; in order for a player to procure a word, that word must be spoken and not merely grabbed.
