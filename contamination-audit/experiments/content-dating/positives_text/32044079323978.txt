CHEVALIERS OF HONOR 

HE great organization of Boy Scouts of America will believe that I am sincere when I say that I am highly honored in being selected to prepare for them a message of encouragement and esteem from the American Society of the French Legion of Honor on the occasion of the ceremony of commemoration which will be celebrated on the 157th anniversary of the signing of the only treaty of Alliance ever contracted by the United States of America. 

As you know, this treaty united the then struggling young Republic of the United States of America and the great and powerful kingdom of France. On one side an autocracy, on the other a democracy. 

At first sight this partnership sounds strange and unnatural, but as you remember your French history you will know that the bond was not between two political systems different in principles but between two nations alike in sentiments. [3]

[4] In a letter written in 1779 to Josiah Quincy our first Ambassador to France, Benjamin Franklin, spoke of the French as "most amiable." "There is nothing wanting in the character of a Frenchman," said he, "that belongs to that of an agreeable and worthy man. The spaniards are supposed to be cruel, the English proud, the Scotch insolent, the Dutch avaricious, but I think the French have no national vice ascribed to them." What brought together the French and American people was their common love for Liberty, their respect for Justice, their regard for Honor.

The very spot upon which the Statue of Liberty stands on Bedloe's Island in the Harbor of New York is dedicated to the most potent of those ideals that unite the two Nations, the ideal of Liberty. This noble statue that symbolizes that ideal is a gift by the people of France to the people of the United States. One hundred and eighty French cities contributed more than $700,000 towards the cost of the statue and this, too, at a time when France was prostrate as a result of the Franco-Prussian war, the loss of Alsace-Lorraine and the imposition of a heavy war indemnity by victorious Germany. 

At the time of the laying of the cornerstone of the Bunker Hill Monument in 1825, Daniel Webster uttered words which are as applicable to Bartholdi's statue as they are to the obelisk that overlooks Boston Harbor. "We wish," said he, "that this column, rising towards heaven among the pointed spires of many temples dedicated to God, may contribute to produce in all minds a pious feeling of dependence and gratitude. We wish that the last object to the sight of him who leaves his native shore, and the first to gladden his who revisits it, may be something which shall remind him of the liberty and the glory of his country. Let it rise! let it rise till it meet the sun in his coming; let the earliest light of the morning gild it, and the parting day linger and play on its summit.' 

The motto of the Scouts is "On my honor I will do my best." The motto of the Legion of Honor is "Honneur et Patrie," Honor and Country. So you see, the members of the American Society of the French 

Legion of Honor and the Boy Scouts have something in common. 

Just after the war, we grown-ups took alarm at the apparent collapse of morality among the youth of this great nation. Tests taken among children of school age showed that under temptation more than

[5] half of those undergoing the tests would cheat, lie and steal. What could the future have in store for us if more than half of our eventual citizens started upon their life careers with such an unethical outlook on life! And then, as I read along in the report of these tests, I came upon something that restored my faith in the youth of the country. I saw that a group of Boy Scouts which had been organized two years, had been given this test designed to measure character among boys and my heart warmed as I read, for there it said that 82 per cent of the Scouts had come through the test, clean and unmoved by the temptations to lie, cheat and steal, purposely placed in their way. I want to thank the Boy Scouts for this showing. It is good to feel that there is a group of boys growing up into manhood who are alive to the ideals of 

Liberty, Justice and Honor. 

One of the National heroes of France is a boy-François Joseph Bara. They tell his story in the schools for all French boys to ponder on. It was in the early days of the First French Republic when Napoleon was only General Bonaparte.

In July 1792 when the Legislative Assembly declared that the country was in danger the call was sent out throughout all France for volunteers. Bara was only 14 years old and when he answered the call he was deemed too young to be enrolled. The next year, however, upon the return to the front of a soldier from his village, Bara joined him and the two appeared before the recruiting officer of the 12th Hussard regiment. Although the commanding officer still demurred at making Bara a full fledged member of the regiment, he nevertheless engaged him as his orderly and provided him with a horse and a uniform. 

During several months Bara participated in every battle of the campaign in the ranks of the 12th Hussards. During one action he even succeeded in making two prisoners whom he brought safely back to camp.

Upon being sent with a message to the Commanding Officer of another Republican Division, he lost his way and was captured by a group of Royalist soldiers. When questioned about the number and strength of the Republican forces Bara indignantly refused to betray his comrades. Offers of release and money and threats of death failed to shake his loyalty. Finally, hedged about by a dozen men with bayonets, he was offered his life if he would only cry out "Death to the Republic;

[6] 

Long Live the King." Without a moment's hesitation the boy jerked off his cap with the Red, White and Blue cockade that symbolized his allegiance to the troops of the Revolution and raising it high above his head cried out: "Vive la République" (Long Live the Republic).

Bara was killed for this defiance, but in giving up his life he lived up to the Scout Law which commands you to have "The courage to face danger in spite of fear and to stand up for the right despite jeers or threats."

If the Boy Scouts of America had Honorary Memberships to confer, I think that they would want to number Bara as one of their own. 

This little Frenchman who gave his life for a principle, is characteristic of the frame of mind of all Frenchmen and I think I may be safe in saying, of all Americans. Great nations endure and prosper not through their material wealth but through their spiritual qualities. Behind every noble act is a noble thought, and lofty thoughts are expressed by symbols and images. When the Emperor Napoleon imagined to reward acts of heroism by conferring upon those accomplishing them a bit of red ribbon, he was minting a coin more valuable than gold and creating a symbol powerful enough to cause men to die in pursuit of it. The red badge of the Legion of Honor is more than a bit of ribbon. It is a sign which permits men to be singled out among the crowd and it signifies to Frenchmen Courage, Devotion, Sacrifice, Duty gloriously fulfilled, Danger bravely incurred, Privations, Patience, Knowledge, Talent, Honor, Genius, Labor, and all manly virtues.

This belief in symbols which is characteristic of the French people is apparent in the choosing of their national slogan. The motto of the 

French Nation, LIBERTY—EQUALITY—FRATERNITY, appears contradic tory when it is remembered that the Legion of Honor was designed to raise certain individuals above their fellows. When the motto was first conceived it was in the fever of emancipation of the down-trodden mass from the domination of an hereditary ruling class. The great ideal then was to make the laborer and the peasant as eligible to place and power as the noble. Later, however, when arbitrary differences in rank had been removed, the realization grew that equality, instead of being a blessing, might be a curse. By proclaiming equality there was danger of destroying emulation and by destroying emulation, you destroy pride, ambition and heroism. Equality does not exist in nature; it is a

[7] 'will-o'-the-wisp," chased by the unworthy. Some men will continue to be born stronger, more intelligent and handsomer than others. Out of strength will continue to grow courage; out of intelligence, faith; out of beauty, love. Society has tried to invent compensations for those that were less strong, less intelligent and less beautiful than others. 

Such are money, education, position. Nowhere is there equality, however, and everywhere there exists a praiseworthy ambition to rise above the common level through effort and merit. 

The people who have not that ambition cannot constitute a powerful nation. What has made the Power and the Glory of France and of the United States is this natural aspiration of the individual to distinguish himself by more courage, more effort, more labor. All the equality there is lies in the fact that all doors are open to all men. 

As to Liberty and Fraternity, these are virtues to be embraced by all men. The code of the Boy Scouts calls for their observance. Another virtue that is not foreign to the French, any more than it is to the Boy Scouts, is the virtue of Gratitude. I would like you, as members of the 

Boy Scouts of America, to learn about the services of France to Humanity and particularly to our own country so that you may ever be grateful to that generous nation. When Thomas Jefferson returned to America, after serving in France as our American Ambassador, he wrote in his diary as follows: 

"I cannot leave this great and good country without expressing my sense of its pre-eminence of character among the nations of the earth. A more benevolent people I have never known, nor greater warmth and devotedness in their select friendships."

Like attracts like. During the last War we Americans found ourselves brothers to the French. The war is over but the two nations still burn with the same fire, thrill to the same emotions and are inspired by the same ideals.

The soul of France is the soul of America and the doctrines of Descartes, of Pascal and of Rousseau, awake an echo in American intellects and in American hearts.

I recommend you to learn French because you will be taught a language which will not voice thoughts that are abhorrent to you but one that will give expression to thoughts with which you are already in sympathy, a tongue that will proclaim Truths which you already

[8] perceive because your thoughts are attuned to Beauty.

Gratitude is a fruit of great cultivation. The Statue of Liberty, under whose shadow many of you have stood, is the outward symbol of the gratitude of France for Red Cross work done by us during the 

Franco-Prussian war in French hospitals and among bereaved families, victims of the war. As France has remembered this comparatively slight service, let us remember France's service to us at the time when we had no friend but her. The anniversary of the Franco-American 

Treaty brings to mind an event of gigantic importance. The declaration by France to the world at large that our quarrel was her quarrel and that her strength and her treasure and the blood of her sons would henceforth be spent in our defense. 

Another treaty has been signed at Washington providing for the arbitration of all differences that ever may arise between the two sister republics. War between them was never of the remotest possibility but the new treaty is a new symbol, a notice to the world that the Fraternity which bound us with spiritual bonds for over a century and a half is now supplemented by a solemn covenant which binds us for eternity.
