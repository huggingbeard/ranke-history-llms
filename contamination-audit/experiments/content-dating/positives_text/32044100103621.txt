CHAPTER I 

THE TOMB 

OUR thousand years ago Egypt must have been a very different land from what 

Few casualties in battles it is today. We know little about the Delta and can only suppose that it was smaller of 2000 B.C. and far more marshy than it is now. Up the river from Memphis to Gebel Silsileh the cultivated areas were narrower. Moreover, throughout most of its length the Nile flowed in a river bed higher than the land on either hand, where water remained in stagnant papyrus swamps, haunted only by fishermen and fowlers and their game. In an Egypt so closely circumscribed the population must have been far smaller, and it had even less chance to grow because over almost the entire country agriculture was limited to one crop a year. Furthermore, in times of famine the importation of foodstuffs from abroad was unthinkable in a land hemmed in on all sides by practically impenetrable deserts and the sea. 

In a small country, and one whose population was proportionately thin, wars also must have been on a small scale. It is therefore not surprising that a change of dynasties should have resulted from a battle in which the dead on the victorious side numbered no more than sixty men, who fell on two separate days of struggle. Such is the contention of the following pages. Now for a brief telling of the story as it unfolded itself to us, about a score of years before these pages were written. 

In March, 1923, the Arab workmen of the Egyptian Expedition were clearing the hillside at Deir el Baḥri when we realized that what had looked like a gap in the rank of gigantic burial places overlooking the temple was actually the site of another tomb, just a little lower downhill than its fellows and entirely masked by rock fallen from the cliff above. 

One day that spring all the rest of the Expedition staff were going to Howard Carter's place in the Valley of the Kings to see him unwrap a lot of staves from the tomb of Tūt-ankh- Amun, and it looked as though their absence would give me an uninterrupted afternoon in which to make a great discovery. The unblocked doorway of the tomb was cleared of the rubbish which hid it, and the men worked with the greatest care, until the yawning darkness within the entrance at last showed up-and one glance inside disclosed a ghastly pile of torn and plundered bodies. In the intense disappointment which followed, we noted only their unpleasant freshness and without much thought took them for dead monks from the early Christian monastery which had once existed in the near-by temple and had given it its modern name. It was natural to want to make a striking find while everyone in our house was still excited over Carter's discovery, but a lot of plundered Coptic bodies was hardly a sufficient substitute for the magnificent tomb he had found, so we hastily sealed up the

So the tomb remained for the next three years, until during the fast of Ramaḍān in 1926 a few workmen were told to start early after sun-up one morning to hunt among the torn bandages still with the bodies, on the off chance that some one of them might have been marked. We chose the cool of the morning because it would be intensely hot later in the day and the job promised to be thoroughly unpleasant. To assure a good, conscientious search we offered five piasters for any bit of inscription found. As we expected nothing, this offer was a mere gesture on our part. 

Long before noon that day we had found sixty linen marks.¹ Among them were twentynine examples of a sign which we and others had already found on sheets from the Eleventh Dynasty tombs in the Neb-hepet-Rēr cemetery and which we had also discovered on a copper chisel from an Eleventh Dynasty tomb overlooking the temple. On twenty-six other sheets there were names current in Thebes in the same period. And finally, on some five more were unintelligible signs also written in ink. The names on the linen presumably had nothing to do with the bodies wrapped in the cloth, except that both names and bodies must have been of the same epoch. What we had found were the corpses of some threescore individuals who had lived in the exciting period of the struggle which reunited Egypt after about eighty years of civil war. Dr. Douglas E. Derry, Professor of Anatomy at the Kasr el 'Ainy Medical School in Cairo, came up immediately to examine the bodies as a result of our call for help.2 On his arrival, Dr. Derry, James Brewster, who was on our expedition at the time, and I started in together to see what we had found. The bodies had already been brought out of the dark inner passage of the tomb and had been laid outside while Walter Hauser made a plan of the interior. A large tent fly was put up to keep off the glaring sun, and we three set to work. 

It is obvious that the tomb was one of the earliest burial places dug in the cemetery and that it was never intended to be a monument to any particular person, as was its next-door neighbor, where the Chancellor Khety was eventually buried. No attempt had been made to give it a showy façade. In fact, at most the tomb-cutters had roughed out its front at the foot of the cliff, and the quarrymen's chip from this work and from the inner passages had been dumped before it to make a more or less smooth court, sloping down the hillside. Nor were any distinct boundaries ever given this approach, like the brick or stone walls which bounded the ramps leading to the enormous tombs of the Chancellor and his peers to the east. Furthermore, this tomb was not so high up the hill as those of the great nobles, and when eventually a fall of rock from the cliff above had buried it, there had been nothing to suggest that any tomb had ever been dug on this spot.

THE TOMB 

The tomb was, however, still high enough up the slope to be hard to reach over the rough boulders on the hillside below it. From the bottom of the slope to the tomb entrance, if you went straight up the hill, you had to climb from 30 to 35 m. within a horizontal distance of less than 100 m., and it looked as though the ancient Egyptians had solved the difficulty of such a toilsome ascent by constructing a stairway of mud bricks at a far more gentle slope, starting from a point well to the east. When we excavated the lower part of the courtyard of Khety's tomb we found the bottom steps of this stairway, a little over 250 cm. wide-which was doubtless intended for 5 cubits-with a low, round-topped balustrade on either hand. This, like the steps themselves, had been plastered over with white desert clay. There are only five steps left, rising some 59 cm., but they follow a line which, if prolonged, would lead up to the entrance of this tomb. One might consider that these steps were the bottom of a stairway zigzagging up the slope to the tomb of Khety in a number of short flights, were it not for the fact that apparently the stair was buried under the grading of Khety's tomb ramp. It would appear, therefore, that even before Khety planned the approach to his tomb these steps already existed and that up them one climbed to the tomb we were now clearing. 

The tomb is the earliest of three in the vicinity which differ from all others in the cemetery in being designed as catacombs to be enlarged as necessity demanded. At first it had been proposed to cut the forecourt into the hillside a bit more than 23 m. wide, from side to side, but very little was ever done to complete it, probably since the ownership of the tomb was never centered in a single individual who desired to make a show place of it. From the entrance in this forecourt a long, central corridor goes straight back into the cliff, with two steps leading down about halfway along it. As the floor descends, the ceiling becomes lower at an even sharper angle, and from having been over 220 cm. high at the entrance of the tomb, this central corridor ends at the back with a height of less than 160 cm. About halfway along this central passage, just before the first step down, there is a door on the right, one step up from the corridor. At first the side passage starts level, but where it forks the two branches begin to go down slightly to two burial chambers, each large enough for at least one good-sized coffin. Beyond the two steps down the floor of the main corridor descends slightly, with two side passages sloping lower still and not high enough for even a short man to stand upright in them. These side passages end in burial crypts, with an additional small crypt opening off the side of the passage on the right. Beyond these burial places a cross passage was started on either hand, but neither branch was ever finished, and the central corridor comes to an end almost immediately.

Someone filling baskets with such chip had dropped a hoe blade of tamarisk wood, split apart for its whole length, and we found one half still in the tomb (fig. 1). Space could always have been found for another crypt, but this tomb had been sealed up before any of the burial chambers already quarried out had been blocked up as one would expect them to be. Originally it was planned that each crypt should be walled off from the main corridor, once the burial had been laid in it, as had been done in the other two catacombs described above. But the sixty or more bodies which we found in it must have been stacked up in the chambers and along the corridors like so much cord wood and no walls could have been built across them, for there were neither bricks nor stones found. With chambers but little over 1 m. high it is improbable that more than three layers of bodies could have been put in them. Even in the somewhat higher main passage it is unlikely that there could have been more without a chance that some of the piles would topple over. These bodies were not protected by wooden coffins, for we found fragments of only two plain, undecorated coffins in the tomb, and it would seem fair to conclude that there never had been more than a pair of corpses laid in such protections. 

The rest were stacked in their scant bandages one on top of another. 

In the course of time the backs and limbs of those put in on top were contorted by the All at one time uneven surfaces of the bodies on which they lay. Yet all must have been buried at one and the same time, for no ancient Egyptian would have left so many unprotected corpses lying in an open tomb to be disturbed by any passing vandal. It is fair enough to say that the catacomb was sealed with such boulders, because we would surely have recognized the débris of a brick wall at the mouth of the passage while fallen field stones might not have been noticed. 

Tradition would have preserved the catacomb and its contents from disturbance during the Eleventh and Twelfth Dynasties. By the Thirteenth Dynasty there probably would have been no memory of who was buried there, and during the time that Egypt was half overrun by foreign barbarians in the days of the Hyksos someone must have broken into it to rob its occupants. At this period Thebes was once more an unimportant provincial town, the capital of Upper Egypt only. Shortly afterwards, perhaps about 1600 B.C., an enormous section of rock slid off the hillside and buried the entrance to the tomb completely. There it lay, and the tomb entrance being lower than its neighbors, the very existence of anything at this point was forgotten. Otherwise it seems likely that the small amount of labor necessary to remove the fallen chip would have been undertaken by later burial parties, the plundered bodies would have been swept to one side, and this tomb would have become the last resting place of mummies like those laid away in the temple below and in the tombs all around.

The bodies, once they had been brought out into the sunlight, all proved to be those of About 60 men. We actually identified only fifty-nine skulls and fifty-two right femurs, but our record numbers ran up to ninety-six because many of the bodies were so completely dismembered that we soon saw that it would be impossible to reassemble them with any assurance. In fact, so thorough had been the tomb robbers' job of scattering the bones around that in no more than ten cases do our notes bring together whole skeletons with certainty.¹ However, if we say that there were about sixty men buried in the tomb, we cannot be far wrong, nor will we be in error if we add that they were sixty soldiers of the army of King Nebhepet-Re, slain in battle.

One will readily see that we have here skulls of men more robust than the local Predynastic type. They are more like those who lived in Gizeh during the Old Kingdom, and at Sedment, part way up the Nile, during the transition leading to the Middle Kingdom. 

The similarity between these soldiers' skulls and those from Sedment is striking and poses some questions which are impossible, or at least extraordinarily difficult, to answer. The skulls from Sedment were measured by Dr. T. L. Woo in the Biometric Laboratory at 

University College, London, who concluded that they belonged to foreigners. Dr. Derry does not call our soldiers foreigners but would be tempted to suggest that they were born of marriages between foreign mercenaries and local Upper Egyptian women, pointing out that their unusual stature may indicate a foreign element in their ancestry. If they were natives of Egypt, Derry might look on them as being Heracleopolitan soldiers, killed fighting against Thebes, and buried honorably in the Theban necropolis. If such a fate seems a bit fanciful, Derry would have it that they were northerners killed in battle while fighting on the Theban side and honorably buried by the latter in the cemetery at Thebes. 

To me the logical way out of the difficulty is to consider that we are dealing here with the skulls of native, Egyptian-born soldiers, possibly from the northern borders of Upper Egypt, but none the less a body of men selected for the army of King Neb-hepet-Rēr Mentu-hotpe. The majority of these soldiers may well have belonged racially to the people of the borderland, but a few of them may have dwelt as far south as Thebes, though their physical type would probably not figure in our modern tables of Eleventh Dynasty Theban skeletons. That they were, for their period, big fellows should not surprise us. After all they were men picked for the army which conquered Heracleopolis, were in robust health, some being veterans of earlier battles, as their scars show, and none past the prime of life. 

That they were buried in Thebes, which may well have been up river from the homes of most of them, is merely evidence that in actuality all of Egypt up to Gebel Silsileh was in process of falling under the sway of the northern race, even if for the time being its ruler claimed the south as his birthplace. This had happened before. According to tradition 

Menes, the founder of the First Dynasty, was said to have been a Thinite, of the nome of Abydos, though eventually he ruled Egypt from the head of the Delta at Memphis, and this was to happen again when Ath-mose of Thebes founded the Eighteenth Dynasty.

On some sixty soldiers there were forty-five recognizable ante-mortem wounds, and six The wounds of the bodies had been pecked at by carrion birds. Naturally, we may have noted wounds on a body and on a separated head or the arms which belonged to it. To offset such chances of duplication there was many a skeleton that had lost almost all its tissues which could have shown mortal wounds, and from many a well-preserved body an arrow might have been extracted without leaving a recognizable trace on the shriveled surface. Since every corpse had lost more or less skin and every one was shrunken up, there was little chance of recognizing surface wounds which, though apparently small, might nevertheless have been fatal. 

On the other hand, the tearing and shredding by vultures was indirect proof that the men had been killed, because it indicated that their bodies had lain exposed. We have, then, proof of violent death for well over half our soldiers, and while the other bodies had been too much mangled to show wounds, I have no doubt that they too were slain in battle.

Most of the bodies, as far as could be seen, had been laid in the tomb on their backs, and it was usually the tissues of the back of the trunk which had disappeared. The normal position for Middle Kingdom corpses was lying on the left side, but without coffins to hold them they would naturally roll over and lie prone. Now and then a body was seen which, from the collapse of the bones and the decay of the front of the trunk, would seem to have been placed in the tomb face downwards by mistake-a thing which might easily have happened if a great number of corpses without coffins were handled at one time. Some of the bodies had assumed extraordinarily contorted attitudes, as has been noted, the dislocations of those in the lower rows suggesting that they became twisted by the settling of the heap above them. Thus the soldiers lay undisturbed for several centuries, until robbers entered the tomb, pillaging the bodies and casting them hither and yon. Then followed over three thousand years during which they were not molested. Yet few traces of crystalline fatty acids were remarked, the fats still being in a condition to melt and run in the sun, and the bodies were quite as fresh as Roman bodies, or Coptic ones of the early Christian period. The explanation for this must lie in the utter dryness of the tomb itself; for since it was situated high on the hillside, absolutely no moisture could possibly enter it. 

The well-preserved bodies showed that there were no embalmers' openings, either into the cranial cavity or into the abdomen. The head was ordinarily intact, with the skin on the face and the hair complete and the tongue often clenched between the teeth. The skin on the body had usually a blotched and discolored look and had lost much of the epidermis, which was frequently adherent to the inner bandages, and the thick epidermis of the soles of the feet was generally loose. The skin on the trunk was ordinarily intact, except on the side on which the body had lain. The nails of the fingers and toes were for the most part present. Tendons were naturally very well preserved, but it was remarkable to see the thigh muscles in solid lumps 5 cm. thick, still showing to a large extent their general form and structure. The internal organs were frequently recognizable. There was no distention or prolapse of the anus, and the one case of umbilical hernia may or may not have been post-mortem. 35 On the other hand, the bowels were apparently empty of faeces, none of which had been discharged into the wrappings. The genitalia were always bloated to enormous proportions. The feet often looked puffed up, and the arms and legs sometimes appeared to have been swollen, a condition which may have developed in the earlier stages of putrefaction and not be an indication of stoutness in life. All of these conditions may be assigned to the natural progress of decomposition both before and after bandaging. 

The bodies presented every variation of appearance from that just described to piles of bones freed of all tissue but still greasy with the degenerated body materials. Some of these differences in condition were obviously due to the varying fates which the bodies had

These soldiers were buried about 2060 B.C., and, as we have seen, they were probably left in peace for about four centuries. Then, possibly during the Hyksos period, grave robbers broke into the tomb. Every corpse was slit open from its face to its knees as the thieves looked for plunder, and practically all of those bodies which did not fall completely out of their wrappings had obviously lost several layers of bandages. Very shortly after this, fortunately for us, there followed the landslide which covered the mouth of the tomb so effectively that until 1923 it appeared that there had never been any tomb in this space in the cliff.

To recapitulate, we have here some sixty soldiers of the army of King Neb-hepet-Re', all of whom were either killed or grievously wounded by arrows and stones hurled from the battlements of a fortress they were attacking or were dispatched by the garrison during a sortie when their companions had fled out of range. Then they were torn by vultures, during the lull before the attackers once more dared come back to the fray. A second assault saw the fall of the citadel and the defeat of its defenders, when so great was the triumph of Neb-ḥepet-Rer that he had all the bodies of his slain soldiers gathered up from the battlefield, including those half rotted by their exposure since the first assault. All the corpses were scoured off with sand from some near-by desert valley, and their limbs were composed as well as possible. The bodies of two dead officers were more heavily bandaged than the others and laid in simple, undecorated coffins. Each common soldier was given adequate wrappings, and then all were borne to Thebes for burial. There was no such variation in their treatment as we might naturally have expected, had they been the fallen in several different engagements over a period of years. All were the dead of one siege. None had died in hand-to-hand fights from slashes by axes, probably none were the victims of stabs by spears and daggers, nor had any of them arms broken by the blows of maces and clubs. 

These soldiers were killed and wounded with missiles, and such as were grievously hurt and were helpless when the garrison made its sortie had been clubbed to death. In length it is now only 37 cm., for one end has been ripped off, but in the case of another tufted towel which we found in a near-by Eleventh Dynasty tomb the length was 52 cm., or just about 1 cubit. 28 In the case of the towel from the tomb of the soldiers every eighth weft thread is doubled, and at intervals of a little over 6 mm. it is pulled out to make a short, loose loop. Such loops come opposite intervals in the rows on either side and cover the towel with a rough pile (fig. 3). This piece of linen, together with the two others from the near-by tomb, was obviously part of either a "Turkish" bath towel or what we might more properly call a napkin such as was often carried by a servant along with a ewer and basin for washing the hands of the guests at meals. 29
