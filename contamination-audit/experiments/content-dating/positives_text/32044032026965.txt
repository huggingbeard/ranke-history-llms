THE ORGANIZATION OF THE U.S. NATIONAL SPACE EFFORT INTRODUCTION 

Currently, the United States finds itself in the position of competing with the Soviet Union in space activities. Although this competition may not be acknowledged, the psychological effect of its outcome on the peoples of all nations may tip the world balance of power in favor of the winner. It is also conceivable that eventual military uses of space may be of such magnitude as to transcend existing military concepts.

Soviet accomplishments in the past several years have given cause for concern both as to their implications of military capability and as to their psychological effects on the free world. These successes serve as stimuli for more and stronger efforts if the United States is to lead in the military and peaceful exploitation of space. There may have been a tendency in some quarters to minimize the significance of Soviet attainments. The United States has the problems of counteracting any military threat imposed and, at the same time, of preserving the belief of the free world in its ultimate technological victory. This country will be forced to spend more and more of its resources in detecting and tracking Soviet satellites either announced or designed for surveillance of the United States. 

This paper is a study of the organization and management of the space program of the United States. It has been written with the thought in mind that the "race for space" is a matter of national concern and prestige.

Basic management principles require that a goal or target for achievement be set so that plans and programs can be formulated with common purpose. In the military, the planning, tables of organization, and supporting requirements all stem from a statement of objectives approved by the National Security Council. Similarly, there must be a clear statement of national intent in the exploitation of space.

It should contain an indication of the progress desired in relation to that of our potential enemies. A logical national objective might be: To maintain world leadership in the peaceful exploitation of space and astronautical sciences and to insure that military applications in the area are energetically prosecuted in the interest of national security. 

This goal would provide for a series of achievement targets and dates by which they are to be reached. These would permit a review of progress and an assessment of program management. 

Having stated the objective, a national military and civilian space program that outlines the steps necessary to meet the national goal is a logical consequence. It would contain the elements of a balanced military, scientific, and commercial effort that will satisfy the current, intermediate, and long-range needs of the Nation. The program 1

would generally define the degree of participation in space matters by Federal agencies, educational and nonprofit institutions, and appropriate segments of industry. It would provide for the best use of money, manpower, and material in executing space research, development and operational projects. For example, the space activities of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and the 

Department of Defense (DOD) are supported at a comparable level of funding. These activities must be administered with clear indication of priority as to which of several space projects should receive the most emphasis. Both agencies have booster development programs that certainly can contribute to each other without being competitive in terms of industrial and scientific facilities and talents. 

Military and civilian space projects require extensive launching, tracking, and recovery facilities. In the interest of economy, these should not be duplicated but rather utilized to their fullest in accordance with a nationally established priority. Firm priorities serve to resolve associated funding problems since all space programs are in a sense competing for dollars. In some instances in the past they may have been funded on the basis of individual appeal, rather than to any established schedules or priorities of national interest. It is in this area that we begin to see that the essential ingredients of accomplishment-money, manpower, and material-require the prosecution of centralized schedules and priorities for effective management. 

It is not difficult to appreciate the military advantages of Earthorbiting satellites for communication, reconnaissance, meteorological, and navigation purposes. The import of boost-glide vehicles is not quite as clear. When one considers cislunar travel and maneuverable space vehicles with manned Moon stations, the impact on national military posture is not as yet evident. There are opinions that Moon landings are the purview of the NASA and of civilian interest only. 

These feelings give rise to a concentration of military effort on orbiting satellites with potential military applications of manned space flight left in limbo. As presently organized and staffed, it is not clear that NASA is in a position to direct its attention to military potentials of space with the same thought and concern given the extension of our scientific knowledge. NASA is engaged in a space program primarily directed to peaceful applications. The three military services and the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) have sponsored programs in research and development that are slanted toward defense purposes with some generalized research on materials and propulsion related to all space applications. With the transfer of many of the DOD space-oriented resources to NASA, the onus of new accomplishments that may ultimately be applicable to military systems may shift to the latter agency. In the present state of technology, it is difficult to tell what is peaceful and what is a military application. NASA is directed to tell the military if it finds information of military value, but the determination is not necessarily made with military counsel. As presently organized, there is no requirement that the NASA program contribute extensively to the future military capability needed to enhance the national security.

When one considers the diversity of agencies engaged in space research and development, he sees the possibility of dilution of effort that could develop a host of information at appreciable overall cost, but which might not represent the progress that could be made by

The Atomic Energy Commission, National Science Foundation, Na tional Bureau of Standards, Army, Navy, Air Force, Advanced Research Projects Agency, and the Smithsonian Institution, to name a few, are all involved in related space research sponsored or assisted by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration and the

Within the Department of Defense a similar situation exists and, despite coordination of major programs, some duplicative effort in supporting space programs exists. While coordination is very effective in transfer of information and in arranging joint efforts when firm targets and goals have been set, intelligent management and strong leadership can best tie together the efforts of DOD and NASA for the common good.

The services are rightfully concerned with satisfying the defense commitments of our foreign policy which, under available fiscal support, means that almost their entire attention is focused on the logistic and R. & D. support required to meet the needs of the immediate future. Under these conditions it has been difficult to fund the investigations that are necessary to find out what space has to offer militarily some years ahead. Space research competes for funds needed for development of antisubmarine warfare measures, continental air defense, and strategic weapons. With the transfer of space development projects from the Advanced Research Projects Agency to the services, the problem may become even more acute. The military must then depend heavily on NASA for the long look ahead, even though the latter is primarily concerned with peaceful applications.

As the space program grows, the possibility of duplication of effort between the DOD and NASA increases. Both are operating agencies. 

At present, NASA uses many DOD launch ng and track ng facilities As it gets into an expanded program, it may be that the Administrator will consider it necessary to build facilities that would relieve him of his dependence on the DOD. Certainly the missile and satellite booster development programs of both agencies and the more fundamental studies on materials and propulsion would appear to be duplicative to some extent. It is possible that the competent capabilities of industry might be overextended by calls on its services by both the DOD and NASA unless effective coordination and management are maintained. The growth of tracking stations all over the world demands that every effort be made to make them suitable for general purpose use and to schedule and manage their operations to keep the facilities to a minimum. 

There are the very real technological problems of improving propellants and propulsion systems to increase specific impulse and total thrust so that greater and greater payloads can be put into space.

the payload equipment is recognized. Sufficient talent for reliability monitoring is still a problem. Auxiliary power for active satellite systems is the subject of extensive study, but breakthroughs must be made to achieve long-life units capable of supplying substantial power. 

Satellite and booster recovery still remain problems as do those of satellite interception and rendezvous. Certain space applications such as communication, meteorology, and navigation will depend on the ability to accurately establish and control satellite orbits and to achieve a high degree of circularity. 

The roles and missions of the services in what we have come to call conventional warfare have been fairly well defined. With the advent of the space age, each has rightfully wished to exploit the advantages of satellites and to explore the potential of space as it relates to the service mission. This is where difficulties arise. One stabilizing agency in the Department of Defense in space matters-ARPA has been directed to transfer the majority of its systems to the military departments and to engage only in generalized space research. A clearer policy for management of space research, development, and warfare systems and their support in the Department of Defense is emerging under the leadership of Secretary McNamara.

On examining the current organization for space, it can be seen that two major groups are going forward with the bulk of the space investigations, namely NASA and DOD. Some small changes in our national organization for space might facilitate the coordination and planning necessary for a strong national space program. Many knowledgeable people in the Congress, the scientific community, industry, and the military hold the opinion that some changes would help the country to meet the Soviet challenge and to reap the benefits that space can offer.

Any organizational changes that one might recommend must be based upon criteria which satisfy the national needs if they are to be effective. The following criteria were applied to the various organizational patterns studied and discussed later: 

If the United States is to enjoy an effective national space effort- It must be recognized that national prestige is at stake. 

National goals and the implementing program must be clearly delineated.

Effective direction and control of the national space program to meet national goals are the primary functions to be performed. 

The authority for national program direction must be placed at a high enough level to be effective. 

The organization must be dynamic in nature with both military and civilian representation. 

Responsibility of the organization should be defined in clear, precise terms with commensurate authority provided. 

Centralized military programing is essential, regardless of position or structure of the national directing agency. 

The organization should be provided with adequate staffing and funding as essential tools for competent management. 

The organization must be assured of clear and direct lines of communication for program direction and control. 

The management organization must be responsive to and capable of evaluating the requirements of both civilian and military needs.

Management principles of scheduling and control must be exercised to insure program prosecution in conformance with established national goals.

A control system must be instituted to assure a "feedback" or review mechanism which establishes accountability for performance.

A well-defined system of program priority assignment must be established and a schedule closely adhered to in the expenditure of national effort for attainment of national goals. 

Available national resources coupled with a need for the maximum possible rate of national program progress necessitate a highly integrated research and development and operational program.

It is essential that the total national capabilities, including those unique to the individual services, NASA and unified commanders, be fully exploited.

Support facilities must be commensurate with the scope of the technical program to be prosecuted.

This possible solution assumes the transfer of all military space projects to NASA. It has the advantage of concentrating all space activity in one organization. This system worked very well for the development of atomic weapons-a situation in which a specified article of hardware was developed. However, in the case of space systems, much of the training and facilities that would be used for space hardware development exists within the military departments and cannot easily be transferred to another activity. This is true because the personnel and facilities referred to are being used for other purposes (although related), principally the research, development, test, and evaluation of aircraft and missile systems. Further, combining completely the military and civilian development of space systems would be contrary to the expressed intention of this country to explore space for scientific and peaceful purposes. It should be noted, however, that Congress recognized that probable military uses of space do exist and has stated that the Defense Department should be responsible for space programs essential to the national defense.

Further, it would require disestablishment of NASA, would indicate that both the DOD and NASA were incapable of performing satisfactorily in this work, and would separate the military users of the space systems from the developers of these systems.

RECOMMENDATIONS 

It was clear after studying the five organization patterns that no one of them was sufficiently comprehensive in scope to satisfy the basic needs of a national space program. The solution, to be completely responsive to the precepts of good management, dictates the need for a top level organization to act as an executive agent for the President in carrying out his responsibilities which are clearly delineated by the Space Act. Also required is a clearer definition of 

Defense Department relationships with NASA and some reorganization within the Department of Defense in recognition of the complexity and magnitude of the space program. 

The following changes in organization are proposed:

The creation of the Office of Space Management (OSM), acting as the executive agent of the President through the National 

Aeronautics and Space Council. 

The abolishment of the Civilian Military Liaison Committee (CMLC). 

The creation of a Joint Division of Military Applications 

(JDMA) within the National Aeronautics and Space Administra tion (NASA), reporting directly to the Administrator.

THE OFFICE OF SPACE MANAGEMENT 

The President is charged with the responsibility for the formulation of a national space goal, a national program, and for management of that program. He is provided with high level advice and consultation by the National Aeronautics and Space Council. After almost 3 years of operating within the framework of the National Space Act, it is clear that the President, to effectively discharge his responsibilities, must be provided with an executive agent to assist him in coping with the complex management and administration of a national space program which is growing by leaps and bounds each year. 

The mission of the Office of Space Management would be to assist the President through the NASC in the determination of the national goals for the space age, to formulate a national space program, and to manage that program in the interests of national prestige, national scientific achievement, and national security.

Based upon the national goal, the Director of the Office of Space Management would be responsible for the formulation of a national program, both civilian and military. Stated in broad terms, the program must be a bold one that will satisfy the requirements for adding to our storehouse of basic knowledge of space and applying this knowledge to the development of useful systems that will enhance the ability of this country to win the ideological and technical contest with the Soviets. In so doing, the program must define the responsibilities of the Department of Defense, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, the Atomic Energy Commission, and other agencies of the Government in the prosecution of the national program. 

There must not only be a recognition of scientific and military potentialities, but also the commercial potentialities such as communications, broadcasting, television, worldwide weather surveillance, and navigation for commercial sea and air carriers. Not to be forgotten is the provision and coordination of U.S. participation in international efforts for the exploitation of space technology, including peaceful cooperation with the Soviets in this field, as well as with our allies. 

The Office of Space Management would be charged with the responsibility for managing the national space program, both military and civilian. Management would include such things as technical review and approval of the programs of the DOD, NASA, and other agencies of the Government responsible for prosecuting this country's space program. Proper management requires technical and fiscal review authority over the military and civilian space programs which together make up the national program. The OSM would exercise broad management control through the Defense Space Agency (mentioned previously but discussed later), of the national missile ranges, worldwide tracking facilities, and other necessary space support efforts needed to prosecute the national program.

The Director would also establish priorities and schedules for approved national space programs. This would include establishing plateaus of achievement which development agencies can be expected to realistically attain, consistent with national goals and the urgency of the situation. It would require continuous review and analysis of programs in being to insure that they are properly oriented and that unnecessary duplication of effort and facilities is not diluting the national effort. The OSM would sponsor the establishment of a clearinghouse for all Government agencies, commercial contractors, foundations, and universities engaged in solving the technological and operational problems of the space age. It would aid in keeping the scientific and Government community well informed and assist in eliminating undesirable or unnecessary duplication of effort. 

The effective execution of these management functions would seem to indicate the need for the establishment of a management center at the headquarters of the Director, such as that currently operated so successfully by the Navy Special Projects Office in the management of the development of the Navy Fleet Ballistic Missile Weapon System (Polaris). It might be that field offices would be required at the national missile ranges; other DOD, NASA, AEC installations, and centers of industry to provide monitoring and management direction at all levels.

The Congress will shortly be holding hearings on the progress of this country's space effort and will be considering legislation submitted by the new administration proposing amendment of the National Space Act of 1958. Such hearings and the annual congressional review of space accomplishments require months. They require a small army of witnesses to march to the Hill. Much of the information contained in the testimony is the view from "deep among the trees." Should an Office of Space Management be created, Congress would have someone who could in fact look "over the forest" and could present a national space program to the Congress from a position of centralized responsibility and authority. 

THE JOINT DIVISION OF MILITARY APPLICATIONS 

Under the present organization, the military and civilian space programs are loosely coordinated through the Civilian Military Liaison Committee. This Committee is vested with no executive power, but provides a meeting ground where DOD and NASA can discuss areas of mutual interest. No action mechanism is provided in the Committee charter. Mr. William M. Holaday, former Chairman of the

Committee, has testified before the Congress that the Committee serves no useful purpose and should be abolished. The House of 

Representatives recommended such action, but the bill died in the Senate. The type of liaison performed by this Committee is the type one would expect to occur on an informal basis between ranking executives engaged in similar pursuits. Therefore, it is suggested that the CMLC be disestablished and that a Joint Division of Military Applications be established in NASA reporting directly to the Administrator. It is believed that such an organization within NASA would provide the necessary day-to-day liaison and working relationships with DOD to insure that the efforts of the civilian space program are carefully screened for potential military applications. Conversely, it would insure that the requirements for military support of the civil-

The mission of the proposed Joint Division of Military Applications would be to provide a staff organization oriented to assess the military potentials of space technology accruing from the civilian oriented basic research, space flight, and satellite programs; to provide liaison between DOD and NASA in areas of mutual interest and responsibility; and to provide the Administrator with a planning staff for military support of the civilian space program. DEPARTMENT OF DEFENSE REORGANIZATION 

The space age promises to add a new dimension to military strategy and tactics. Many of the established concepts of military operations may change as military space applications become more clearly defined.

Recently, a missile and space ground support organization was set up within the organizational framework of the Director of Defense Research and Engineering. This group, under the direction of Lt. 

Gen. Donald N. Yates, USAF, is responsible for broad management control of the national missile ranges with respect to large missile and space program assignments to these ranges; establishing and maintaining inventories of all ground environment equipment; and recommending appropriate steps which will provide in the Department of Defense for more effective, efficient, and economical administration and operation, eliminate unnecessary duplication, or contribute to improve military preparedness. The assumption used in the study was that the potential magnitude and complexity of the national space program requires that the Department of Defense review its organizational structure beyond the step recently taken. 

Three basic approaches were considered. These were single service responsibility for space program management and operational space management at the OSD level with operational support under the Joint Chiefs of Staff; and all space management and operations under the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Each was carefully studied, a typical mission was written, and advantages and disadvantages developed for each. Again it was found that two of the possible solutions standing alone could not satisfy the stated criteria. 

The major disadvantages for single service management and operational control were: 

Makes two services subordinate to a third.

Abrogates JCS authority. 

May complicate service relationships. 

May not utilize other services' capabilities. 

May inhibit initiative.

May absorb other services' personnel and facilities. 

Cannot be fully responsive to needs of other services.

The major disadvantages for placing the responsibility for all space matters, except basic research and development, under the Joint 

Chiefs of Staff were:

Assigns many additional functions to JCS. 

Requires a large management staff.

It is believed that the most logical organizational step to take within the Department of Defense is the creation of a Defense Space Agency at the Assistant Secretary level or within an existing office of the Secretary at a comparable level, and that a Defense Space Support Agency should be created under the JCS. This recognizes the fact that business management and administration of the entire military space program rightfully belongs in the Office of the Secretary of Defense and that military space operations and worldwide support of the U.S. space program can best be carried out under the JCS. 

The Defense Space Agency would, for the Secretary of Defense, be responsible to the Director, Office of Space Management, for the development, direction, and coordination of military space programs. 

The Director would be responsible to the Secretary of Defense for insuring that military space programs are in consonance with the national priorities and schedules set forth by OSM. The Agency would exercise management control over all military space efforts in the areas of basic research, component development, systems concepts, and development of operational military systems. Management control would include review and approval of military procurements, facilities, budgeting, funds allocation, and scheduling. The Director would assign space systems, projects, and scientific investigations to the military departments based upon capabilities and requirements. 

In addition, it is envisioned that DSA would exercise, through the military departments, broad management of the national missile ranges and all space vehicle detection, tracking, and computing facilities assigned to DOD by the Office of Space Management. The 

Defense Space Agency would also be responsive to the requirements of the civilian space programs delineated by OSM for military support. 

The Defense Space Agency would consist of a Director, either civilian or military, a Deputy, and necessary staff for planning, for facilities, for fiscal matters, and for scheduling. The staff would consist of balanced representation from the Army, Navy, and Air Force. Liaison representation from NASA and OSM would be appropriate. If either the Director or Deputy were military, the position(s) would be rotated among the services. 

At this point, it might be well to clarify certain internal relationships with other offices within OSD, the military departments, and the JCS, should the DSA be created as a separate agency.

DSA would coordinate the prosecution of the military space program which has been reviewed and approved from the standpoint of technical feasibility and research, development, test, and evaluation funding by the D.D.R. & E. space program commensurate with the needs of national defense. 

With respect to the military departments, the DSA would act as the central managing, directing, and coordinating agency for the Department of Defense in the prosecution of the military space program. 

In accordance with established schedules and priorities, the military departments would carry out the development of space systems, projects, and scientific investigations as assigned by DSA. DSA would also act as the focal point in DOD for the placing of requirements by the OSM and NÅSA upon the unique capabilities of the military departments to support civilian and scientific areas of the national space program again commensurate with the requirements of national defense.

Within approved policies and programs and in consonance with pertinent DOD-NASA agreements, the DSA would act as the central coordinating agency for the DOD on matters pertaining to the technical and operational support of nonmilitary scientific and civilian space programs as delineated by Director, OSM. Primary liaison contact in NASA would be through the Joint Division of Military 

Applications (JDMA). 

To close the loop, it is proposed that a Defense Space Support Agency (DSSA) be established under the Joint Chiefs of Staff. It would perform in the space field somewhat like the Defense Atomic Support Agency. The Agency would be responsible to the Secretary of Defense through the Joint Chiefs of Staff for planning and conducting worldwide military space operations. It would be responsive to the requirements for operational support of approved military and civilian scientific and developmental space programs and projects. 

The primary functions of the DSSA would be to exercise for the JCS the control of operating forces assigned to support the national space effort. The Agency would determine unit assignment and formulate task force elements for worldwide military operational support and conduct all military satellite and manned space vehicle operations for the DSA. The Agency would, as directed by DSA, plan and execute military support operations of an R. & D. or scientific nature assigned by the Director, Office of Space Management to the Department of Defense. The latter includes planning and executing military operations in support of approved NASA programs as directed by higher authority. Primary contact in NASA would be through the Joint Division of Military Applications. 

In summary, when considering the problems involved in management of the national space program outlined in this study, it seems appropriate to consider certain changes in the national organization for space. National goals must be reaffirmed and a national militarycivilian space and astronautics program formulated, authoritatively coordinated, and managed. In prosecuting the program, it must be recognized that a sense of urgency must motivate the efforts of the United States if we are to win the technological and ideological contest with the Soviets. The people of the United States must recognize that national prestige is at stake. There is no prize for coming in second.
