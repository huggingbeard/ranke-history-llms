INTRODUCTION 

The Divine Comedy is truly the "great poem in whose production "11 both heaven and earth have had a hand.

In it we find a complete philosophy which embraces all creation, a deep insight into human nature, a study of man in relation to both his terrestrial and his heavenly end, and a clear exposition of the great truths of theology. 

It was written with an ethical purpose, in order to show mankind how to achieve happiness, both in this life and in the life to come It shows us the horrible consequences of sin, which has robbed us of the perfection of our nature, and then goes on to show us how we may reconstitute this corrupt nature, regain the life of Eden, and finally reach our real blessedness in the Heaven of Light and Love.

Dante was a profound student of all the knowledge of his age. 

His knowledge of the books available was almost encyclopedic. He was equally familiar with the Scriptures and the works of the Christian Fathers, and with the works of the great pagan authors known to his contemporaries. His writings are full of references to both Christian and pagan authorities. In fact, he likes to put such references side by side, almost as if he gave equal weight to works inspired by divine revelation and those produced by the human reason alone .3 1. Paradiso XXV, 1-3

Moreover, in the Divine Comedy he makes constant use of implicit references, single words which are meant to suggest to the reader the full statement of some basic philosophical doctrine. Unfortunately for us, we often lose the exact meaning of the word because we are not familiar with the authority to whom he is referring. As a result, we often find the poem hard to understand and accuse Dante of being obscure. 

This is especially true in the case of the Paradiso, which more than any other part embodies the basic doctrines of scholastic philosophy.

The Concept of Nature 

The concept of nature is the foundation on which a great many other concepts are based. Since nature is one of the efficient causes of objects, it must be considered in relation to the other efficient causes and to its final cause, which is God. Since it is rooted in the stars, we must consider its astrological implications. 

Since man is the highest species in the natural order, we must include him in the study, and this involves the fields of ethics and politics. In fact, the study of the concept of nature might well be called an epitome of the philosophy of the Middle Ages. 

The "rediscovery" of Aristotle in the thirteenth century made very fundamental changes in the medieval ideas about nature. At first the Church condemned his physics and psychology, which seemed almost blasphemous in some ways. Up to that time, nature was considered worthy of study only in so far as she suggested a reality beyond herself. She was really just a system of symbols. 

God revealed Himself in Scripture in analogies and metaphors so that man's limited and imperfect intellect could grasp what He is. 

To understand these analogies and metaphors, one had to understand the symbols of nature. Consequently the only real reason for studying nature was that the better man understood nature, the better he understood Scripture, and the better he understood Scripture, the better he understood God. Thus, for St. Augustine philosophy was 9.

just the use of the intellect to decipher Scripture by the aid of nature. Man, of course, was something apart from and above nature, having a special destiny beyond this world. Grace was given to him so that he might destroy this nature and so be able to pursue his true life.

It is no wonder that the Church was shocked by the works of a man who taught that man and his moral, social, intellectual and spiritual activities are all natural phenomena and can be studied as such. However, far-sighted men like Albertus Magnus and St. 

Thomas saw the superiority of the Aristotelian physics and knew that it would eventually win out. As a result, they worked to bring these new ideas into relation with the faith, to make a synthesis that would combine the world of nature with the world above. Upon the recognition of Aristotle as the supreme authority in the field of natural phenomena, and the acceptance of the Thomistic synthesis, nature was rediscovered as an object of study for itself. 

It was no longer a divine language containing a direct message from God; it was the handiwork of God and showed traces of the Craftsman 10 who made it. The body was no longer something evil that kept man from reaching God. Man had a higher destiny beyond this life, but his body was prerequisite to the exercise of his higher activities, the activities which enabled him to reach his higher end. Grace no longer destroyed nature. Now God used nature as an instrument in the perfection of human souls.

The Four Causes 

Matter and form do not, however, explain how a certain object has come to be what it is. A complete explanation can be made only 33 in terms of the four causes. The four causes are what are responsible for the existence of things. When we see something for the first time, we ask what it is; and we can answer the question in several ways. Of a horseshce, for example, we say that it is made of iron, that it is formed in a certain way, that it was made by 34 a smith, and that it was made to protect the hoof of a horse. 

These are respectively the material, formal, efficient, and final causes of the horseshow.

Taken together they give a complete explanation of just what a horseshoe is. Each cause makes a distinct contribution to the effect which all four produce. The material cause is that in which occurs the motion which results in the production of the effect; the formal cause is the line of development followed; the efficient cause is the initiator of the movement; and the final cause is the goal to which the motion is directed. In speaking of the flight of an arrow, we can call the arrow itself the material cause, the line of flight the formal cause, the bowstring the efficient cause, and the target at which the arrow was aimed the final cause.

The Source of Motion 

Now we are prepared to see more clearly just what is this inner principle of motion which is the nature of a natural object. Both matter and form can lay claim to the title. Matter can be called the inner principle of motion in so far as it possesses a definite potentiality for a specific kind of motion. It is the passive inner principle. However, only form is the genuine active principle of motion. We have seen that the efficient, formal, and final causes coincide, and that the efficient cause is the source of the motion. Moreover, it is form which determines what kind of thing a substance is, and the substance is really itself only when it has attained its full actuality by the reception of a specific form. The Order of the Universe 

Each

The great multiplicity of natural things all engaged in their loving motions toward their ends or proper places makes up the natural order. This natural order is the form of the universe. member of the order has a specific nature which is the source of its motion toward a specific end or perfection. The natural appetite or love which each possesses for this end causes the natural processes of the world.

This multiplicity is made possible by the existence of creatures of various degrees of perfection. Each degree or form has a part to fill in the ladder of forms which makes the form of the universe, and exists for the sake of this ladder. Each form actualizes certain potentialities of the form below it and is itself in potentiality for the higher form above it. This does not mean that there is any evolution of one form from another. Each species represents a real cleft in nature. The ladder is just a constant ascending movement of the whole world of nature from the potential to the

Since man could never humble himself as far as he had exalted himself in disobeying God, God Himself took on the form of a man, and as a man who outweighed all other men, took the punishment for all and so provided for their return to God. By this means God made Himself more comprehensible to man and gave him grace which makes his acts meritorious in God's sight.

Summary 

We have seen that nature is the inner principle of motion in natural things, and that this motion is the result of the love which everything has for its proper place. These natural things, all seeking the places where they are most fully actualized, make up the order of the universe. This order has as its end God, Who originally planned it and created it through the instrumentality of nature. 

Man broke the order when his nature became corrupted and lost its natural motion. However, by grace operating through the church and the empire, the order will be restored when man's nature is fully reconstituted in this life and he will again be capable of linking the higher and the lower realms.
