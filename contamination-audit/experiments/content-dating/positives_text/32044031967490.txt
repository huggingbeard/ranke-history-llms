FLORIDA GAME AND FRESH WATER FISH COMMISSION AMENDMENT JOHN M. DeGROVE ASSISTANT PROFESSOR OF POLITICAL SCIENCE UNIVERSITY OF FLORIDA 

The amendment relating to the management a Florida's vital game and fish resources propose a number of major changes in the present arrange ment under Article IV, Section 30. These change have important implications in regard to th power of the governor, the cabinet, the legisla ture, and the Game and Fresh Water Fish Commi sion itself to manage Florida's fish and gam resources in the public interest. 

At present, Florida is one of at least 31 state that manages its game and fish resources throug an independent commission. The Florida Gam and Fresh Water Fish Commission is an independ ent agency drawing its powers and responsibilitie directly from the state constitution. The independ ent status of the commission is further enhance by: (a) a source of funds not subject to the con trol of the legislature, comprised of the variou license fees and other "funds resulting from th operation of the Commission"; (b) a constitu tional provision that "the legislature may enac any laws in aid of, but not inconsistent with, th provisions of this amendment"; and (c) a re quirement that board members, appointed by th governor with the consent of the Senate, b named for five-year, staggered terms, making unlikely that any one governor could completel replace the commission with his own appointee The history of how Florida came to adopt a approach involving such a large degree of inde pendence from legislative control over the opera tions of its game and fish commission bears d rectly on the present proposal to alter the existin arrangements.

Historically, the administration of game an fish resources, including those of Florida, hav been subjected to strong pressures from interes groups. The pre-1943 situation in Florida feature a form of legislative interference that made th state's game and fish laws virtually unenforceable Through hundreds of so-called "local bills," specia acts of the legislature provided for separate hun ing seasons and bag limits for the various countie 2

so that no uniform practice in setting such rules and regulations was possible. This procedure created a situation that has been described as "intolerable," since it allowed efforts to develop and control the state's game and fish resources to be bogged down by constant political hauling and tugging at each session of the legislature. 

It was within the framework of this unsatisfactory situation that the 1941 legislature proposed a constitutional amendment, approved in the general election in 1942, that set up the present Commission. This amendment provided for perhaps the broadest grant of power not subject to the control of the legislature ever made to a state agency. The amendment was supported by the organized efforts of conservation groups all over the state who argued that, given the political facts of life, no adequate management of fish and game resources could be accomplished unless the control of the legislature was virtually eliminated. The approach taken in Florida was not an unusual one in states that have failed to develop adequate policy and administrative arrangements for particular function. It has been the pattern (in fields such as health as well as in the natural reources area) for strong supporting interests to eek at least a minimum degree of independence from the legislature or governor so that a satisfactory program could be worked out. The unusually strong degree of independence achieved in Florida beginning January 1, 1943, was simply a reflection of the extreme confusion under the "local bill" approach.

CHANGES UNDER THE NEW PROPOSAL 

The changes that would be provided for should the constitutional amendment be adopted can be highlighted by comparing the present arrangement with the proposed change in those areas where an important shift is involved. 

Method of Appointing Commissioners 

Present: By the Governor with the consent of the Senate.

Proposed: By the Cabinet. 

Fiscal Independence from the Legislature

Present: Virtually complete, in that most operating funds from state sources come from licenses, fees, etc., and are not subject to any control by the legislature. The legisla ture may appropriate funds over and above this amount. 3

Proposed: Independence would be considerably limite 

While all proceeds from hunting and fishing licenses an other such sources would continue to go into a Stat Game Fund to be used only for carrying out the pro sions of the act, provision is also made that "the Commi sion," as a state agency, shall submit a budget estimate the amounts needed in the operation_of_the_Commissio for approval by the legislature through the Budget Con mission as provided by general law. 

General Independence from the Legislature

Present: Virtually complete, because in addition to th fiscal independence noted above, the constitution provide that "the Legislature may enact laws in aid of, but n inconsistent with, the provisions of this amendment • · • Proposed: No significant difference other than the fisc controls. The section barring the legislature from enactin laws inconsistent with the constitutional powers and dutie of the Commission is retained. A section specifically en powering the commission to control such matters as ba limits, hunting seasons, and methods of taking and tran porting fish and game is also retained. On the other hand the introduction of budget review power by the legislatur does give that body added supervisory powers over th 

Game Commission. 

PROS AND CONS 

The arguments for and against the propose amendment bringing about the important change noted above may be summarized as follows: 

For the Amendment 

1. Democratic government demands that n agency be for all practical purposes beyond th control of both the legislature and the executiv branch. 

2. The amendment does not seriously threaten the financial independence of the Game an 

Fresh Water Fish Commission, since all fund received from operations under the amende constitutional provision would still go into 

State Game Fund and could only be used t further the goals set forth in the amendment. 

3. A sound fiscal program and general ac countability demands that the Game and Fish

Commission subject its projected spending pro gram to the Budget Commission and the legis lature for review. Since all other agencies hav this responsibility, there is no logical reason why the Game and Fresh Water Fish Commis sion should not be included. The argument tha the legislature might reinject partisan politica considerations back into the management of the state's game and fish resources assumes tha the legislature is not capable of acting in the

public interest on this matter. Such an assumption is unsound. 

4. The democratic process is further strengthened by the provision that the Commission be appointed by the entire seven-man Cabinet rather than by the Governor alone. It is better to have executive responsibility shared among the members of the cabinet since this procedure avoids the concentration of too much power in the hands of the Governor. 

Against the Amendment 

1. The political conditions that led to a strongly independent status for the Game and Fresh Water Commission in the first place have not changed substantially since 1943. Government by local bill is still the rule in many fields, and might well return in the area of fish and game management if the Commission's independent status is weakened. 

2. Under the present arrangement, an expert, professionally trained staff of wildlife and fish biologists, as well as other professionally trained individuals, has replaced the patronage-oriented, often untrained personnel force that characterized the pre-1943 period. Because Florida still has no general service-wide merit system, (the present plan includes something less than onehalf of all state employees) there is a danger that the old approach in hiring personnel would be reinstated under the proposed amendment. 

3. The record of the Game and Fresh Water 

Commission since 1943 in developing an effective program of game and fish management is an excellent one. The changes brought about since 1943 have caused one student of fish and game management in the state to remark that the amendment taking effect in 1943 "was probably the greatest single advance which has been made in the administration of Florida's wildlife resources." The independent status of the 

Commission has not led to irresponsible behavior on the contrary, it has removed the protection and management of Florida's wildlife from a chaotic political situation and made an effective, positive program of management possible.

Hubert Marshall and Robert J. Young, Public Administration of Florida's Natural Resources (Gainesville, Florida:

University of Florida Public Administration Clearing Serv ice, 1953). Ch. 5.

4. The new fiscal controls would open the door to a return of legislative domination of the Commission, because the power to review a budget implies the power to reject. 

From the viewpoint of the student of govern mental organization, neither the existing prac tice nor the proposed change corresponds to what has generally been agreed to as "best practice" in recent years-the consolidation of all natural resource functions into a single de partment, headed by a director appointed by the Governor. Such an arrangement must, of course assume a comprehensive, effective merit ar rangement for staffing agencies. Insofar as the proposed change weakens the hand of the Gov ernor by rescinding his power to name the Com mission, it might be viewed as a step backward The budgetary controls provided for might be considered a step forward. In any case, the amendment cannot be described by the student of government as a major step in conforming to best practices.

CONCLUSION 

Florida's efforts to manage properly its wildlife resources were markedly unsuccessful during the period to 1943, for reasons noted above. The pattern followed in Florida under which conser vation interests banded together and succeeded in getting their program "out of politics" is a familiar one that has been carried out in a large number of states across the nation. The Florida practice differed in that the unusally poor prac tices existing under the pre-1943 conditions re sulted in the pendulum swinging very sharply in the other direction to establish a constitutionally based Commission with virtually autonomou powers. The record of accomplishment under tha commission, though far from perfect, has been a good one. The proposed amendment to be voted on in November 1960 would maintain the con stitutionally-based independent commission, bu its autonomous character would be limited in the manner outlined above. It seems clear that both in its immediate and potential effect the pro posed amendment would indeed re-establish a least some legislative control over the Game an Fresh Water Fish Commission. It is up to the voters of the state to decide for themselves whethe the political framework for game and fish manage ment has progressed to the point where full in

dependence for the wildlife management function is no longer necessary to an effective management program.

DISTRIBUTION OF STATE RACING REVENUE TO COUNTIES WYLIE KILPATRICK, RESEARCH PROFESSOR BUREAU OF ECONOMIC AND BUSINESS RESEARCH 

The proposed state constitutional amendment to Section 15 of Article IX regarding state racing revenue will, if adopted, freeze into the constitution the recent distributive practice by providing that "not less than 45 percent of all excise taxes levied and collected by the state, or under its authority, from the operations of pari-mutuel pools shall be allocated and distributed in equal parts to the several counties of the state". The term “excise taxes" is likely to be interpreted to mean not only the several percentage taxes on pari-mutuel pools, which furnish the bulk of racing revenue, but also the revenue from race track admissions, licenses, and other minor sources.

Since 1955-56, the county share of racing revenue has been slightly above 45 percent of the total yield as a result of the 1955 law enacting a 2 percent additional tax on pools at dog tracks. In the years immediately preceding, the county share hovered around 41 percent of the total revenue. 

Instead of putting a ceiling on the county share, the amendment puts a floor, which later could be raised in legislative discretion, under the county share.

RECORD OF COLLECTION AND DISTRIBUTION 

The key purpose of equal division of the county share of state racing revenue dates back, of course, to the original 1931 tax of 3 percent on parimutuels and 15 percent on admissions. Passage of the law legalizing racing gambling was possible only on the consideration that the entire tax yield would be distributed equally among the 67 counties. Later amendments increased the rates differently for horse and dog tracks, added frontons to the tax base, and regulated "breaks" (odd cents above the nearest multiple of 5 for horse bets, and of 10 for dog and fronton bets).

As classes of revenue multipled, the disposition of the revenue varied between complete allocation to counties, complete retention by the state, and a percentage sharing of revenue between the two levels. As a result, the county share is derived

from the mentioned 1931 law, 50 percent of the additional 4 percent tax on dog tracks, and the allocation to counties of a share of the "breaks" and of all or most of the licenses. The state share is derived from the additional 5 percent tax on horse track pools, one-half of the additional 4 percent tax on dog tracks, 2 percent of fronton pools, and a large part of the "breaks”. 2 

The importance to counties of racing revenue is revealed by the growth of the amount received by each county from $9,226 in 1931-32, the first distribution, to $33,100 a decade later, $112,000 two decades later, and $178,300 in 1958-59, the latest distribution. The increasing size of the dis tribution points both to the additional racing taxes levied since 1931 and to the increasing popularity of racing gambling both with tourists and Floridians. During this period, the size of the state government has grown at an even faster rate. Consequently, racing taxes as a percentage of total state taxes shrank from 9.6 percent in 1947 

48 to 4.9 percent in 1958-59; and the distribution of racing taxes to counties declined between the same years from 4 percent to 2.3 percent of state taxes. The county distribution, therefore, is to be judged not so much as a drain upon state taxes, since the recent county proportion has been so small, as upon the desirability of the distri butive plan.

SCHOOL AND OTHER AIDS 

Racing revenue distribution to schools invites emphasis because the schools are the recipients of the Minimum Foundation Program and other aid for equalizing minimum costs among rich and poor counties alike. In 1958, the full amount of county racing revenue was alloted to schools in four counties-Broward, Hillsborough, Escambia, and Sarasota-and in six other counties the larg est part of racing revenue was similarly alloted. 

Interestingly enough, six of these ten counties were among the larger and wealthier counties better able to support schools than many counties in which schools received a smaller part of the racing revenue. The opposite outcome, failure to allot schools any racing revenue, occurred in five of the richer counties-Collier, Duval, Orange, Palm Beach, and Polk.

More important was the outcome in the counties small in population and restricted in resources. 

School racing revenue in 1958 was larger than the required county contribution to the Minimum

Foundation Program in as many as 30 counties, Since racing revenue is classified as local reve nue, even though in fact from the state, the county schools can make the required matching from taxes paid by race tracks often hundreds of miles away. Thus, Liberty schools received $100,000 from racing revenue as against a requirement of $14,505, and Levy schools were alloted $159,000, of which $63,907 was a required county contri bution.

The significance of this comparison should not be exaggerated because all counties raise more local funds, at times much more, than they are required to contribute to the minimum school program. A better comparison is afforded by the excess of school racing revenue over the school property tax in nine counties. For example, Union received $85,500 in racing revenue and collected $12,482 in school property taxes. Wakulla's school racing revenue was $127,500 as against $38,996 for school property taxes. In another nine counties, school racing revenue approached, in amount, the school property tax. In the other 49 counties, school property taxes exceeded school racing revenues by amounts that at least were appreci able and often large.

State school aid of $167,325,296 to schools, in 

1958, dwarfed school racing revenue of $5,251,558, which was less than one-half of the federal school 16

aid of $11,674,511. Distribution of the Minimum

Foundation Program funds on the basis of relative need and ability attempts to provide equity by making disproportionately large payments to small counties at the same time that the big counties receive substantial payments. Even in the small counties, state school aid over-shadows racing revenue. Thus, school aid was $217,430 in Union and $384,076 in Wakulla in contrast, respectively, to school racing revenue of $85,000 and $127,500. Although the state distribution of gasoline and motor fuel taxes to counties is not so explicitly on an equalizing basis as is school aid, the effect is to equalize by much larger highway aid, in relation to personal income and population, in small counties than in large ones. 

State school and highway aids are not a substitute for racing revenue but suggest that indexes of comparative need and ability be used to assure fairness in the racing distribution, especially to the small counties now suffering discrimination in that distribution.

Since Florida has accepted the policy of a special racing distribution to aid small counties, in addition to the regular functional aids, the central questions regarding the racing revenue amendment to be voted on in November 8, 1960, are whether the present distributive plan is satisfactory and whether it should be incorporated into the constitution. The most plausible argument to vote "yes" is that the avowed purpose of the present plan to aid small counties is carried out if the test is a contrast between big and small counties. Big counties are not injured because their racing revenue, though a minor source, is not unwelcome. The state treasury is not injured because the proportion of state taxes taken for the racing distribution is so small as not to jeopardize other state programs. If racing revenue was withheld from counties, the state probably would be forced to substitute replacement revenue for counties. To insert the present plan into the constitution, so the favorable argument goes, merely safeguards a plan that is likely to be continued in any event. 

The argument to vote "no" is that the present plan is only superficially fair among counties.

ABSENTEE REGISTRATION FOR MILITARY PERSONNEL House Joint Resolution No. 813 proposes to liberalize the constitutional requirements with respect to registration for voting (Article VI, Sec 

2) by permitting the legislature to provide for absentee registration of members of the armed forces and their spouses who live outside the state. This amendment would place Florida in line with a vast majority of states (47) in permit ting absentee registration of members of the armed forces. A safeguard the legislature can adopt would be the requirement that registration in person be done upon completion of military service and return to the state if permanent regi stration as the same address is to continue unimpaired. The legislature in 1955 provided by statute for absentee registration of military personnel and their spouses (Fla. Stat., Ann. 101.692), but it did not include this safeguarding provision Without the proposed amendment, the current statute has no effect as absentee registration must be specifically authorized.

So long as the federal government requires military service of able-bodied males at just about the time most of them reach voting age, there will be a need in the states to permit absentee registration of such selectees or enlistees and their spouses. Furthermore, it would be the rankest kind of discrimination to continue to exclude from 22

one of the most precious rights of citizenshipthe ballot— those young persons absent from the state through no choice of their own and never able to acquire voting residence in another state by not extending to them any means to register. 

To ignore the continuing military demands the government of the United States is compelled to make upon its citizens in the present posture of international relations—with no end in sight for such demands-is scarcely a realistic or equitable attitude for the states to take toward their own citizens. The legislature can undertake proper safeguards against any possibility of fraud under such absentee registration by requiring countersignature of commanding officers for completion of absentee registration and by requiring absentee registrants to re-register in person upon release from the armed services and return to Florida. 

Against this amendment can be argued the difficulty of detecting registration by one person for another in the complete absence of face-toface confrontation as a part of the registration process. However, as pointed out above, this can be averted by the safeguards indicated that can be written into any changes in the registration law which the legislature would enact under the authorization of this amendment.

ADDITIONAL JUDGES FOR DISTRICT COURTS OF APPEAL The amendment to Article V, Section 5, Sub sections (1) and (2) on the Judiciary would permit the legislature to add two additional judges to the bench of any District Court of Appeal. 

Three judges, however, must constitute a panel for each case, with a majority opinion required for decision. In other words, additional judges over and above the required three cannot be placed on a panel in order to influence the results of a case, but the additional judges which the legislature may add to any district appellate bench will permit more panels of three judges to be constituted and a greater volume of cases to be handled more expeditiously. 

The facts back of this amendment are that very heavy dockets have rapidly built up in the Second and Third Appellate Districts in South Florida.

District appellate judges in these districts are handling from six hundred to seven hundred 23

cases per year, which is an impossibly heavy case load. An excessive case load always means delay to the litigants and in the execution of the law in which all citizens have a stake. If delay is to b shortened in hearing and deciding cases on ap peal in these districts, more judges must be added as three judges cannot adequately handle tha many appellate cases with promptitude and depth of consideration.

The District Courts of Appeal, which were created in 1957, would not have their structur affected by this amendment as the amendmen merely permits an addition of two judges to these courts. Furthermore, this amendment does not affect the structure of the lower courts which i now under study by the Judicial Council. 

This amendment originated in the Judicia

Council, and there was no controversy over i either inside or outside the Legislature. In other words, there is no case against it, and it it dif ficult to see how reasonable individuals could oppose it.

AMENDMENTS OF LOCAL APPLICATION VOLUSIA AND HIGHLAND COUNTIES House Joint Resolution No. 409 proposes an amendment to Article XVI granting permission to the legislature to allow civil jury trials to take place in municipalities other than the county seat in Volusia and Highlands counties. Permission is also granted by this amendment to the legislature to allow the clerk of any court or any other court officer in the county to establish an office within a municipality where such trials may be held. The limitation on maintenance of such an office is that it must be in addition to the principal office of the court officer which must remain at the county seat.

It is significant that up to now only Pinellas County has gained the constitutional privilege of having trials of civil suits by juries take place in municipalities other than the county seat but with the safeguard that such municipalities must have at least 75,000 population. The proposed amend ment has no such safeguard as to size of communi ties for branch court houses in Volusia and High land Counties. It merely provides that municipali 24

ies must be designated for this purpose in Volusia

County by the circuit judge of the Seventh Judi cial District and in Highlands County by the circuit judge of the Tenth Judicial District. 

The arguments for this amendment simply rest apon the concept of permitting flexibility at the discretion of the circuit judges for place of civil jury trials in populous counties with a number of municipalities. The convenience of the litigating public, counsel, and citizens drawn for jury panels becomes a factor for consideration. Possibly no good reason could be offered against permitting circuit judges sufficient discretion to determine when and where such trial places shall be established in their circuits. It is also entirely possible that in some minor civil cases it might be easier to obtain witnesses if there were assurance that attendance at court did not entail going all the way to the county seat in another town. 

On the other hand, opponents can argue that detail of alternative trial places in two particular counties, to the exclusion of others, does not properly belong in a state constitution. Furthermore, opponents may object that there is no certainty as to trial locations under this amendment; trials might be in the smallest as well as in the largest towns, first in one town and then in another as whim would strike judges. Moreover, attorneys coming from other counties to participate in trials would be confused as to the exact location for the trial unless clearly notified in advance. Furthermore, municipalities would be put to extra expense to provide suitable court rooms.

BREVARD COUNTY 

A rather similar permission is sought for Brevard County under House Joint Resolution No. 

1073, also to Article XVI, to the effect that civil jury trials may be held in any designated branch court houses in the county, as provided by law. All records of any civil trial conducted in any such branch court house are to be filed in the main court house at the county seat.

The same arguments pro and con as were set forth above in respect to the amendment covering 

Volusia and Highlands County apply to this amendment except that the argument against the previous amendment with respect to lack of cer- 25

tainty as to where trials might be held and lack of any population requirement at all for a town in which to hold trials applies even more force fully to the Brevard amendment. Theoretically, by special act the legislature could make a barn out in a field a branch court house. If the people of the state desire to do away with all system and reasonable expectancy as to the place, at least, for civil jury trials these two amendments are the way to do so. 26
