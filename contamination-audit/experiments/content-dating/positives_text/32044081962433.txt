Foreword 

ANY friends in business and academic circles have expressed sufficient interest in my occasional writings to encourage me to have printed in the following pages a recent letter to my daughter, a senior at Smith College majoring in economics. It has to do with some aspects of price making and the uses of cost information. As is fitting from a fond father to one entirely competent in skeptical appraisal of parental wisdom, the letter is written with as much care as the time available at its date permitted. Though compressed, its contents are matters of mature reflection, and some of them of long personal experience. It is printed substantially as in the original, except that the footnotes have been added.

The justification for making this material available in this way lies in my supposition that it presents some new considerations in the economics of prices, specifically concerning the nature of price units and two little noticed functions of price schedules; that it discusses the limitations of cost data from the viewpoint of a general executive; and that it suggests one aspect of the "conflict of logics," an important element I believe in present social and political problems. When I say "new considerations" friends will add "so far as I know," for it is understood, of course, that I cannot pretend to scholar- 3

ship. Even with this disclaimer I cannot altogether avoid a feeling of uneasiness, having read J. R. McCulloh's dictum ("The Literature of Political Economy" quoted in Arthur 

W. Marget's "The Theory of Prices" Vol. I, vii); "Whether a writer or speaker undertakes to unfold principles, to set them in a novel and more striking light, or to recommend their application, he should know what has been already undertaken, what has been accomplished, and what remains for discovery and elucidation."

Despite this caution and my scanty research, I venture to believe that economic theory as yet does not succeed in stating approximately the principles of present day prices, is misleading as to the behavior of business men in the construction of price schedules, and misapprehends the significance of cost data in connection therewith. This, of course, may not be a reasonable and valid criticism, but at any rate it may be useful to reveal the attitudes and understanding of one business man or manager of business operations. It appears to me one of the important difficulties of economists that so large a proportion of business men do not express their knowledge and judgment in terms suitable to the larger frame of thought of economics. On the other hand, specialization of a career in one of the branches of economic science does not easily provide intimate responsible experience in the operations to which a special field relates. Without such experience the behavior of any one of the numerous species of business man is easily interpreted in terms not in fact relevant to their conduct. My observation of business men of unquestioned and generally recognized ability has shown me that perhaps nine out of ten usually express what they are doing or thinking in language definitely misleading or false except to one knowing the particular techniques 4

and jargon, the "situation," and even the personalities, of the "shop" concerned.

These difficulties are of more than academic interest. They are in part at the root of the attempts at regulation of business operations and at the control of business executives simultaneously from several different points of view by various authorities and systems of regulation chiefly based on negative principles. It is tempting to discuss some of the possible consequences of this situation in the new day of formal integration of productive economic operations with the broader planning of a society conscious of itself and impatient of its faults. But it is better to let it go merely with the suggestion that the new day requires that any economic study of wide field be also sociological. Economics has become again, perhaps in a new sense, “political economy."

Dear Frances: 

When you were here during the Christmas vacation we talked about several economic questions without time to discuss them sufficiently. 

In any event they are so difficult that I could not express an opinion adequately without considerable deliberation; and I told you I would attempt to write you something later. Here are my answers which make a long story and even so are too much abbreviated, I fear. 

Your questions may be placed in two general groups as follows: I. What is the nature of systems of rates or price classification involving what you called "discrimination on the basis of what the traffic will bear?" 

Are they due to monopoly or to the joint use of fixed plant, and do they obtain in competitive or only in monopolistic operations? II. Is there such a thing as marginal costs? What is the significance of variations in variable costs?

So here we go, right off the deep end into the highbrow side of a practical world. 

a. Units of Purchase and Sale I. 

I should begin by discussing the nature and function of the units of price, a subject quite unorthodox since not even the mathematical economists have studied it so far as I have observed. 

A unit of price is an arbitrarily or conventionally established abstract measurement of a commodity or service. Usually, when commodities are involved, this unit of measurement is lineal, or in terms of gravity, or of cubic content or by count of discrete items, (e.g., dozen eggs). The economic unit is not defined until the commodity to which it is applied is named-for example, a “pound" is not a unit of price or sale, but a "pound of butter" is. Thus what is so often thought of as a strictly quantitative unit is as a matter of fact highly qualitative. The quality aspects are usually not explicit but are implicit in the name "butter" for example. Now, note well that the qualities connoted by the name "butter" are of an entirely different order for the producer of butter than for the consumer of butter, a fact of which either may be unaware. "Butter" for the producer spells expenditure in terms of land, cattle, fodder, separators, labor, trucks, silos, labor problems, personnel, etc.; also processes and conditions of technological rather than of economic interest. On the other hand, for the consumer "butter" connotes uses

in cooking and eating; also practices of social rather than economic interest. Thus the unit “pound of butter" from the point of view of trade is only at most an abstraction of a few elements-weight, color, consistency, smell, taste, perhaps chemical characteristics of something which is concrete in two radically different systems, one of production, the other of consumption.

Any such composite abstraction is a construction. In the case which 

I shall cite later, and which is not rare, the construction of a unit of price is deliberately and consciously made; but most of the units of price with which we are all familiar in competitive markets for stable products were handed to us with the rest of our vocabulary, so that we are neither aware of their arbitrary or conventional character nor of the nature of their functions in exchange. 

The principal function of a unit of price, which I shall henceforth call a "unit of purchase and sale" or more simply "unit of sale," is to serve as a common basis of the diverse calculations which both producers and consumers each must make in order to agree upon an exchange. 

It needs but little reflection to see that each party starting with this arbitrary unit proceeds to entirely different types of calculation or appraisal. 

Butter for example is used not in pounds, but in pats and spoonfuls.

Its appraisal, quite aside from the matter of taste, requires estimates of time, refrigerator space, number of persons to be served, etc., etc. The producer calculates in entirely different terms; and frequently, if not usually, uses entirely different units in production. Thus the unit of purchase and sale must be reasonably and stably adapted to two different types of calculations and two different groups of calculators simultaneously. It follows that if either conditions of demand or those of production radically change, a customary unit of sale may not be a mutually satisfactory starting point of calculations. This may be observed to have occurred when new units of sale are devised, which is not rare. 

Since I was a boy the units of sale of many common things have been changed; and an important part of commercial skill-I believe it is a valuable art-consists in adopting, devising or inventing units of purchase and sale suitable to the satisfactory calculation of utilities and values on one hand and the efficient calculation and planning of production elements, processes and costs on the other. 

Sometimes these changes are desirable because of an altered character of the uses of a product, sometimes because of new production processes or conditions. At other times they are required by the effect upon exchange money value of increased demand or of increased scarcity of

production elements; for, broadly, the higher the exchange value the more desirable a smaller unit of sale-other things, especially the general value of money, being equal. If the money value of a product increases greatly, then much greater efficiency either in consumption or production or both is in order; hence, a refinement of units of sale is convenient for both user and producer as exchange values rise. For example, common pins sell by the packet, gold pins by the piece. If platinum was as common as copper (as easy to produce or secure) it might sell by the pound, (not the ounce or gram); but if demand then was no greater than at present for platinum, it might possibly sell by the cwt.; and if much greater than at present for copper, would probably sell by different units (cwt. wholesale, pound for larger consumers, ounce for smaller consumers).

Thus a unit of purchase and sale is a resultant of (a) the quantity demand; (b) the distribution of demand; (c) the degree of elasticity of demand; (d) the nature of the uses; (e) the magnitude of exchange money value; (f) the scarcity of production elements; (g) the nature of the productive process; (h) the elasticity of production. (Not an exhaustive list.) Conversely, the character of the unit of sale reacts to some extent and sometimes substantially upon the uses of commodities, the distribution of demand, the extent to which producers compete, and the character of the production process. 

I should point out to you that the adoption of a small unit of sale instead of a large one, where the kind of measurement involved is the same, say gravity, is a radical change from the economic point of view, even though not significant from the abstract physical point of view. 

Thus, a ton is in the abstract 2,000 times one pound; but a sales unit of one ton of a given commodity is not equivalent economically to 2,000 times a sales unit of one pound of it, not even when the charge for a ton is 2,000 times the charge for one pound. I assume that your readings in the theory of utility-concerning the diminishing utility to the consumer of equal increments of a commodity-and of your study of the effect of increasing supply upon unit exchange value, make it evident that a reduction of the size of a unit of sale radically changes the character of a market, and the economy of uses and of production.

Origins of Price Patterns

Let us pass on to a second and more complex stage of the argument. 

How are units of sale changed? Some obvious and simple ways I have already indicated. Pecks are changed to pounds; tons divided in pounds; pounds are changed to packages, etc. But although such changes have been common, they may not be the most important and are not to me the most interesting. Refinement of definitions of units of sale is a characteristic development of the modern economy. Its most simple form is that of grading the product (including classification by size, weight, etc.). Very often the grading is attended by a new name, so that it is not easy to recognize that it is the same old thing now sold under two or more names with differences, as "cream" and "milk." But often also it is named by grades or sizes-as A, B, C, etc., now applied to milk, 10

PRICE SYSTEMS AND RELATED RELATED MATTERS or No. 1, No. 2, No. 3 

for wheat. A new name or a new grade usually means the adoption of at least two new units of sale of what was formerly a homogeneous product from the standpoint of exchange. 

How does this come about? Sometimes, no doubt, the producer is the initiating factor; but probably more frequently the consumers are the field of origin. When they begin to distinguish grades—e.g., better, poorer-from the point of view of utility, they offer more for the better, and less for the poorer grades. If they do so widely, persistently and consistently they force producers to recognize their discriminations, which become conventional, even if there is absolutely no distinction (rare case) from the producers' point of view. Is it not obvious that such discriminations force the producers' hands? If they do not agree, they will find much product left on their hands, unless they sell the remainder cheap; and if they sell it cheap they thereby have in effect agreed. In other words, producers are obliged to recognize a pattern of demand, because their customers establish it in very concrete terms of bidding up what they consider superior, even though the producers as such cannot make the distinction. (As in good and poor apples from the same tree.)

The converse-where the producer makes a distinction in product because of difference in the production process (or materials)—is not strictly converse. For if the consumer will not recognize the difference, the producer cannot force the consumer to recognize a production pattern, whereas the consumer always can force the producer to recognize a consumer pattern. But if the producer finds (or regards) a certain part of his product more costly in labor or materials than others, he will restrict its production (if he can) and increase thereby its scarcity. This itself may, within narrow limits, create a pattern of demand even if the distinction from the standpoint of utility is negligible. (I shall use the word "pattern" as the most conveniently suggestive term. It is used copiously nowadays by the "do gooders” and “God-savers” to quote or paraphrase Hugh Johnson, and by near scientists, would-be scientists, and pseudo scientists ad lib. But to any one who has actually constructed and worked with schedules of prices it is quite inadequate, chiefly because it is too static in its connotations.) 

Probably in most cases of importance there is an interaction between the pattern of demand and the pattern of production. What the effect of this interaction will be cannot be determined by reasoning on a priori assumptions. Indeed, it is an organic type of process that in principle is never ended. I can remember designing an early schedule of charges 11

for radio broadcasting (for WEAF early in 1922, I think) on the analogy of telephone rate schedules. Except as they served as a basis for acquiring experience, they were utterly useless. They neither corresponded then or later to production patterns nor to consumption patterns, where as an effective schedule must correspond to both. 

Whether the process starts with consumers or with producers, or evolves from both sides, a classification of products or services is primarily a refinement of units of sale; but when it is accomplished we have three patterns-one for consumption or demand, one for production, and the third for sales-a schedule of units of sale. I must note here rather summarily that the schedule of units of sale is not effective for action without the associated money quantities (I have already pointed out above the interaction between units and money quantities). 

c. Cost Factors in Patterned Price Making 

We are now in a better position to discuss the question of costs. 

Perhaps I should first point out the implications of classical theory, namely, that increase of demand is a factor in increasing both aggregate and average (unit) costs where isolated (non-classified) products are in question. This is true for the reason that increased production can normally be attained (given the state of the production art) only by the addition of marginal (high cost) production or through new marginal (high cost) producers. Thus there is nothing new in the idea that price is a determinant of costs. But this idea, instead of being a broad generalization for convenient exposition of economic theory, is absolutely fundamental in patterned or scheduled price making. A schedule by restricting demand, controls costs; and by restricting costs it conversely expands effective demand. Its function is to facilitate attainment of an equilibrium between revenue and costs. 

This function relates, however, primarily to the patterns as wholes, not to the specific sub-units of consumption, sale, and production. This is not necessarily due to the production side-joint costs, for example. 

It applies equally when the classification arises solely on the demand side and there is no possibility of making a corresponding differential on the production side, for example, in the case of better and poorer apples in a primitive state of horticulture, when the "better" and "poorer” are accidental or non-controllable in the production process. In this case the revenues are in part determined by the interaction between classes of demand on each other as well as upon price differentials. The revenues depend upon the pattern of consumption and the pattern of prices-both 12

patterns being largely interdependent-and costs are affected or are determined only in a long run way and then only as a whole. 

However, in most modern cases of importance, the jointness of costs is also a reason why the primary concern is with patterns of consumption, production and prices as wholes. I shall discuss the cost question in 

Section II of this letter. But here I should point out that while neither specific demand nor "specific" costs nor specific prices have independent status when units are refined by schedule making, this does not mean that correspondences between effective demands at specific values, prices, and correlated cost trends are unimportant. I suspect that this is an idea hard to get over without a rather sophisticating experience, for it is heaping relativity upon relativity-somewhat suggesting "the change in the rate of change of the rate of change” (“derivative functions" I think they are called) that bother the boys studying calculus. I shall try to illustrate as follows: 

Let the classification of the product be A, B, C, D, for which respectively the demand is a, b, c, d, units at prices respectively of P1, P2, P3 and P₁, and let the costs be the aggregate X. Now let the demand for 

A be increased from a to a + 10. Then the total revenues will increase by the difference between a (P,) and (a + 10) (P,). If the aggregate costs X are increased by y when the quantities are increased by 10A and y is more than 10 P₁, the demand as a whole will, at the price schedule as a whole, reduce the net proceeds of production as a whole. Thus the price P, for these conditions of demand and production does not properly coordinate the trends of the factors of demand and production. 

Nevertheless, if the price P, were changed without regard to the pattern of prices (P1, P2, P3, P4), both the pattern of consumption and the pattern of production might be radically changed, so that no more than a tentative suggestion, not a causal relation, is conveyed by the fact that y exceeds 10 P₁. 

d. Technological and Developmental Functions of Price Schedules 

There are two other functions of patterned price schedules especially to draw to your attention because they have an important bearing upon your questions and because very little study has been given to them. 

I recall scarcely a suggestion of them in the academic literature and they are rarely referred to even in indirect form in the literature of public utility rate regulation. They are both highly important in telephone rate making, but I believe the first is rather generally important and increasingly so in other kinds of price schedules, while the second 13

though less frequent is probably important in a number of instances. 

I shall call the first the technological function and the second the developmental or "regenerative" function.

Concerning the technological function: Often one of the vital functions of a pattern of prices is to protect or facilitate the technological production process itself. I could agree to disrupt telephone service, electric service, railroad service and many manufacturing operations by mere "carelessness" in the setting of differentials in prices or by letting amateurs make the schedules. The reason is not economic, but technical. 

It is that a complex mechanism, producing several classes of product jointly, cannot operate from the technical point of view except certain proportions be maintained. To give a specific example, it is impossible to give good telephone service as a whole if more than a certain proportion of the subscribers take four-party service regardless of the price paid or of the costs involved. (What proportion actually takes that class of rate in a given situation will depend chiefly not on the absolute price but the price relative to others in the schedule, i.e., those alternatively available.) Thus a major factor in modern price making is not economic in the restricted sense. It is not at least directly concerned with revenues, costs, profits, or aggregate sales or production. It is concerned primarily with the effectiveness of the production system as a system. 

Concerning the developmental or regenerative function of a price schedule: It is well understood, of course, that where there are several classes of product (units of sale) and prices are alternatively available, the price differentials will control the distribution of sales by classes. It is also often stated that "what the traffic will bear" means either increasing profits or widening markets at the expense of exorbitant charges for certain units of sale. I do not refer here to either of these functions (if and when the latter is a legitimate function) but to one which is related to and easily confused with them. In the practices just cited there is no element of creating utility to the consumer, but there are involved merely particular ways of treating the pattern of utility. The function which I am now to discuss is to create utility through price treatment. 

This is a fundamental function of telephone rate schedules. It depends upon the fact that the value of this as of some other services depends upon their comprehensiveness. The utility of the telephone is closely related to whether others can be reached by means of it. This was found out very early, in fact about as soon as the telephone was introduced. It compelled at once making low rates for some users and classes of use in order to create service for other classes. 14

In the telephone case just cited the development comes exclusively through increased utility, not through the effect of increased sales upon costs, since the business in most aspects is believed to be one of decreasing or at best constant returns. But the same principle applies in at least two other types of cases, one where the risk is reduced and the other where costs are reduced by creating more use and users. 

I would put insurance in the first category. It requires large volume to pool risks economically. Insurance on 100 lives would be quite hazardous and therefore costly, and the security of insurance would be doubtful. 

Insurance on 1,000,000 or 10,000,000 lives is not greatly hazardous in the aggregate, and large numbers here give security of contract, which is an important source of utility to the insured. This is definitely facilitated by the way insurance price patterns have been designed. I have in mind especially the "level premium" system of prices. 

Somewhat similar are all the cases where economy lies in mass production. In this type of case there is usually some directly increased utility of the individual units of sale (better quality, assurance of replacement of parts, for example) but the main point here, (taking the automobile as an example), is the increased utility (because of economy per unit) of the productive system as a whole to the community as a whole through a period of time, and the effect of this upon good roads, thus indirectly increasing the utility of cars to individuals. Automobiles long ago became a patterned price industry.

These three instances of what I like to call the "regenerative” function in schedule making, you will note, are quite diverse in character. I emphasize this because when I once talked to a group of economists about this function, the greater ease and assurance with which I could refer to telephone rates for illustration created the impression that I was talking about something peculiar to the telephone rate system. 

This, I think, is not so. e.. Answering Questions at last

Now we come down to some of your main questions: Is patterned or scheduled or "what the traffic will bear" price making ascribable to monopolies, or is it characteristic of competitive price making also? 

Perhaps you already agree with my view that patterned price making is at root a matter of refinement of units of sale based upon discriminations among consumers and the development of technologies permitting the multiplication of varieties, sizes and styles of products. Large scale joint production and highly capitalistic processes no doubt increase the need for and usefulness of patterned price making. Since most large monopolies 15

(except some patent and copyright monopolies) are of this character there is perhaps more apparent patterned price making in monopolies than otherwise; but it is so common in non-monopolistic and semimonopolistic operations that I think the monopoly condition is clearly not a fundamental factor. Moreover, both the technological and regenerative functions of price schedule are related to questions of technology rather than to type of industrial organization. 

In the monopolies the making of the pattern is a relatively deliberate conscious affair (in public service monopolies a very meticulous one) and is probably not so much so in competitive enterprises in general. 

In the latter the major competition, however, is often between patterns rather than between specific prices, and perhaps is usually and certainly increasingly so. I will cite two cases-life insurance which is highly competitive; and automobiles which is perhaps more monopolistic but still competitive. Both are businesses obviously operating with patterned prices, especially life insurance where the patterns are very complex. 

In the more generally competitive businesses, where there are larger numbers of independent producers, you will find much patterned price making. What is more important, you will observe that the aggregate of specializations of competitive production often presents, when regarded as a whole system, a combination of consumption-price-production patterns, determined by the interaction of numerous independent producers rather than as a whole deliberately constructed. I will not undertake to develop this further; but it would be an interesting field for investigation.

II. 

We now come to the matter of costs. It was not in Pandora's box, but should have been. Cost, as distinguished from expenditure, is a matter of definitions, which are largely established by convention for particular businesses; and also for particular purposes. I am a director of five companies in as many different lines of business. I repeatedly observe that the concepts of costs are radically different in them. Cost is also often different in some respects for the income tax collector's purposes than it is for other purposes. I know one company which in effect has to keep three different sets of books in order to have costs which conform to different theories, each "valid" for a particular purpose. Moreover, the general understanding of cost is different as respects the business man, the accountant, or the economist. But this is only the beginning. The use of cost data and the understandings with reference to average costs, unit costs, joint costs, distributions of cost, marginal costs, and the specific productivity of particular factors of production, reveal a mess of conflicting practices and theories. The reasons for this confusion will be suggested, I hope, by the following discussion. I had better present it under these headings: (a) The problem of aggregate costs; (b) average costs; (c) unit costs; (d) joint costs; (e) variations of costs; and (f) correlations of cost variations. a. Aggregate Costs

The first thing to say is that there is not much difficulty about the meaning of "cost" if no capital is involved, i.e., if no expenditures are made for durable productive structures and materials, and if product is immediately disposed of. (I shall use "capital" in the physical or, rather, non-financial sense.) But if this is not the case, we at once run into difficulties in deciding (1) whether an expenditure is for capital or for current expense or cost; (2) how to determine the loss of capital value from use and from physical deterioration and obsolescence; and (3) how and when to determine and to treat as a cost a loss or gain in capital value due to factors of valuation other than depreciation, i.e., other than (2). (1) I am not sure that even from the point of view of abstract economic theory the distinction is always clear between expenditures for capital and current expense, for the question of duration is one of the fundamentals involved, and any time criterion I think must be arbitrary. 

But in practice—and your questions all relate to what actually happens 18

in the world-there not only is difficulty but wide diversity. For example, the rule generally in my business is that expenditures for materials or parts having a life of less than a year are expense, otherwise they are capital expenditures. (Neglecting the case unimportant relatively with us when they are temporarily “in supply.") But in a department store (and in some manufactories) where fixed capital is quite small but inventory very large but active, it is quite obvious that our rule would be practical nonsense. While the capital here is of a "liquid" type, its aggregate is relatively constant in amount. 

Take a third case, a life insurance company. Here expenditures are being made constantly for investments, for running expenses and for claims. The expenditures for investments are derived from premiums which are capital payments to the extent that legal reserves are set up corresponding to the increase in investment assets which are at most but obscure representations of capital. But when claims are paid they are in part repayments of these capital advances, not merely expenses or costs. Here the analogy to anything in the first two cases is not close. 

Take finally a fourth case, expenditure for good-will, or “intangible” capital. In some cases such expenditures are always treated as expense, hence are direct costs, and are not capitalized. In other cases, a more numerous class, some expenditures for good-will, intangible rights, etc., are treated as direct expense, and not capitalized, while other such expenditures are not treated as direct costs but are capitalized. In still a third class all such expenditures are capitalized, as for example, when a company is organized to purchase the rights of a patent. There are many different and good reasons for the differences in practice which we need not consider, except to note that proportions and relative importance have much to do with them. As I do not wish to discuss this again, I also note that when expenditures for intangible things are capitalized, they may then be amortized-hence become costs in the end but through a number of accounting periods-or may not be amortized, and hence never become costs (except as losses on failure). 

All that I wish to emphasize here is that at the outset we are confronted with the fact that what is a capital expenditure and what is not is open to the most diverse solutions. These differences are more or less reconcilable by the hocus-pocus with which business men, accountants, actuaries, lawyers and economists rationalize our ways of keeping the world moving despite our ignorance or our orthodox theories. (2) However, once we have set up the distinction between capital expenditures and others then our real trouble begins. For capital 19

(physical) wears out, becomes obsolete, and the expenditures for it (as well as for "intangible capital," "good-will," "rights") have often to be amortized. Depreciation is usually regarded nowadays as an item of cost by business men, accountants, tax authorities, and economists; but it is by no means universal to treat depreciation as such, and other methods such as treating replacement as an expense are used. (This suggests that I merely mention that the deferring or non-deferring of maintenance is an important practical question as to the meaning of costs in any conventional period.)

Now it is quite apparent that at least so far as concerns the relation of costs to prices, the idea of waiting until capital is finally exhausted or abandoned to get the cost of depreciation is utterly impracticable. 

I suppose some of the reasons are obvious. Consequently, estimates of cost are usually made. For this purpose we must impute losses of capital to causes. The principal assumptions on which this is done are (1) that the cost is a function of time; or (2) that cost is a function of use. If the first, then cost for any period of time (a month or a year) depends upon the duration of the capital, and also upon assumptions as to whether this function is even at all equal periods of its life, or varies as between periods. Thus to get cost for a particular period requires estimating duration (and salvage), and assumptions as to the distribution by time periods. On the other hand, if depreciation is taken to be a function of use, then it is not in principle necessary to estimate how long a capital will be used, but only salvage and the amount of use (in terms of some selected units) and depreciation can then be translated into time periods according to the units of use during particular periods.

We need not discuss the numerous variations of practice and combinations of reasoning governing practice. Obviously, under the circumstances cost is not fact but variable estimate, the use of which is largely a matter of convention, and the amount of which is the effect of a conglomeration of assumptions based upon a very few valid historical facts. I cannot stop to discuss this subject further. (3) Depreciation concerns the amortization of the initial expenditure for separable items, or classes of capital. Whatever the difficulties and diversities of practice as to this subject, they seem to me to be moderate compared to the difficulties and diversities involved in losses of value of capital (other than by depreciation) and gains in capital value. 

The most obvious and common loss of course need not detain usthat involved where revenue does not cover costs. But there are serious 20

losses of quite different kind. One is the loss of capital not counted as loss in current operating, brought about by forced sale, insolvencies and receiverships. This also could be dismissed were it not for the fact that the treatment of such losses is greatly affected by the size and organization of a business. Thus a small business operating at a loss or with insufficient liquid capital may not survive, and therefore suffer great liquidation losses-not counted cost-when the same business as a department or branch of a larger business may also be liquidated as a failure, yet have the losses treated in effect as a cost. Indeed, this is almost necessarily the case, no matter what the detailed accounting, if the concern survives. Again, what is conservative and honest practice in "writing down" and "writing up" assets varies widely between businesses. Inventory losses are obviously a part of aggregate costs. Are they to be offset by inventory gains if the latter occur? Practice differs; and there are good reasons for many of the variations. 

This is all so complicated that I should summarize. I am not discussing average costs, unit costs, marginal costs, only aggregate costs, which have first to be determined before it is possible to find any kind of average costs, unit costs, etc. I am pointing out to you that as soon as we reach the stage of the use of capital we at once necessarily encounter diversity of theory and of practice as to what expenditures are expense or direct cost, and what are for capital, usually involving indirect cost. 

As to the latter, we find that for any conventional accounting period what cost is depends upon whether depreciation is viewed as a function of time or as a function of production, if it is to be accounted for at all, and that the amount of cost is dependent upon estimates as to duration of capital or as to the use of it and as to the salvage estimated as of some future date. And, finally, the question of capital loss or gain is difficult to take into account as either cost or profit in a conventional period (a year) especially in the smaller concerns. 

Addendum: 

It was my intention in writing this section merely to mention some of the principal classes of costs in respect to which there is generally vagueness, uncertainty and arbitrariness in stating aggregate costs for a conventional accounting period. I thought it would be sufficient to make the point by discussing chiefly depreciation. 

On reading it, however, it seems to me I cannot avoid, as I hoped, saying something about the matter of cost of finance, which I think theoretically more difficult than depreciation, valuation losses, intangible capital treatment, or deferring of maintenance, in its bearing on the meaning of statements of aggregate costs. Cost of finance looms large in price problems to the extent that costs enter into them, however it may be formally accounted for. Here we again find diversity of practice and theory and note wide discrepancy between the views of the economist 21

� and of the business man, of the price maker and of the accountant; and between the large and the small business. 

If we start with a free open economy of individual entrepreneurs and partnerships, interest and net profits in the aggregate of the economic system are the cost of money or credit (finance capital) from the economist's point of view, and probably usually are not costs from the individual point of view as respects a particular enterprise. 

Borrowings relate to the total position of the individual, just as his saved capital funds, and not to a particular enterprise or venture, and they are made on personal responsibility, not organized responsibility. Under these conditions net earnings are the difference between revenues and costs exclusive of interest. As soon as businesses become stabilized, however, not being regarded as an isolated venture, which is the situation in most cases of incorporated businesses, then money is borrowed for the business, not for the personal position of the individual, and on the responsibility of a business organization. Interest (but not profits) is then very clearly seen to be a cost of doing business as much as wages. This position is usually met by the accountant not by including interest in costs, but by recognizing "charges" or "fixed charges" against net earnings before profits.

In a continuing enterprise, and especially one that expands, it becomes clear to the managers that, within limits, profits as well as interest are necessary for finance, and that the distinction between interest and profits (dividends) is a secondary one of convenience, custom and legal status. If dividends must be paid in the long run to secure funds (either borrowed or subscribed), dividends within limits are also a cost of money. Such business men view the costs of capital funds more nearly in the way of economists. Thus either in taking costs into account for pricing purposes or for deciding between alternative processes involving different amounts of capital expenditures, they treat interest and profits (within limits) as costs. Accounting does not proceed this far, nor does the income tax collector; but regulating authorities and courts in effect do so as respects the limitations of income and rates of public utilities; and many large competitive businesses do so administratively; i.e., they do not consider that they have made a profit until after "normal" interest has been earned on the total of capital funds of all kinds (including previously earned and undistributed surpluses).

Very obviously this situation further beclouds the meaning of aggregate costs no matter what particular practice or theory obtains in a business. What would you say was the cost for the year 1940 of a business which earned no profit after contract interest, and which was conducted on the theory that a normal rate of return on capital funds was a cost of doing business? Would it make any difference in your judgment if it paid normal dividends out of surpluses on the theory that continuity of dividends was important to its credit and the interest cost thereof? 

Note also that the larger, more stable and comprehensive a business the more it tends to adopt the concepts of the economist. Here I am pointing out that concepts, method of reasoning, and conventions vary depending upon the degree of isolation or comprehensiveness of the system under consideration, a statement applicable to other affairs and to science and not only to business. What is or is not cost, especially for the short period, will properly differ between the great institutional business in which pluses and minuses can usually be compensating, and the small personal business in which minuses may easily mean failure. 

Now the purpose of this long argument is to show you that long. 22

before you get to averages, marginal costs, and the other paraphernalia of business statisticians and theorists you have to recognize the conjectural and conventional character of the basic data. The more capitalistic operations become (whether under communism, state socialism, or relatively free enterprise), the more this is true. What it means, so far as behavior is concerned, is that business men don't and can't behave according to the intersection of demand and supply or price and cost curves, and the analytical apparatus of abstract economic theorists who are developing intellectual tools for conceivable "pure" or limiting cases. 

If not that, then what? I think clearly they are compelled to think and act in terms of patterns of production. In practical language this means that they react to markets in terms of the maintenance of productive organization or of going concerns, not in terms of the advantage or disadvantage of isolated transactions, unless (as they would say) "other things are equal.” The modern business man-executive or entrepreneur-acts in terms of organization-the very time element of capital life compels it—not in terms of individual motives, the implicit basis of so much economic theory. 

It is so important that you get the essential correctness of what I have said that I wish to give an authoritative confirmation of it. The problems of accounting for profits are chiefly, though not exclusively, problems of accounting for costs, so that uncertainties as to the nature of profits are primarily uncertainties as to costs. b. Average Costs 

An average mathematically is a quotient. The divisor is a number obtained by counting things regarded as equal to each other, and taken 23

as a group. The dividend is a number obtained similarly. The things counted in the divisor are usually different from those counted in the dividend, so that the quotient as an average has to be labelled with the names of the things counted in both the divisor and the dividend. 

You see that I am defining "average" by stating the operations performed to get it. But unlike the division of homogeneous things into equal groups, which can be performed physically even without counting, an average can only be performed by the mind; and when the mental operations have been performed what seems to result is an intellectual tool and nothing more. It is a statement about an abstract relationship between the quantities numerically stated with reference to two different kinds of measures of two different kinds of things. Whether the statement means anything I have some doubt unless you also state what you are going to do with it; and whether it is a good intellectual tool you have constructed will depend in the last analysis, I think, upon whether you find the results of its use good.

In other words, when someone quotes an average to you, he hasn't really said anything to you until he answers your question "So what?”

You will find that a very disconcerting question to ask; for most people seem to think that an average is something like a rock or an apple right out of nature, that you can hit someone with or eat. 

Although the ultimate test of an average is the usefulness of it—which usually depends in part upon the skill and knowledge of the user-there are some preliminary tests of the tool that can be applied before using it, and it is a good thing to apply them just as it is to look at a jack before trying to lift the car with it. (I got badly hurt once by not looking at a jack-handle first, and I have also been hurt by not looking some of my averages in the eye.) Outside the obvious test of seeing whether the division is correctly made-it sometimes is not—the most important test is that of inquiring whether the dividend and the divisor each mean what they say and are correctly stated. Now, most statements of the dividend and of the divisor used to compute an average look exact but are not; they are at best approximations, and the nice question usually is whether the approximations involved are sufficient to make the tool (the average) useful for the purpose to which it is to be put. You will find that experts in their own fields and statisticians when they are criticizing other statisticians are perfect fiends in finding flaws or inadequate approximations in the dividend or the divisor used in getting averages; but other people don't.

I know of no field in which this, perhaps boring, reiteration of simple 1 24

fundamentals is more pertinent than with respect to average costs. The assumptions involved are that the dividend (aggregate costs) means something which we all agree to and is stated correctly; and that the divisor also means something, the units counted being homogeneous to a sufficient approximation. I find such assumptions often not correct in capitalistic complex enterprises, and where the units are purchase and sale units. Let me discuss these statements briefly. 

I have already suggested the vagueness and indeterminate meaning of "aggregate cost." In those cases where an enterprise or venture has terminated there is not much question as to costs for its entire duration; but this case is of little importance. Usually the aggregate costs desired are those for a month or a year. Now I have mentioned several classes of costs which are either vaguely defined or arbitrarily determined. Here, for illustration, I shall refer only to depreciation. The elements of capital structures have lives varying from one or two to fifty or more years. The distribution of the capital costs involved depends upon the assumptions as to the "cause" of depreciation as to each element, and how it is to be distributed for each. Thus the aggregate cost for a given year will depend upon the direct cost expenditures and upon the indirect costs distributed variably over a period of years, and therefore for a particular year complicated averages based upon estimates, made by conventional methods on conventional assumptions. So the dividend (aggregate costs for a certain period) used in getting an average cost per unit, is a combination of fact and estimate, the latter being itself usually an average. This is not important, perhaps, if you are dealing with the average cost per pound of mackerel caught in a row-boat; but it certainly is if you are dealing with a shipyard for battleships, a steel plant, a water system, a coal mine, a telephone system, a meat packing plant, a flour mill. In each case, you need to know the accounting practice, the assumptions as to depreciation and other items, and the relevant conventions to know what cost means and to judge whether it is useful for a particular purpose. The notion that it represents something definite and reliable for general purposes is in my opinion pure myth. 

Turn now to the divisor, the count of units. I have already shown you that the unit of sale is an abstract construction, and one which is neither specially adapted to the measurement of productive factors nor of utilities. It may therefore be expected to be a selected heterogeneous conglomerate of abstractions of some kind (cf. a unit like "yd. gray homespun 30 in. wide, 3 lb. to yd.") and to be defective as a unit of cost averages. Its use will therefore be found to be highly restricted and 25

often abandoned for a still more general constructed unit. For example: 

The most general unit for telephone service is the telephone station (telephone plus associated coils and ringer). No such thing is sold. 

The most general unit for automobile production or use is the automobile; but it is not an automobile but a car of a particular model at a particular place, that is sold. Neither of these is of much use in the analysis of cost. If the classification of telephones as between successive years, or the distribution of cars, where several classes are produced, is constant, then average costs give some indication as to whether factors of production (or methods of accounting) have changed or not. But if the classification or distribution or the method of accounting has changed average costs by themselves are significant of almost nothing. 

If, however, we try to use specific price units in dealing with total costs, we are confronted either with the problem of the allocation of joint costs which I shall discuss below, or with the attempt to construct an "artificial" over-all unit, in which some "weighting" of specific price units is taken into account. This is sometimes attempted but it is an arbitrary proceeding which rarely satisfies any group or purpose. I think 

I am approximately correct in saying that no general unit for ascertaining average costs has any validity or more than the most restricted usefulness, and is subject to great dangers of misuse even by those who are thoroughly informed. I make exception of the business in which only a single product and single sales unit is involved. It appears to me to have been far too convenient to overemphasize such simple conditions, without sufficiently correcting for the meaninglessness of the idea, except for highly restricted and special purposes, as applied to complex production and sales enterprises. 

c. Unit Costs 

These considerations are, I believe, at the root of the development of unit costs. Units here are purely production units, and in any large business of almost any kind they are of several or many types. I think historically they came into wide use beginning fifty or sixty years ago. I shall not attempt to discuss the pitfalls in the definition of such units, nor the circular reasoning often involved in them. In the hands of experts they may be valuable, and indeed the planning and pricing of production is largely dependent upon the use of unit costs. 

However, what I ought to point out here is that the more units of production are refined the more the costs related to them are joint, and not specific. This subjects money costs per unit to increasing vagueness and unreliability for financial purposes. The situation reminds me of 26

the indeterminacy principle in atomic physics which I think might be stated as this: the more accurately the position of an electron is determined the less accurately its velocity can be determined; and vice versa. 

Similarly, the more units of production are refined, the less the meaning and reliability of their use in cost averages. Hence, production units are nowadays widely used for determining averages only of expenditures for particular factors directly correlated with the particular production operations being measured; and are very often not in terms of money at all, but rather in terms of hours of direct labor, or special materials, or space occupied, etc. 

d. Joint Costs 

This leads to a few remarks about the nature of "jointness" in joint costs. There are two senses in which costs are joint. In one, two different products result from a single operation or class of operations; in the other, a single product or unit of product is produced by a number of different operations or is compounded of two or more materials or both. In the first the costs are joint because the product is joint; in the second the cost is joint because of the combination of separate costs of operations jointly or serially performed.

I know of no logical way to distribute costs to joint products. The logic of costs is a "cause and effect" logic. The idea is that the production of a thing "caused" a certain cost; or, better, that the product is teleologically the cause of the cost. Now it is apparent in the case of joint products that the cost was caused jointly, not specifically by each product. There are, to be sure, a number of illogical ways in which a division of a total cost is imputed to each joint product. One, in the case of a main and a new by-product, is to assume that the by-product cost is O, so that the main product "bears" all the cost. This sounds good for a little while but not long; for when the price of the main product falls below the cost so computed, the implication is that the main product should be discontinued as causing a loss; but this is nonsense, for discontinuing the main product eliminates the by-product also, whereas both products together will produce sufficient revenue to cover total costs. So the busy little business man will play a new cost hunch. Perhaps he will impute to the by-product a cost which equals its price; or he takes its cost as found by another producer; or he will use arbitrary percentages found by gazing at the stars or a table of atomic weights, or he puts it up to a committee which may agree by the process of arguing itself into an exhausted state of willingness to agree to any arbitrary thing-whatever will serve to "justify" or ration- 27

alize, as if a result of cause and effect reasoning, something that common sense and experience show is good, but in fact cannot be based upon the kind of causal analysis that specific costs imply. The business world, as I see it, is full of this kind of self-delusion or self-deception, some of which is very complex and subtle. Most of it is sincere enough; but it often leads to serious errors, I suppose.

Let us now turn from the case of joint products to that of a single product jointly produced. At first sight it appears quite different from that of joint products. It is the case when the cost of a product (in a given period) is built up of the average cost of the separate operations and materials going into it, the separate operations and separate supplies of materials being used for many units. This may be a "good" cost figure if only one product is made, and all of the factors in its production are exclusive to it. But even here it should be noted that separate units of a homogeneous product are themselves jointly produced; so that an average unit cost if treated as specific is subject to all the fallacy in principle which I have suggested above as applying to the assignment of costs to joint products.

Let me prove this to you. We are producing, let us say, something called "camelots" made of brass parts. One of the operations is polishing the camelot on an emery wheel. A good man paid, say, $8.00 a day can polish 100 camelots per day. The cost of polishing is then $.08 per camelot. But obviously there is no specific cost of a single camelot, for any figure you get depends upon the number of camelots produced. If the volume is such that the polisher has only 75 a day to polish, the cost would be over 10 cents. Any average cost figure is dependent upon the production as a whole. The single unit has no independent production status, but is a joint product with other units of the same kind of product.

This illustration is about as simple as can be found in modern production, which is based on mass-large or small-not piecemeal custom work; and so any average cost is solely a kind of measurement of the whole production in an arbitrary period of time, not the cost of a single unit. 

Production today is preponderantly of joint products jointly produced, i.e., it is joint in both senses stated. The cost of unit of a product is, therefore, indeterminate. Whatever the convenience of using average costs for many purposes-and it is often useful to have them-never forget that they are not specific costs of units of production, though they are often so treated.

f. Correlation of Cost Variations 

I must not undertake to discuss the analysis of costs and cost differences extensively. The object of analysis is to discover means of controlling trends or tendencies which are adverse and of reinforcing those which are favorable. Broadly, the method of doing this is to correlate changes in costs with changes in factors affecting production, stated quantitatively. The most frequently "found" correlations are average costs in terms of units of production, but such averages generally are merely a first step in analysis, conveniently indicating whether costs have changed between two periods in greater or less proportion than production, without indicating the "causes" of the disproportion, if any, so found. 

Beyond this point the processes of analysis for purposes of control of production factors are numerous and we may pass them by here. What 

I wish to concentrate on is solely the use of cost analysis in connection with prices, and particularly upon the closely interrelated problems of increasing costs, diminishing costs, and marginal costs.

I take it that marginal cost means that increment of total costs that occurs in connection with the latest increment of output during a specified period of time. I wish particularly to call your attention to the time factor usually involved in any statement of cost or production increments -they are additions in a particular period of time. An increment of 29

units per annum may be of quite different significance than an increment of 10 units each month for 12 months.

Thus, if the increment of output in a given period is taken as 1 unit added to an output of 100 units, and the cost for 100 units is X and of 101 units is X + y, then y is the cost of the marginal unit in the total of 101 under the conditions. If _X > y, then costs are said to be diminishing; if _X 100 100 <y, costs are said to be increasing. Your question is (1) What is the meaning of y, the marginal cost?, and (2) how can marginal cost be used? (1) My answer is that y, stated as a marginal cost, has no meaning whatever taken by itself. In conjunction with the marginal unit, it may be called the function of the latter. This is purely a descriptive statement that the increase y is observed to accompany the production of the additional unit 1; but no use can be made of this description except by stating when or under what conditions the statement is found to be true. This is contrary to the common view that y is the effect of the production of the marginal unit 1 which may thus be called the cause of y; and this leads to the statement that the marginal cost is y, and y comes to be looked upon as something specific and absolute, disassociated from the conditions. Now, it is possible to conceive of the increment of unit 1 as an independent thing distinguishable and separate from any other units, and absolutely controllable by a free decision to produce or not produce that unit; but this is in no event similarly possible with respect to y which is an increment added to X by reason of the total of production operations which are not separable as between 100 units and 101 units. Stated in another way, each unit of 100 produced or of 101 produced is a joint product, and the cost of any unit jointly produced is indeterminate. Thus the statement that y is the marginal cost is merely a convenient statement that the increment y is a function of (101-100) units in a total production operation, and it is nothing more. 

Such a statement may often be made very useful. 

The problem here involved is, on the logical side, that of "cause and effect" reasoning as against "functional" reasoning, and on the economic side that of the specific effect of a particular factor of production among several jointly operating. As to the latter, I will only say that the idea of a specific effect of a joint factor is a logical contradiction-one that underlies, for example, labor theories of value; and which, in the form of the marginal cost of increments of particular joint factors, runs (from recollection) e.g., in J. B. Clark's work and in parts of Keynes' "General Theory of Unemployment, Interest and Money." 30

Concerning cause and effect reasoning, its fallacy (except under careful qualification) I have too little time to go into. Its alternative is called "functional" reasoning (Hermann Weyl) "schedule" or "pattern" or "administrative" reasoning (by myself), "multi-dimensional" reasoning (by Karl Mannheim). Our everyday language is unfortunately a "cause and effect" language which is one reason why business men so persistently misrepresent how their minds really work. I attempted (inadequately) to bridge the gap in my book in the Chapter "The Theory of 

Opportunism" by calling what most people call "cause" "strategic factor," a term which I borrowed from John R. Commons. Perhaps you do not know that "cause and effect" are abandoned in physics. For example, H. Margenau (Phil. of Science, vol. 1, p. 135-6) says: "There is no law of connection between cause and effect known to science; moreover, these concepts are foreign to physical analysis." I could find statements of P. W. Bridgman (“The assumption of the principle of causality is not necessary to thought because physicists have been doing valid thinking without it." The Intelligent Individual and Society, p. 35) and others of the same character. (2) Now, as to the uses of marginal costs: I should say that the correlation between increments of two factors of a productive system such as marginal costs, states a trend in the pattern of a system consisting in part of interdependent variables. If this trend is considered favorable or unfavorable, an effort may be made to change that trend by the alteration of some factor or factors or the introduction of a new one in the pattern. Thus if the trend of costs is unfavorable, effort may be made to change processes, to avoid wastes, to secure better prices of materials. If the pattern be taken to include the price pattern associated with production and also to include profit or loss, the action may be to change the pattern of prices either so as to control the production system or so as to change the trend of revenue, taking into account the changes of the pattern of demand accompanying the changes of the pattern of prices, and the changes in the accompanying production. 

CONCLUSION 

The answers to your questions in the final analysis come down to the statement that "what the traffic will bear price making" is one reflection of underlying conditions of both modern marketing and modern technology. The chief factors in these conditions are capitalistic processes, large scale production, complex organization, close interdependence between the utilities of individuals as respects specific products, increasing competition between products with decreasing com- 31

petition between producers, and increasing competition between geographical areas and specialized groups with decreasing competition between individuals. The answer is not significantly related to the type of organization-monopolistic, competitive, state owned, privately owned -except as large scale production tends more to semi-monopolistic, monopolistic, and commonly-owned or managed operations than to widely competitive enterprises. The criteria of prices conforming to these conditions are appropriately of systematic character not related, except in final approximations, to specific prices treated individually. 

The proper comprehension of such systems of prices, as is true of systems of administration, of complex organizations, of physical and chemical systems and organisms, requires a technique of thought of a different type than is convenient when it has been proved by experience feasible to treat the simple correlation of two factors as if it were independent of numerous others. The latter type, which characterizes ordinary cause and effect reasoning, is, however, well adapted to the ordinary behavior of each of us in ordinary and customary circumstances and is the type to which our language is specifically adapted. The only language suitable for dealing with systematic interrelationships of complexity is mathematics, which is not adapted to the treatment or description of qualitative factors except in the most highly abstract degree. Hence, not only in the particular field of economics, but in other social fields, we are confronted with the perpetual dilemma of using language inadequate to our problem but suitable for routine and individual purposes. Upon the basis of thinking in terms of an unsuitable language we are continually constructing generalizations on false judgments that conflict with the understanding and behavior of those whose duty it is to plan complex systems. This dilemma in my opinion underlies much of the serious conflict of our world, which is a good reason for giving it sympathetic attention.

Should you have difficulty in grasping the sense of systems of interdependent variables, you may some time be able to look up Dr. L. J. 

Henderson's little book on Pareto's Sociology, especially the appendices, in which you will find both mechanical and chemical illustrations involving no more mathematics or chemistry than you have had. An excellent more philosophical approach to the multi-dimensional type of thought is in Karl Mannheim's valuable "Man and Society in an Age of Reconstruction" pp. 147-155 and 164-173.
