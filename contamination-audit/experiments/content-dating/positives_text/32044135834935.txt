INTRODUCTION 

Public interest in the labor problem has been centered in recent months in the activities of the 

Antitrust Division of the U. S. Department of 

Justice. Certain unions have been prosecuted for alleged violations of the Sherman Antitrust Act passed fifty years ago by the Congress of the U. S. to curb the growth of monopolies in restraint of trade. 

The issues have been both confused and confusing to the layman. It has been difficult for many people to understand the reason for this action. 

Mr. Broach, the Director of Education of Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 of the Inter national Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, has sought to present labor's side of the case in simple, non-technical language.

This pamphlet is worthy of study for anyone who wants to understand labor's viewpoint on the current issue, written by a representative of labor.

LABOR AND THE ANTITRUST LAW Two Pictures 

There are two pictures to the story of labor and the antitrust law. One is that of private suits brought by employers for alleged damages. Treble damages are allowed in such cases. These suits have been occurring since 1902.

Just now the Republic Steel Company has a suit against the Steel Workers for a total of $7,500,000 

-plus lawyers fees and costs. The Teamsters' 

Unions in New England are being sued for $990,- 

000 “damages" because of a tri-state strike. There are other such suits including that of electrical manufacturers against Electrical Workers Local 

Union No. 3. 

The other picture is the crusade of the Antitrust 

Division of the U. S. Department of Justice. This has resulted in many criminal indictments being recently rendered against unions and their officials throughout the country. The Government has very rarely used the law against labor. The menace of criminal prosecution "has lain dormant these many decades".

In both pictures the results are somewhat the same: Unions are dragged through the courts, some go bankrupt defending themselves and their - 5

officers, members are frightened and discouraged, officers' time is consumed, unions are discredited, many become afraid to move, effectiveness is sometimes destroyed-and the lawyers have a holiday. 

THE BACKGROUND 

was

Let us have some of the background: The Antitrust Law known as the Sherman Act adopted in 1890, fifty years ago. It was to prevent the growth of trusts. The people were afraid of monopoly. The Civil War had wiped out one kind of slavery. Now it was believed another kind of slavery was taking its place. 

One Supreme Court Justice wrote: "The convic tion was universal that the country was in real danger from another kind of slavery . . that would result from the aggregation of capital in the hands of a few, controlling for their own advantage exclusively, the entire business of the country, including the production and sale of the necessities of life."

Now you would think that all the agitation against trusts and monopolies of 50 years ago would have nothing to do with labor. And when you read the ancient law you would think labor unions and their officials would not be involved. 

Read these words:

I. Every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy in restraint of trade or commerce among

the several states or with foreign nations is hereby declared to be illegal.

II. Every person who shall monopolize, or attempt to monopolize any part of the trade or commerce among the several states, or with foreign nations, shall be deemed guilty of a misdemeanor.

UNIONS ARE NOT TRUSTS 

Yes, we know labor unions are not trusts. Not monopolies. Not huge combinations of capital. 

Not in the profit-making business. Not dealing in trade or commerce. They only bargain for the sale of labor and skill of human beings. Unions have a right to strike-and to refuse to work for or to aid non-union employers. Unions are doing what they have done since their beginning. 

But you would be wrong when you think that all the cry of 50 years ago against trusts and monopolies and the plain wording of the law-would not touch labor unions and their officials. That is exactly what union labor thought at the time. 

And so did the late Samuel Gompers-then head of the A. F. of L. But he was wrong.

Gompers tells us he had definite assurance that labor would be exempt from this law-that it was designed solely against trusts and corporations. 

Senator John Sherman, who introduced the law, said:

"The combinations of workmen to promote their interests, promote their welfare and . . to get their fair share in the division of production, are not affected in the slightest degree-nor can they be included in the words or intent of the bill as now reported."

NEW CLUB AGAINST LABOR 

But despite all this despite Congress not making labor subject to the law certain judges held otherwise. They decided that the Sherman Act did not mean what it said that it meant something entirely different. So, little time was lost in using the antitrust law as a basis for labor injunctions and devastating damage suits against unions. 

The Pullman strikers (1894) and their leaders were among the first. The famous Danbury Hatters' Case came in 1902. The Hitchman Coal Case against the miners, the Duplex Printing Case against the machinists, the Bedford Cut Stone

Case against the stone cutters, and various other cases followed.

The claim against the hatters was that they had, by instituting a boycott against non-union hats, violated the antitrust law. They were "interfering with interstate trade". The court awarded damages of $252,000 to Loewe, the manufacturer. The U. S. 

Supreme Court upheld the verdict (1908). The strikers had no such sum. Many of them were old men-with not enough to eat.

But the full pound of flesh was demanded. So the men lost their little homes-homes they and their children had grown up in. The blow was too much for some- -so they committed suicide. The 

A. F. of L. called upon other union workers to help. And enough was raised to recover the homes for the remaining hatters.

LAW IS AMENDED 

Union labor was aroused by the U. S.

Supreme 

Court's decision in the Hatters' Case. Congress was asked to make it quite clear that labor unions and their officials were exempt from the law designed against trusts. This resulted in the law being amended in 1914 by the Clayton Act. This plainly stated: "The labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce. Nothing contained in the antitrust laws shall be construed to . . forbid or restrain individual members of such organizations from lawfully carrying out the legitimate objects thereof; nor shall such organizations, or the members thereof be held or construed to be illegal combinations or conspiracies in restraint of trade under the Anti-Trust Laws."

Congress went even further and said that no federal court from • • "Shall prohibit any person or persons ceasing to perform any work or labor, or

from recommending, advising or persuading others by peaceful means so to do or from ceasing to patronize or to employ any party to such dispute, or from recommending, advising, or persuading others by peaceful and lawful means so to do.. ably assembling • • or from peace- nor shall any of the acts specified in this paragraph be considered or held to be violations of any law of the United States." 

Surely those words are clear. Men could hardly make their intention any clearer. Anyway, Mr. 

Gompers hailed the labor provisions of the Clayton Act as a "Magna Carta to labor". But a whole series of court opinions soon made it all about as "meaningless as a European report".

JUDGES DECIDE THE LAW 

You see, in the reality of things the judges decided what shall be law. They say what was intended or desired and what Congress meant by its words, even when Congress protests. Judges may change the law or destroy it altogether. At first judges even held that labor unions were "illegal" and men were beaten and jailed for mere attendance at meetings. And judges have reversed themselves and each other over and over. 

For example: The Duplex Printing Company was one of four concerns making newspaper presses.

The Duplex Company insisted on a 10-hour day and the "open" shop. The other three companies 10

granted the 8-hour day and the union shop. Competition of the Duplex Company endangered the other concerns and the wages and hours of their union men.

So the union machinists in another state refused to work upon or install the Duplex presses. The 

District Federal Judge held the action was legal. 

The next higher court, with three judges, divided. 

Two said the action was legal. One said it was not. 

Then the U. S. Supreme Court, with its nine judges, divided (1921).

Six of these last nine held the action was "a conspiracy in restraint of interstate trade". Three of the nine held otherwise. So the decision of the majority of the U. S. Supreme Court became "Law."

Now this made a total of 13 federal judges-all of about equal legal ability-who considered the case. Six held the law did not apply to laborthat Congress had clearly said so in the Clayton Act. Seven, expressing their own economic and social views, held the law did apply. 

Federal Judge Amidon said this: ... “In my judgment, all such actions by courts are a direct refusal on their part to obey a statute which was intended to limit their powers. . . There is no reason that a just court can assign why American courts should not as cheerfully obey the Clayton Act as the English courts obeyed the Trades Disputes Act of 1906." 11

Perhaps the English judges were influenced by what once happened to some of them. When they attempted to nullify or set aside a law passed by Parliament, the Chief Justice was hanged and the rest of his court exiled to France for life. On another occasion, under Alfred the Great, 44 judges were hanged in one year for violating the right of trial by jury.

DANGER THAT PEOPLE MAY SEE 

If you think we are too critical of judges just listen to what one of their own-Judge Seymour

Thompson once said to the State Bar Associa tion of Texas: 

"There is danger, real danger, that the people will see at one sweeping glance that all the power of their government, Federal and State, lies at the feet of us lawyers, at the feet of the judicial oligarchy; that those powers are being steadily exercised in behalf of the wealthy and powerful classes. Great trusts may place their yokes upon the necks of the people. 22 

Now hear Judge John Ford, former New York

State Supreme Court Justice: 

"The judges of the United States are the greatest obstructionists. . . . No impartial student of the subject can doubt that the courts are partial to accumulated wealth; that they are on the side of the powerful employer and 12

against the employees, and that they are daily through judge-made law, oppressing the poor and lowly in the interest of amassed capital." 

TWO SETS OF RULES 

Of course the antitrust law has been a farce to corporations. What does it matter, for instance, to the General Motors Corporation that it-and three of its affiliates are fined (Nov. 1939) $5,000 each, for violating the antitrust law. 

Professor Francis Downing says: "It scarcely requires saying that monopolies and trusts and corporations have continued their consistent and vigorous growth since that day (1890) Warring against trusts has hardly been the sleepless task of our government .... "Distinctions have been made to permit what were called 'reasonable trusts' .. But there is no rule of reason for labor unions.

When corporations act they, the courts say, may act reasonably: when labor acts it is malicious. The conclusion is inescapable that there have been two sets of rules." 313

THURMAN ARNOLD'S CRUSADE 

Is it trust busting or union busting? 

He is an Assistant U. S. Attorney General — in charge of the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice. Before this he was a law profes sor at Yale. And he wrote a book-"The Folklore of Capitalism". He wrote about trusts. His book, it is claimed, landed him his present position. 

In his book Mr. Arnold said: "Trust busting . . . made Theodore Roosevelt a great man. 

Theodore Roosevelt never accomplished anything with his trust busting. 

The crusade was not a practical one . . . "Men like Senator Borah founded political careers on the continuance of such crusades, which were entirely futile but enormously picturesque, and which paid big dividends in terms of personal prestige . . . 99 

Theodore Roosevelt had 5 attorneys to help him. 

Mr. Arnold has 196 and his crusade includes labor unions and their officials. He says "the Clayton Act exempts legitimate labor activities from the antitrust laws." So his position is based on 15

what he says are not legitimate activities. He calls these "unreasonable restraints of trade".

WHAT IS UNREASONABLE? 

Mr. Arnold first says what is unreasonable. He first decides who should be prosecuted. Then the courts take their turn at saying what is unreasonable. But we know this word unreasonable has many meanings to different people. 

It was not unreasonable to a few judges to rule that the U. S. Steel Company, the United Shoe 

Company and other huge concerns are "reasonable" trusts. It was not unreasonable for these to combine and charge monopoly prices in all states all the traffic will bear without killing their markets.

But it was unreasonable for union men to object to installing material produced in other states by non-union men at lower wages. This was a "con- spiracy in restraint of trade". It was unreasonable for labor even to try to control the price of the only commodity it has to sell-labor lives. It often becomes "unreasonable restraint of trade" when labor interferes with the "profits of those who buy labor lives".

Mr. Arnold prosecutes unions and their officials under the 50 year old antitrust law for five types of "unreasonable restraint” made public Nov. 

20, 1939. These follow: 16

1. UNREASONABLE RESTRAINTS TO PREVENT THE USE OF CHEAPER MATERIAL, IMPROVED EQUIPMENT, OR MORE EFFICIENT METHODS. 

Well, in the electrical field there is a National Board of Fire Underwriters. It determines the risk. It fixes the insurance rates. It decides what material is safe or unsafe. Inspectors pass on the materials. The union and its officials have nothing to say about the cheapness or quality of materials. 

But unions may be interested because a product is made by non-union workers-perhaps by convict labor or child labor—and which threatens to displace union workers. And what is wrong with this? Supreme Court Justices Brandeis, Holmes and Clarke said: "May not all with a common interest join in refusing to expend their labor upon articles whose very production constitutes an attack upon their standards of living and the institution which they are convinced supports it?" 

(Duplex Printing Case.) 

However, states even exclude products from other states. One state even requires outsiders to put a red coloring in milk when sending it into the state. Naturally most people won't buy red milk.

But this is NOT held to be an "unreasonable restraint of trade". When union workers say, "Buy 

OUR labor; protect our wages and living standards" that IS an "unreasonable restraint", says 17

Mr. Arnold. Then they are criminally indicted, as were the Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 

and its officials for "denying employment to those in another state".

The indictments charge the use of a label (union label) to identify products. But to guard against chiselers and cheats, did not the government itself urge the public to buy goods bearing the Blue 

Eagle Label of the NRA? And was anyone criminally indicted for this? What was done under the NRA was considered a virtue. But now it is a crime, Mr. Arnold says.

IMPROVED EQUIPMENT 

"Improved equipment or more efficient methods" brings up the whole question of labor-destroying devices and machines. Workers do not want to stop progress. They simply want to share in what is called progress. They want to share more in the benefits of machines and improvements not by going jobless. And they object to being crushed by the labor-destroying kinds of inventions. 

Efficiency is all right in its place. But human beings, human character, are more important than efficiency. It is not wisdom when efficiency brings misery.

Students of the subject know that human labor is being rapidly eliminated just as surely as the horse has been removed from the streets by the 18

automobile and auto-truck. President Roosevelt himself recently said to Congress: "To face the task of finding jobs faster than inventions can take them away is not defeatism. . . . We must press on to attain full employment for those who have been displaced by machines.”

Consider this: Today the Bell Telephone System employs about 20% less employees than in 1930. 

Yet it has more telephone stations in use—more average daily calls and greater profits. (Dynamic 

America, July 29, 1939) Henry Epstein, New York

State Solicitor General, had this to say: "In the period of 1929-35, about 185,000 workers were dismissed by that corporation . despite maintenance of the famous $9 dividends, of $52,000,000 increased dividend payments, 185,000 workers were discharged. Efficiency, mechanical improvements, etc., all contributed. "Take the relative social deficit in the destitution of 185,000 families as against the social value of improved gadgets and the cash dividends to a few 

Times, Nov. 26, 1939). 99 (Letter to N. Y.

The Appellate Division of the New York State 

Supreme Court recently noted that employers do not use the latest improvements when these would destroy their investments. This court held that 19

unions are not required to submit to "the disadvantages which flow from the introduction of such improvements". The court said: "To prevent unemployment is a legitimate labor objective. . . . This right should be entitled to the same protection as is the right of an employer to use machinery for the purpose of making a profit." (Opera on Tour, Inc., v. Weber, et al.)

Recently new stockings for women appeared, the press reported. They were processed to wear indefinitely. But they were withdrawn from the market almost at once-because profits of many manufacturers would be hit. This apparently was 

NOT an "unreasonable restraint of trade". But when union workers try to save their jobs-their bread and butter-that IS an “unreasonable restraint", says Mr. Arnold. 

2. UNREASONABLE RESTRAINTS TO COMPEL THE HIRING OF USE- LESS AND UNNECESSARY LABOR. 

Mr. Arnold gives an example of an extra man on a delivery truck. But the principles is what matters—not Mr. Arnold's example. And just who is to say what is "useless and unnecessary" labor? 

Who is to decide the proper speed of a worker?

Surely not lawyers and judges who never did a day's manual labor in their lives? And who will decide the borderline of exploitation? 20

Two men working 6 hours each often means “useless and unnecessary" labor—if one man can be induced or forced to work 12 hours. And what about the older workers being thrown on to the human scrap heap? Is it better for human beings to starve than to have "unnecessary" labor? 

And what about unnecessary profit? If Mr. Arnold and his assistants are to decide what is unnecessary labor, why not also decide what is unnecessary profit?

When the General Electric, Westinghouse and others pay low wages and show huge, unnecessary profit that is NOT an "unreasonable restraint".

And when lighting fixture manufacturers in other states pay poverty wages so as to make unnecessary profit, that is not an "unreasonable restraint”. 

But when union electrical workers say, "Buy our labor so we may eat; we get little work; don't drag us down to the lower paid workers" then that IS an "unreasonable restraint". It creates "useless and unnecessary" labor, Mr. Arnold claims. So they are criminally indicted, as were the Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 and its officials.

Anyway, was there any "hiring of useless and unnecessary labor" under the NRA, WPA and other government agencies? How many men were pictured as leaning on rakes, picks and shovels and "resting" in swamps? Was this any “unjustifiable tax on industry"? And was anyone criminally indicted for this? And are men now to be sent to prison for fighting starvation? 21

The indictments against Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 and its officials do not charge any present officer or representative with graft, extortion, fraud or violence. District Attorney Dewey also fully investigated and found none of these.

The indictments are based solely on the charge that the union and its officials conspired to obtain, spread and maintain work for members and that the union has a "monopoly and control of the supply of labor".

However, let us add that certain types of men are just as revolting to union people as to anyone else. There is no thought of trying to whitewash any betraying official-whether in an old union or a new fly-by-night union. But realize that for every one such man in unions, a dozen of his kind have been and can be found in public life, on the bench, in the legal, business, financial and even the religious worlds. Surely labor unions have had no monopoly on human weakness and cussedness. 

But it is unfortunate that the general cry of "racketeer" is used to conceal and confuse the attack on unions. And it does seem a sad day, doesn't it, when such matters cannot be dealt 22

with under the many criminal laws? Samuel Untermeyer did so.

Thomas Dewey did so. Both won much success and fame. And they did not find it necessary to use a 50 year old federal law-or any state law-designed against trusts. 

When court judges have been caught selling their decisions for cash the antitrust law was not used against them. Such law was not used to convict dishonest lawyers, politicians, government officials, stock manipulators, ministers and bank officers. 

Surely we have not so soon forgotten the Falls, Dohenys, Sinclairs, Forbes, Whitneys, Mantons, Costers and many others.

But when Mr. Arnold uses the antitrust law against a betraying labor official the whole union is dragged in. And why thus cause the workers to foot the huge bills for defense of a betraying official?

Besides, competent attorneys claim that when convictions are obtained under the antitrust law in Mr. Arnold's cases, this then lays the basis for more private injunctions and suits for treble 'damages" by individuals and employers. 66 

JOHN SMITH'S CASE 

Mr. Arnold says he uses the antitrust law against the "labor racketeers" because "you do not have to prove corruption to bring them within the 

Sherman Act. You only have to prove they are 23

unreasonable". But when a man is prosecutednot for being corrupt—but for some labor activity which Mr. Arnold considers unreasonable, the man's position is strengthened. 

For instance: John Smith is believed to be corrupt. But John opposes handling articles produced in another state by non-union workers at lower wages. Or he opposes a new invention that puts more men on the street. Or John demands the boss hire some "useless and unnecessary" labor. Perhaps John wants an extra man simply to ride on a delivery truck. This may seem wasteful-but to the extra man it may mean food for himself and his children.

So John is prosecuted-not for being corruptbut to prove he was unreasonable. Just imagine how union members now feel about John-especially when many are jobless. And imagine how 

Mr. Arnold himself would feel if he had been tramping the streets begging for work. Surely he knows the first law of life is to live-and that the world of brutal actualities is quite different from the world found in a classroom at Yale. 

5. UNREASONABLE RESTRAINTS TO DESTROY AN ESTABLISHED SYS- TEM OF COLLECTIVE BARGAINING. 

Mr. Arnold says this covers jurisdiction disputes and strikes. These occur between unions over which one shall do certain work or jobs. For instance: For many years, when the new York Edi- 24

son Company built a power house it had a contractor do the electrical construction work with members of Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3. 

Later the Edison employees formed a union and the company wanted to use these employees to do this work at 50% less wages.

There are several types of jurisdiction disputes. 

Here is one type used for organizing purposes:

In the building field the C.I.O. offers to work for $3 a day below the A. F. of L. men—and the

C.I.O. also offers to work longer hours under poorer conditions. 

Here is how a "jurisdiction" dispute can be created to wreck unions: There are union trades on a building large or small. The builder signs an agreement with the C.I.O. at lower wages and hires some of its men. The union trades strike. 

Then they and their officials can be criminally indicted for "destroying an established system of collective bargaining". Seems fantastic doesn't it?

But it is all too true. Mr. Berge, aid to Arnold, made this quite plain at the Rand School, New 

York, March 29, 1940.

However, labor has never before heard that jurisdiction strikes are unlawful under the antitrust law. And labor has certainly never had a monopoly of such disputes. Various states, many departments of government, courts and even churches are constantly involved in jurisdiction disputes of one variety or another. 25

The United States was born in a fight over jurisdiction. Cities, towns and villages often squabble over territorial jurisdiction and against encroachment. It seems that a democracy is impossible without jurisdiction disputes of various kinds. 

Of course such disputes between labor unions do harm. Bad feeling is created all around. Almost all humans deplore and condemn them—the same as they do war. But humans still go to war. And such disputes and strikes are a form of war- -a war mostly for jobs. Provide enough jobs for men and you will hear little or nothing of such disputes.

MR. ARNOLD'S AMAZING CLAIMS 

Can He Be Serious in These? 

Robert Jackson, U. S. Attorney General, once occupied the position now held by Thurman 

Arnold. Mr. Jackson then made a speech saying the Sherman Antitrust Law was "perverted" when applied to labor. Mr. Arnold approved that speech in his book "The Folklore of Capitalism". Now 

HE applies the law to labor. He has now reversed himself so completely as to say: "Labor has been lead to believe that the Sherman Act is a weapon given to their enemies. 

Nothing can be farther from the truth than this belief. . . Properly construed the 

Act is a charter of freedom for labor." (Written speech to American Labor Club, New 

York, Jan. 27, 1940.) 

No wonder the thoughtful worker asks: "Say, what is this anyway?" But surely Mr. Arnold could hardly be serious. Surely he knows about the labor-crushing decisions the millions spent in defense and the deadly blows dealt labor before he left Yale. He could hardly be ignorant of such things as "yellow dog" contracts and broken strikes. 27

Debs, Gompers, Morrison, Mitchell and other past leaders and their unions-hit because of the Sherman Act-never seemed to find anyone to "properly construe" the Act. When Debs sat in the 

Woodstock, Illinois, prison he hardly thought the 

Act was any "charter of freedom". Neither did 

Gompers and the others who faced prison. And no present day leader or union member has yet discovered any such "charter".

Of course Mr. Arnold is doing the "construing" just now with many criminal indictments. And countless judges have been doing their construing of the Sherman Act since 1890 and not one ever found Mr. Arnold's "charter of freedom". Judge

Hadley once said: “We are lost in a jungle of conflicting decisions. We don't know where we are." 

CLAIMS HE MUST PROSECUTE 

Mr. Arnold amazingly claims he has no "right to exempt labor from all prosecutions. . . . We have no choice in this matter." In effect he also adds: "Blame the law, not me." Again he could hardly be serious. There is a big difference between the law and Mr. Arnold's opinion of it. His whole crusade is based on opinion. And his opinion differs greatly from that of many learned judges and attorneys. His witch-hunting goes far beyond any bona-fide application of the law. 

Mr. Jackson, now U. S. Attorney General, and dozens before him refused to prosecute labor under the Sherman Antitrust Act. All of them 28

seemed to have a choice. As far back as 1895, Attorney General Olney-when urged to prosecute the Tonawanda Lumber Shovers Union-wrote that this would be "a perversion of a law from the real purpose of its authors". He reported to 

Congress that this "puts the whole power of the federal government on one side of a civil controversy of doubtful merits."

Congress certainly showed that it did not want any such prosecutions to occur. For Congressfrom 1913 to 1930-each year put a proviso in the sundry appropriations bill to prevent the Department of Justice from using any funds to prosecute labor under the antitrust law. 

SAYS GO TO CONGRESS AGAIN 

If his "interpretation is wrong", says Mr. Arnold, "labor will have a concrete case to present to Congress for relief instead of the general claim for exemption." He adds: "If any group wishes the Sherman Act repealed or amended the place to urge it is upon Congress. That is the only body that can give them any relief.” 

How utterly ridiculous! Congress in three instances clearly tried to extend relief to laborpassage of the Clayton Act (1914)-preventing funds from being used to prosecute-and passage of the Norris-LaGuardia Act (1932). Just what more should Congress do? Could it amend the 

Sherman Act to prevent the courts from interpreting the Act against labor? 29

Congress seemed to do all the amending necessary when it adopted the Clayton Act. Labor then believed the matter had been settled for all time. 

Samuel Gompers then hailed this amendment as labors' "Magna Carta". During the debate at that time, Congressman Henry said: "We are now about to correct the error and make it plain and specific, by clearcut and direct language, that anti-trust laws against conspiracies in trade shall not be applied to labor organizations and farmers unions."

And could another amendment prevent a lawyer like Mr. Arnold from getting a government appointment, then reversing himself and "construing" the law or amendment against labor? But

Congress could again prevent certain funds going to the Department of Justice. It could demand the removal of Mr. Arnold. 

Congress could demand that Attorney General

Jackson have all indictments dismissed against unions and their officials for alleged violations of the antitrust law. It could impeach any federal judge who "construed" the antitrust law against labor. It could refuse to confirm the appointment of any more corporation lawyers to be federal judges or officials.

But Mr. Arnold knows about the timing of things. 

He knows the cold realities of the situation. He knows labor had plenty of "concrete cases" to present to Congress before it adopted the Clayton Act and the Norris-LaGuardia Act. He knows of 30

the many years labor struggled to get Congress to extend relief by those acts. 

Now he would have labor make the same long and costly struggle all over again—perhaps with doubtful results while he goes merrily on trying to bankrupt and hamstring unions and imprison their officials. His position is much like the fellow who said: "If you don't like what I am doing, then go get the U. S. Constitution amended." 

CLAIMS SUPPORT OF JUDGES 

Mr. Arnold says: "We are not subjecting to criminal prosecution practices which can be justified even under the dissenting opinions of the United States Supreme Court." But he apparently ignores much of what these dissenting opinions say. The dissenting judges did more than merely disagree as to the "unreasonableness" of certain labor activities.

They believed that it made no difference whether an activity was considered reasonable or unreasonble that labor unions do not come under the antitrust law at all. In the Duplex Printing Case, Supreme Court Justice Brandeis joined by 

Holmes and Clarke-wrote: 66 "... The Clayton Act substituted the opinion of Congress for that of differing judges. ...

Congress, not the judges, was the body which should declare what public policy in regard to the industrial struggle demands... 31

it is not for judges to determine the limits of permissible contest." 

Later, in the Bedford Cut Stone Case, Justice

Brandeis wrote: 

"The serious question on which the court divided in the Duplex Case was not whether the restraint imposed was reasonable 99 So it seems clear that Mr. Arnold is attempting to put his own ideas into the law. He is substituting his opinion for that of Congress, Justices 

Brandeis, Holmes, Clarke and others. HE is attempting to decide "what public policy in regard to the industrial struggle demands" and "the limits of permissible contest". He also goes against what the New York Court of Appeals held: "The interests of labor and capital are at times inimical and the courts may not decide controversies between the parties so long as neither resorts to violence, deceit or misrepresentation to bring about desired results." (Stillwell Theatres v. Kaplan.)

CLAIMS HE PROSECUTES NO LEGITIMATE ACTIVITIES 

Here is real confusion for the uninformed: Mr. 

Arnold repeatedly claims he is prosecuting ONLY those labor activities "which are not legitimate” -and "only such boycotts, strikes or coercion by labor unions as have no reasonable connection 32

with wages, hours," etc. Then just why are the 

Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 and its officials being prosecuted? 

The activities complained of go direct to the very heart of maintaining wages and hours. They are clearly a struggle of union people to survive. But what about the following kind of boycott? Is it a legitimate labor activity? And does it have any "reasonable connection" with wages and hours? 

Here it is: "The Smoked Fish Workers Union of New 

York had attempted to unionize... a smoked fish producer (in New Jersey). Finding its efforts in New Jersey expensive and ineffective, the union picketed those retail stores in 

New York City which purchased the product with signs announcing to the public that the smoked fish was produced under nonunion conditions. . ... "Such secondary picketing has been upheld by the highest court of the State of New York.

However, the non-union producer rushed to the U. S. Attorney . . with the result that the union was threatened with criminal prosecution for the simple and legal act of orderly picketing. "The union not having the financial resources (to fight long criminal prosecution) surrendered its legal right to picket peacefully." (Louis P. Goldberg, American Federationist, March, 1940.) 333

When Mr. Arnold was asked how his claims could stand up against the simple facts in such cases as the above and more could be cited-he wrote: "After careful consideration of these matters, there appears to be no reason why the Department of Justice should not proceed to take whatever steps appear to be necessary with respect to this situation."

A REASONABLE STRIKE? 

What about this kind of strike? Is it a legitimate labor activity? And does it have any "reasonable connection" with wages and hours? Here it is: "The witness, Mr. Morgan (fur manufacturer) has told the court that in 1933 a strike was called in his shop after his refusal to sign a contract with the Fur Workers Union.

The witness has answered that approximately 85% of his finished products are shipped out of the state. 

Judge: 'I don't see what this has to do with interstate commerce.' 

Henderson (prosecutor for Arnold): "There is a strike and it affects interstate commerce.' 

Judge: 'Does the government contend that any man engaged in interstate commerse and he has a strike, that that affects interstate commerce?' 

Henderson: 'Yes.' 34

Judge: 'Mr. Henderson, suppose tomorrow I go into the manufacturing business. . . . Do you mean to say that if a labor union leader comes to me and honestly and honorably says: 'We want our union in here and if you don't take our union we will strike'-does that affect interstate commerce?' 

Henderson: 'It does if the strike absolutely cripples his business.' 

Judge:... If it does then every industry of the United States is subject to federal jurisdiction without any conspiracy charge.'" 

(Excerpts from Legislative Letter of National 

Emergency Conference for Democratic Rights, March 15, 1940.)

Here we have Mr. Arnold's representative—prosecutor Henderson-boldly repudiating all claims made about strikes for wages and hours and about "legitimate labor activities”. One can now realize -as the Conference for Democratic Rights says "that this contention of the Department of Justice would smash labor from one end of the country to the other."

REGARDING NON-UNION MADE GOODS 

Mr. Arnold wrote that he "instructed the attorneys in the building investigation not to institute criminal prosecutions" when unions refuse "to work upon goods made in non-union shops". But why did he send a letter and questionnaire (Feb. 35

2, 1940) to non-union electrical shops throughout the country saying: "Your helpful cooperation in this matter will be greatly appreciated”?

He asked them to relate any troubles they ever had with Electrical Workers Local Union No. 3 and its officials. (But he did not ask the union to relate any troubles it had with such concerns.) 

Here are some of the questions he asked: "Has Local No. 3 ever interfered with the installation of any of your products in New 

York City?" "As a result of any rule or regulation of Local 

No. 3 were you ever forced by representatives of this union to rewire any of your products which were shipped to customers in 

New York City?" “As a result of any rule or regulation of Local 

No. 3 . . were you ever forced by represen tatives of this union to ship any of your products to customers in New York City in an unassembled or unwired condition?” "What are the unit costs for wiring each type of product manufactured by your company, if wired at the factory?" "What are the unit costs for wiring each type of product manufactured by your company, if wired in New York City by union electricians who are members of Local No. 3 ... ?" 36

One may see from those questions whether Mr.

Arnold was serious in what he said about working "upon goods made in non-union shops". Also one can see how a union and its officials are charged with conspiring to enforce "Unreasonable restraints to compel the hiring of useless and unnecessary labor"-and for "denying employment to those in another state".

CLAIMS HE HELPS LABOR 

Here are more amazing claims of Mr. Arnold: "Our interpretation is a distinct advantage to labor. . . . In taking this position we are on the side of labor. . . . The only remedy for the evils of private policing (private suits) is to substitute policing by public officers. . . "The prosecution policy of the Antitrust Division will be a protection of the right of labor to organize.. The Antitrust Division will utilize its labor prosecutions to take the yoke off the back of labor. "The resulting sound trade unions will draw the teeth of labor-baiters 99 (Written speech to American Labor Club, New York, Jan. 27, 1940.) 

What burning affection for labor! What a day of promise! Labor will not even have to decide what is good for it! The yoke is to be lifted! The teeth of labor-baiters are to be drawn! No more “racke- 37

teers", damage suits, injunctions, lockouts, blacklists, labor spies or wars on unions! 

Too bad Mr. Arnold did not also promise to wipe out unemployment and child labor-and to see that cheating employers stop paying adults as low as 10¢ an hour when the federal law sets a minimum of 30¢.

But it seems the same kind of affection was expressed for the Czechs, Poles and others. It seemed "necessary to protect" them from their leaders to jail or shoot the leaders. It is even said that "at times Satan has cited the Sermon on the Mount to serve his purposes."

POLICING BY PUBLIC OFFICERS 

However, labor had the right to organize for over 50 years before the Wagner Act-long before Mr. 

Arnold came forward. And not one private antitrust suit against labor has been withdrawn-or any such assurance or hopes given-since his crusade began. In fact, as already stated, competent attorneys claim that when Mr. Arnold obtains criminal convictions this will lay the basis for more private injunctions and suits for treble "damages" by individuals and employers.

Besides, labor had a good taste of "policing by public officers" long before Mr. Arnold began setting his example. Public officers often have been more oppressive-broken up more union meetings prevented more peaceful picketing — and 

38

violated more civil liberties than the worst types of employers ever hoped to do.

So the informed union members would doubtless prefer "private policing" (private suits) anytime to Mr. Arnold's ideas of "policing by public officers". Informed union members will also probably recall that under the most unfriendly administrations under Harding, Coolidge and Hoover-they never knew anything so vicious and brazen as Mr. Arnold's sugar-coated drive against unions, all to "help labor".

But organized labor has enough such friends. Tom 

Girdler, head of Republic Steel, says: “We have never been against organized labor and no one in the company is opposed to organized labor." (N. Y. Times, April 11, 1940.)

Even Walter Gordon Merritt-the notorious laborbaiting attorney who has made his fame and fortune fighting unions-insists he is also a friend of labor unions: "By Mr. Walsh: ‘And you claim here and now to be a sincere and real believer of labor unions, do you, Mr. Merritt? . . . "Answer: Most decidedly . . . I think it is one of the greatest institutions in our democracy " (Testimony, May 3, 1938, in Federal Court, New York City.)
