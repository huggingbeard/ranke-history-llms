TEXAS YOUTH COUNCIL Statutory Responsibilities, Purposes and Organization 

Article 5143d, V.C.S., enacted by the 55th Legislature, created the 

Texas Youth Council and charged it with the responsibility of administering the State's correctional facilities for delinquent children, providing a program of constructive. care, treatment, education and training aimed at the rehabilitation and re-establishment in society of children adjudged delinquent by the courts of Texas and committed to the Council, and providing active parole supervision of delinquent children released from the State Training Schools. The statute also vested in the Texas Youth Council responsibility for the administration and operation of the State Homes for dependent and neglected children and responsibility for the "coordination and combination of such facilities" and for "the creation of new facilities within the appropriations provided by the Legislature. 11 The Texas Youth Council consists of a three-member policy-making board appointed by the Governor of Texas with the consent of the State Senate. 

Members of the Texas Youth Council receive no pay for their services and must be "influential citizens in their respective communities who are recognized for their interest in youth." The Executive Director, appointed by the Council, is the administrative head of the agency, and the powers, duties and functions granted to or imposed on the Texas Youth Council by any provision of law may be delegated to and performed by the Executive Director. 

The statute directs the Council to report to the Governor and the Legislature its activities, accomplishments, findings and needs and provides that the Council's "report shall include specific recommendations for legislation, planned and drafted as part of an integrated, unified and consistent program to serve the best interest of the State and the youth committed to

the Texas Youth Council. 

This report, therefore, will attempt not only to make lucid Council activities and accomplishments with appropriated funds and other available resources but also to analyze problems presented by the children and youth committed to the care and custody of the Council by the courts of the State. 

Some Activities and Accomplishments of the Texas Youth Council for the Fiscal Year Ended August 31, 1965 The Texas Youth Council is pleased to report that for the fiscal year ended August 31, 1965, State funds appropriated for the use of this agency have made possible continued progress and improvements in physical plants, personnel and training programs of all institutions and facilities under its administrative jurisdiction. The Council's programs for rehabilitation of young offenders and its programs for care and training of dependent and neglected children are considered to be investments in more productive, selfsupporting citizens. These programs could not have been achieved without the genuine understanding and concern of the Governor and the Legislature or without their continued support of the Council's long-range plans to meet these vital functions of State Government. 

The Council is especially pleased to report that its programs of education and rehabilitation for delinquent youngsters have continued to return dividends in which the people of Texas can take justifiable pride. 

In addition to providing fully accredited academic and vocational education for all delinquent youngsters committed to its care and custody, the Council now conducts formal high school commencement exercises for those who complete the necessary requirements for high school graduation while in the 

Training Schools. 

- 2 -

The Council is pleased to point out that of the number of youngsters released on parole under the supervision of TYC Parole Officers, during the period covered by this report, 58% returned to regular attendance in the public schools while an additional 28% of the parolees were placed in gainful full-time employment utilizing skills learned while in the Training Schools. 

These accomplishments are especially significant when compared with the virtually 100% drop-out, unemployment and delinquency records of these youngsters prior to commitment to the Texas Youth Council.

Another significant dividend has been realized by the comtinued progressive reduction of broken paroles from the previous average of 42% to 25% in the areas where TYC Parole Officers were assigned. This is the lowest recidivism rate for juvenile offenders in the history of the state. 

The Council also is gratified to report that our follow-up records indicate a drastic reduction in the number of ex-students from the State Training Schools who are subsequently brought into adult courts for offenses which require confinement in State prisons. Only 3.9% of the persons committed to the Texas Department of Corrections during the period covered by this report had ever been wards of the Texas Youth Council at any time. 

The Texas Youth Council has developed and established policies, procedures and long-range plans for the efficient and economical accomplishments of its statutory responsibilities. 

Effective administrative supervision and leadership have been provided in evaluating, organizing and coordinating programs and operations of all institutions and facilities of the agency, and the Council also has provided leadership for counties and communities of the state in the area of delinquency and crime prevention through consultations, conferences, workshops, and the distribution of literature. Local initiative and leadership in action programs by communities, cities and counties have

STATE TRAINING SCHOOLS FOR DELINQUENT CHILDREN 

The Council has continued its careful study of the multitude of problems presented by delinquent youngsters committed to the agency by the courts of the state, analyzed these problems and attempted to meet them with both immediate and long-range programs designed to meet the criteria of good child-caring practices and treatment, education and training appropriate to the responsibilities of state government. 

The Council has succeeded in providing programs in the institutions under its jurisdiction which, according to expert evaluations, are a credit to the State of Texas. The Council will exert every effort to improve these programs as new knowledge and new resources become available. The Council is convinced, however, that good institutional programs must be followed by adequate parole supervision, resulting in continued education or gainful full-time employment for all youngsters, if maximum benefits and economy are to be realized from its efforts and expenditures. 

The Council currently is providing fully accredited academic and vocational education for all delinquent children in its custody. All of the 

State Training Schools for delinquent children have Independent School District status and meet the standards established by the Texas Education Agency for elementary and secondary schools of the state. 

On the campuses of the various State Training Schools the Council has established seven (7) separate fully accredited academic and vocational school plants for boys and two (2) accredited academic and vocational schools for girls--one school on each State Training School campus. These facilities are in full operation eleven (11) months each year for delinquent children and attendance is compulsory. During the month of August each year, however, - 6 -

other constructive activities are scheduled at all schools in order that the teachers might take annual vacations and the youngsters may be permitted a change of pace.

All academic school work completed in the State Training Schools can be applied coward the completion of the requirements of a high school diploma. 

Credits earned can be transferred, at full value, to the local high school in the home town of any boy or girl at the time of release from the Training 

School. 

The type and quality of instruction provided in the State Training Schools is comparable to the type and quality of instruction found in other public schools of the state. The teachers, counselors, supervisors and principals must meet the same educational and proficiency standards as are required for employment by other accredited school districts of the state and all personnel are paid at the same rate as personnel employed in the independent school district of the city nearest the Training School. 

Remedial instruction is stressed at all levels and each school has its own staff of social workers and counselors. The teacher-pupil ratio ranges from one to twenty-five (1 to 25) in certain classes down to a ratio of one-to-one (1 to 1) in certain types of remedial classes where glaring deficiencies require individual instruction. 

The Council considers education essential to successful rehabilitation of delinquent children and special stress has been placed on preparing the "drop-outs" from the public schools for return to the public schools when they are released from the State Training Schools. If this goal is to be realized, the Council must continue to enrich its programs and to provide instruction which will enable youngsters not only to re-enter the public schools in grades appropriate to their ages but also to work competitively and successfully along with their peers in the community. - 7 -

Admissions to Training Schools during 1964-65 

During the fiscal year ended August 31, 1965, the Texas Youth Council received 2,720 delinquent children into its training school facilities. A total of 2,211 boys were admitted into the Reception Center, where they were classified and 2,141 were placed in the five (5) diversified units comprising the Gatesville State Schools for Boys and 70 chronic, serious offenders were placed in the Mountain View School for Boys. (In addition, 63 students from the Gatesville Schools were re-classified during the year and transferred to the Mountain View School.) The Gainesville State School for Girls received 

357 and the Crockett State School for Girls received 152, making a total of 509 delinquent girls admitted into Youth Council facilities during the year. 

According to type of admission, there were 2,036 youngsters received for the first time as New Commitments (1584 at the Gatesville Schools, 69 at the Mountain View School, 264 at the Gainesville School and 119 at the Crockett

School). In addition, 680 children who had been in the training schools before were returned as parole violators (555 to the Gatesville Schools, 1 to the Mountain View School, 91 to the Gainesville School and 33 to the Crockett 

School). Also, four youngsters were recommitted following discharge, two boys to Gatesville and two girls to Gainesville.

The increases in New Commitments (2,036 total new commitments) during the period covered by this report, represents a 15% increase for boys and a 1% increase for girls over the number received during the preceding fiscal period.

Effects of Parole Violations on Training School Programs 

The Council has found that rehabilitation of juvenile delinquents cannot be accomplished completely within the confines of a State Training School program. Such training must be followed by effective parole supervision in the community. Unless adequate and intensive supervision is provided for the children placed on parole, constructive progress made while in the Training School frequently is lost within the first few weeks after release, and both the money and effort expended at the Training School are a total loss to the state as well as to the child. 

One of the most serious problems faced by the Texas Youth Council through the years has been the return of youngsters to the State Training

PAROLE SUPERVISION BY THE TEXAS YOUTH COUNCIL 

The first state-financed juvenile parole program in Texas began September 1, 1961. The 57th Legislature provided funds for a Director of 

Parole, to be headquartered in the Central Office in Austin, and five parole officers, to be located in the five large cities of the state as specified by the Legislature. The 58th Legislature provided funds for the employment of ten additional officers and they were employed during the 1964-65 biennium. 11.

The 59th Legislature granted funds for fifteen additional parole officers to be employed during the 1966-67 biennium. Therefore, by the end of August, 1967, a staff of thirty parcie officers will be supervising TYC parolees placed in twentynine (29) counties of the state.

Even though the parole program has not been in operation long enough for its full potential to be realized, the Council is able to report substantial decreases in the number of broken paroles from areas where TYC parole officers were assigned. During the period covered by this report, 1474 wards were placed on parole in TYC supervised areas. The rate of broken paroles for boys in these areas had averaged 42% during the decade preceding the beginning of the TYC parole program but was reduced to 25% during the period covered by this report. 

The Council estimates that a monetary saving to the State of Texas of at least $300,000 in care and maintenance expenditures was achieved through the efforts of the State Juvenile Parole Program of the Texas Youth Council for the year ended August 31, 1965. 

Twenty-three hundred and seventy-two (2,372) wards of the Texas Youth

Council were on parole at the beginning of the period covered by this report, and twenty-six hundred and twenty-eight (2,628) youngsters were paroled from the State Training Schools during the year. A total of five thousand (5,000) 

Youth Council wards received parole supervision during the period covered by this report. On August 31, 1965, there were twenty-seven hundred and fiftytwo (2,752) wards on parole under the legal custody of the Texas Youth Council.

Sixty-two percent (62%) of the boys and twenty-six percent (26%) of the girls were being supervised by TYC parole officers. Courtesy supervision by volunteers and county officials was secured for those youngsters not under the direct supervision of TYC personnel. - 12.

THE STATE HOMES FOR CHILDREN 

Even though the physical facilities are not new and require considerable repair, the Texas Youth Council is pleased to report that the child care program in Texas State Homes for Children have improved to the degree that people of our state can take pride in comparing them favorably with any--public or private--in the nation. Funds appropriated by the Legislature have allowed the Council and its staffs at the Children's Homes to plan, accomplish and maintain high standards of child care, essential services for education and training of children, and necessary placement services for children leaving these institutions when individual needs for group care have been alleviated. 

Waco State Home 

The Legislature, for the period covered by this report, appropriated funds for the care, education and training of three hundred thirty (330) dependent and neglected children at the Waco State Home and four hundred twenty-one (421) individual children were actually served by the Home during this period by the multi-service programs provided. 

On August 31, 1965, the Waco State Home was serving two hundred and eighty-eight (288) children with the funds provided. Two hundred thirty-nine (239) were in residence at the Home; ten were in paid foster homes; and thirty-eight (38) were with their own relatives in temporary home trial placements, pre-adoptive placements, free foster homes, college, or various job placements.

Efforts to learn more about each child and his or her family have been greatly accelerated and every effort has been made to return each child to his or her own family, or other appropriate placement, as soon as practicable in the best interest of the child. - 16 -

All children of scholastic age at the Waco State Home attend Waco Public Schools on an annual contract basis, and the cost to the State is much less than it would be for the Home to operate its own school system. 

The children have made rapid and satisfying progress and adjustment in all public school activities, religious activities, athletic and recreational programs. 

Corsicana State Home 

The Legislature, for the period covered by this report, appropriated funds for the care, education and training of three hundred thirty (330) dependent and neglected children at the Corsicana State Home and four hundred fifty (450) individual children were actually served by the Home during this period by the multi-service programs provided. 

On August 31, 1965, the Corsicana State Home was providing group care for two hundred and ninety-five (295) children in a home-like atmosphere where each child could be cared for according to his or her individual needs. A multi-service program outside the Home was providing care for another eightyfive (85) children, nine of them in paid foster homes. Many children were placed outside the Home during the period covered by this report and other children were accepted into the vacancies so created, thus allowing the Home to serve a maximum number of children with the funds provided. 

Children of scholastic age at the Corsicana State Home attend the Corsicana Public Schools on an annual contract basis, and the cost is much less than it would be for the Home to operate its own school system. The educational advantages and opportunities for community participation are considerably greater for children of the Home than would have been possible had the Home continued to operate its own schools. Public school teachers and authorities have assisted and encouraged children from the Home to

Children were received into the Training Schools from 174 Texas counties and four parolees were returned from other states during the year ended August 31, 1965. Based on population groupings by Juvenile Court ages (10-17 for boys, 10-18 for girls), admitting counties included all twenty-five with juvenile populations of 10,000 and more, as well as 130 counties with 1,000 to 9,999 children, and 19 counties with less than 1,000 children.

Texas five largest counties, with 40% of the total juvenile population, accounted for 47% of total admissions and 45% of all parole violators returned during the year. These percentage figures compare with 

43% total admissions and 44% parole violators returned during the year ended August 31, 1964, while containing 39% of the state's juvenile population. 

The twenty next-most-populous grouping of counties had 27% of the state's juvenile population and contributed 28% of all admissions and 31% of all those returned from parole. Counties (162) within the 1,000 to 9,999 grouping sent 24% of all admissions and 22% of the parole violators, while counties (67) with less than 1,000 children sent 1% each of total admissions and parole violators. (The remaining 1% of the parole violators were returned from four other states.) The latter two groups of counties encompassed 

31% and 2% respectively of the state's Juvenile Court age children. 

Comparison of returns from parole during the current year with those during the previous year reveals the following numerical differences: the five most populous returned 33 more children (a 12% increase), the 20 next-most-populous returned 13 more (a 6% increase), the 162 next counties returned 15 more (an 11% increase), and the last group of 67 small counties returned five children as compared to six returned last year (a 17% decrease).

A comparison of first admissions (included above in "total admissions") during the current year and those during the previous year reveal some interesting numerical differences: 184 more new children were sent from the five largest counties (a 24% increase); from the next twenty counties there were twenty-one fewer children sent for the first time (a 4% decrease); the 162 next-most-populous counties sent thirty-nine more children for the first time (a 9% increase); and from the last group there were 

10 more children sent for their first admission (a 50% increase).
