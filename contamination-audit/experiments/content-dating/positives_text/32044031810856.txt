CHARITABLE TRUSTS AND SOLICITATIONS INTRODUCTION 

In the past few years, numerous changes have taken place in the supervision and enforcement of charitable foundations. These changes have had a substantial effect upon Attorneys General because of their historic involvement in the field. Further, Congress is now considering legislation that, among other things, would entitle the various states to federal funds for auditing of charitable foundations. With this background in mind, Attorney General 

William J. Brown, Ohio, requested that the Executive Committee of the National 

Association of Attorneys General create a Subcommittee on Charitable Trusts and Solicitation to the Consumer Protection Committee in June of 1973. Attor ney General Richard Turner of Iowa, Chairman of the Consumer Protection Com mittee, named General Brown as Chairman of the Subcommittee. General Brown sent a letter to the Attorneys General of all states inquiring whether or not they were interested in participating in the work of the Subcommittee: the responses were overwhelmingly favorable. 

November, 1973

In January of 1974, a special NAAG meeting will specifically deal with Attorneys Generals' problems in the enforcement and regulation of charitable trusts. It is thought that this report will initially provide an exchange of information among the states and lay the ground work for the work of the Subcommittee.

At the Subcommittee's request, COAG circulated a questionnaire on charitable trusts to Attorneys General on August 9. The questionnaires were based in part on a draft prepared by the Ohio Attorney General's Office. Forty-two jurisdictions completed the questionnaire. The questionnaire asked questions dealing with staff, enforcement authority, number of trusts and assets, use of standard forms and data processing, and problem areas. 

This report will present the results of that questionnaire as well as future federal-state activities that may affect the Attorneys Generals' role in the field. The report also contains an appendix which provides examples of standard forms and a brief historical background of the Attorney Generals' involvement in the regulation of charitable foundations, reprinted from a report on The Office of Attorney General. 

CHARITABLE FOUNDATIONS Administration and Enforcement 

Enforcement Authority. In most American jurisdictions, primary or exclusive authority for the supervision and enforcement of charitable trusts is vested by statute and case law in the Attorney General. In addition, the laws of some reporting states allow for the enforcement of charitable trusts by donors, settlors, representatives of the charity or class of beneficiaries, any interested parties and a fixed number of interested parties.

Fremont-Smith points out that it is generally impractical to allow individuals of varying interests and rights to challenge charitable funds designed to promote large public interests. The Attorney General is the proper party to bring suit. While he is the proper party, he is not, however, the exclusive one. Members of the public may bring suit when they can establish that they are beneficiaries of the trust with interests beyond those of the general public. The Attorney General is usually considered a necessary party to such suits, however, in those states where he has the duty of enforcing charitable funds under statute, court rule, or precedent. There is no clearcut rule as to whether the state court will consider him a necessary party, but case law of the jurisdiction must be consulted. 1

Standard Reporting and Registration Forms. Several states have employed standard reporting and registration forms in their regulation of charitable trusts and of solicitation and fund raising. Examples are given in the Appendix to this report.

A basic method of control and enforcement of charitable trusts and solicitations is to require that all such foundations be registered with the state. 

Usual provisions require each foundation which is subject to that state's regulatory law, to file a standard instrument with the proper state agency. This provides a floor upon which effective enforcement is built, and it tends to make trustees aware of their public duty. 

Registration forms usually call for information regarding the charitable organization's name, history and directors or trustees. Most forms also call for a description and value of the trust assets as well as the stated purpose of the trust. If specific persons are to be recipients of benefits under the trust, then they must also be listed. The principal reason for such forms is to allow states to identify which organizations are operating in their states. 

In conjunction with the filing of a registration form, several states require a bond to be furnished by the principals of the organization. 

Another method of control and enforcement is to require all foundations to file regular reports of financial activity of their respective solicitations or trusts to a specific state agency. This obviously allows the regulating agencies to audit the foundations' accounts, and identify abuses of administration.

Reporting forms are generally required to be filed on an annual basis. 

The forms require an accounting of revenue received and revenue expended. Specific information regarding fund-raising drives may be required. Many forms require costs of administration not chargeable to solicitation. In many cases, the opinion of a certified public accountant must be attached. 

Information from the C.O.A.G. questionnaire indicates that 17 states provide for registration or some form of regular report. The states requiring registration or reporting include: California, Colorado, Delaware, Illinois, Iowa, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New 

Jersey, New York, Ohio, Rhode Island, Washington and Wisconsin.

Several states that do not use standard reporting or registration forms do maintain records on charitable organizations by filing Form 990-A. This form is required to be filed with the Internal Revenue Service by all organizations that claim tax exempt status. A copy must be sent to the Attorney General. 

Data Processing. In those states which have a large number of charitable organizations, it might be desirable to store information gathered on data processing equipment. Presently, only California and Rhode Island have employed such a system. Assistant Attorney General Anthony C. Joseph provides the following explanation of the California system: -8-

Both initial registration information and information received on annual reports are kept in the electronic data processing system. The EDP system is programmed to run various tests from the information given to it which will indicate potential problems with each registrant.

Problems and Complaints Encountered Most Frequently. The C.O.A.G. questionnaire asked states to list their most common problems and complaints that they receive. Twenty-five of forty-two reporting jurisdictions responded to the question. The most common responses are summarized below. As

Although specifically labeled illegal, the most common complaint received was that of self-dealing by the trustees or administrators of the trust. its name implies, this practice involves the use of funds from the trust for the personal benefit of others than the intended beneficiaries. Questionable practices exist in cases involving mortgages, loans or investments by the trustees or administrators.

Another common problem is the non-distribution of funds under a trust which specifies distribution to a specific needy group or charity. In many cases, the trust will retain its funds, opting to accumulate interest on the corpus or to make speculative investments.

Related to the two above abuses is the imprudent investment or accumulation of trust funds. In several cases, those managing the trust lack the expertise or skill to prudently invest funds accumulated by the trust. The result is a loss of income which ultimately hinders the rights of the intended beneficiaries. Further, quite often trustees inadequately distribute funds to the beneficiaries.

Several states noted complaints of individuals who solicited funds under the guise of a charitable organization. Such actions are generally actionable under consumer fraud statutes. In addition to the above complaints, others noted include: failure to register; excessive fund-raising costs; high attorneys fees; and lack of adequate exchange of information with the Internal Revenue Service.

November, 1973 of the Tax Reform Act of 1969 relating to private foundations affect the Attorney General of every state and his enforcement of state laws relating to private foundations.

The Tax Reform Act of 1969 was designed to place the following controls on charitable foundations: foreclose self-dealing in the management of foundations; insure a reasonable annual payout; institute strengthened auditing of foundations funded by an audit fee; phase divisture of substantial interests in companies; prohibit speculative investment; place stringent restrictions on the funding of voter registration drives and political activities affecting legislation; limit deductions for gifts to most private foundations, and levy a four percent "excise tax" on net investment income. 

Of prime 

Federal-state relationships were also strengthened by the Act. note was the requirement of federal reporting of foundation misconduct to Attorneys General and disclosure to them of federal investigative records that are relevant to any determination under state law. Although, there have been several problems under this reporting requirement, most states have at least benefited by being able to identify foundations operating within their state that had not been previously identified. 

At its 1970 Winter Meeting, NAAG adopted two resolutions aimed at further improving the enforcement of charitable trusts through federal-state cooperation. One urged Congress to promptly enact legislation concerning charitable organizations other than private foundations that will, in purpose and effect, extend tax treatment to all charitable organizations like that afforded private foundations by the Tax Reform Act of 1969. The other urged a "single, common form of annual report to be required of private foundations that will conform both the requirements of the Tax Reform Act of 1969 and the corresponding laws of the several states." This would minimize duplication of effort by foundations and encourage prompt, and accurate reporting. 

Proposed Legislation 

Recently, four bills dealing with charitable foundations have been authored by members of the United States House of Representatives. These bills will potentially have a profound effect upon the Attorneys Generals' traditional role in enforcing and regulating charitable foundations. Two of the bills deal with use of the current 4 percent excise tax on investment income of private foundations. The other two deal with charitable solicitations and corporate investment respectively.

Federal Funding of State Enforcement Programs. H. R. 5728, authored by 

Representative Wright Patman, Texas, would earmark the current 4 percent tax on investment income of private foundations for auditing and supervisory purposes. 

The bill would require a mandatory sharing of this "audit fee" with the states which set up a specific mechanism to audit and supervise the foundations within their borders. To qualify for the sharing of the tax revenues, each state must establish, to the satisfaction of the Secretary of the Treasury, that it will audit the private foundations in its jurisdiction and that it will exercise "substantial supervision over their activities to insure that such private foundations will promptly and properly use their funds for charitable purposes." 

The states must also agree to make specific reports to the Secretary of the Treasury each year concerning the use of the funds and a detailed description of the activities of the private foundations supervised by the state.

Initially, each state which qualifies will be entitled to receive a basic amount of $250,000. The remainder of the states' one-half share of the four percent tax shall be apportioned to the states on the basis of the foundation assets within each jurisdiction. It is estimated that the states would be entitled to about $30 million in the first year and predicted that the share would increase sharply as the income of the foundations increases. 

The legislation would require that all funds collected under the foundation tax be used for "auditing and supervisory" purposes and that none of it would be put in the General Fund of the Treasury. If at any time more funds are collected that are needed for auditing and supervisory purposes by the Federal and state governments, the surplus will be credited to future payments by the foundations.

Corporate Investment. A second bill authored by Representative Patman,

H.R. 5729, would limit to 10 percent of a foundation's assets the amount which could be invested in any single corporation. The bill would allow foundations five years to diversify their portfolios and come within the 10 percent rule. 

This would provide new safeguards for the foundations and would substantially increase their income and result in more pay-outs to charity. Recent studies by the Banking and Currency Committee show that numerous foundations have an extraordinary amount of their portfolio invested in a singel company. Further studies conducted by the Committee indicate that a substantial number of foundations with heavy assets in a single company have experienced declines in the market value of their portfolios in recent years resulting in a loss of funds available for the charitable purposes for which the foundations are granted tax exemptions.

Future Activity 

The regulation and enforcement of charitable foundations is a matter of increasing concern with Attorneys General. This increased concern has been mainly brought about by a recognition of abuses within the administration of foundations and by the Tax Reform Act of 1969. 

As discussed earlier, most states responding to the C.0.A.G. questionnaire indicate complaints of abuses in the administration of charitable foundations. 

These abuses may have an impact of the most serious nature, since, as Table IV indicates, total assets in a state may run into billions of dollars. To pro- mote and preserve the public purpose of these foundations, Attorneys General have expanded their programs.

Since the Tax Reform Act of 1969 required all private foundations to submit annual reports to the Attorney General as well as to the Internal Revenue Service, most states have obtained records on a substantial number of organizations which had not previously registered in their state. This has vastly increased the volume of auditing required by the Attorney Generals' Office. Further, the Act has enhanced the chances of discovering wrong-doing, for which legal action is required. New York's experience is typical. In 1970, the State of New York litigated a total of 616 cases. In 1971, the figure rose to 770, and in 1972, New York litigated 793 cases.

Greater regulation and litigation requires an increased staff. 

In 1970, only 23 states had staff within the Attorney General's Office assigned to charitable foundations. the recent COAG questionnaire indicated that 27 states now have staff assigned on a regular basis to the area. Ohio's experience was recently summarized by Chief Assistant Attorney General J. John Stevenson:

The increased responsibilities of the Attorney General generated by the Tax Reform Act of 1969 necessitates the expansion of our facilities and the employment of a dedicated staff. Such a program has been actively undertaken by Attorney General William J. 

Brown. Since his term began in 1971, the Division has assumed -16-

November, 1973 a positive role in regulating and assisting charitable organizations. Through its efforts registration has increased to 3,000 organizations with an additional 1,000 non-registered charities on file. Our staff has increased from one attorney and one legal aide to five attorneys, five legal aides and three accountants. 

This will increase to ten attorneys, five legal aides and seven accountants by November of 1973.5

Although activity in the area of charitable foundations has increased, budgets have not. Only California reports a special budget provision for the enforcement of charitable trust laws. All other states apparently administer the charitable trust laws out of general appropriations for the Attorney General's Office. This fact was established at recent House committee hearings on proposed federal legislation to provide funding for the states. At those hearings, Ohio, New York, California, Illinois and Massachusetts all expressed a need for increasing funding. Attorney General William J. Brown, Ohio, expressed Ohio's needs as follows: 

While we have managed to resurrect the Charitable Foundations Division and direct it on a positive, constructive path, we seek to reach higher goals through closer cooperation with the Federal Government. Charitable Foundations staff, we have had to reallocate the limited resources of our budget. Such a move was clearly in order, but this has resulted in great sacrifice to other vital needs of the State.

APPENDIX B HISTORY AND CHARACTERISTICS OF CHARITABLE TRUSTS The following is reproduced from The Office Of Attorney General, published in February, 1971 by the Committee on the Office of Attorney General. It is intended to provide a historical perspective of the Attorney General's role in Charitable Trusts.

Common Law Basis 

Enforcement of charitable trusts has consistently been recognized as a common law power of the Attorney General. Professor J. Ll. J. Edwards, in his authoritative study of the Attorney General of England, discusses the Attorney General's responsibilities in relator actions, where he is the Crown's principal agent for enforcing public legal rights, and notes that:

It has been suggested that the progenitor of the modern relator's action is to be found in the original procedure whereby the Attorney General, representing the King as parens patriae, would proceed by way of information to enforce rights of a charitable nature for the benefit of those interested persons who either declined or were unable to enforce their claims in person. 

Professor Edwards notes the "well-established recognition of the Attorney General as the repository at the public conscience" in connection with charities. 

The power to enforce charitable trusts has been traced to the early 14th Century, when legislation was adopted restricting the alienability of land to religious organizations. To evade such limitations, resort was made to the use, the antecedent of the modern day trust. Early common law courts refused to recognize the use; however, the king enforced charitable uses through exercise of his authority as parens patriae. In time, this power to enforce charitable trusts became vested in the general courts of equity. The process by which this was accomplished has been summarized as follows:

After the Chancellor's Court of Equity had become distinct from the King's Council, the King would exercise his prerogative to have a charitable trust enforced by authorizing his attorney general to file an information in Chancery. This was the origin of the cy press power and its exercise was not a judicial function of the Chancellor. If the charitable trust was sufficiently definite, and individual with a sufficient interest, such as a trustee, could bring an action in Chancery to have the trust enforced. 

No provision was made to require registration of charitable trusts; hence, the King often had no knowledge of their existence. Further, there was no requirement that he enforce even those trusts he did know existed. Parliament took action to offset these deficiencies by enacting the Statute of Charitable Uses in 

1601, which specifically gave Chancery power to enforce charitable trusts.

An early Virginia case, Trustees of Philadelphia Baptist Association v. Hart's Executor, 4 was decided before it was known in America that charitable trusts had been enforceable in England by equity courts prior to the Statute of Charitable Uses. The court held that charitable trusts were not enforceable in equity since there was no evidence that they had been so enforceable in England prior to the Statute of Charitable Uses. Virginia had repealed the Statute of Charitable Uses in 1792.

Although the decision in the Hart case was based on incorrect assumptions, it remained the law of the land until 1844. In that year, Mr. Justice Storey, in the case of Vidal v. Girard's Executors,6 held that a charitable trust was enforceable in the equity courts of Pennsylvania. The Vidal case established the rule that equity courts in the United States could enforce charitable trusts independent of the Statute of Uses. By 1860, most equity courts enforced such trusts.

Characteristics of Charitable Trusts 

In her definitive work on charitable foundations, Marion R. Fremont-Smith notes three distinct characteristics of charitable gifts: (1) The privilege of indefinite existence; (2) The privilege of validity even if the gift is in general terms, so long as its objective is exclusively charitable; (3) The privilege of obtaining fresh objects if those laid down by the founder became incapable of execution. 

The Restatement of Trusts lists as valid charitable goals the relief of poverty, the advancement of education or religion, the promotion of health, governmental and municipal purposes, and "other purposes the accomplishment of which is beneficial to the community". One commentator notes the widespread legal consequences of creating such a trust:

In the first place, courts will apply liberal rules of construction to sustain trusts which evidence a charitable intent. Once established, charitable trusts are generally exempted from various restrictions applied to private trusts. For example, common-law rules invalidating certain restraints on the alienation of trust property are usually held to be inapplicable to charitable trusts, as are prohibitions against the perpetual duration of trusts and the accumulation of trust income. More important are the tax advantages enjoyed by charitable trusts, including exemptions from federal income taxation and normally from local ad valorem property taxes as well. Federal laws actually encourage philanthropic giving by allowing the donor to deduct charitable contributions in computing his income, espata and gift taxes. Finally, the cypress power, which permits a court of equity to modify a trust to meet changed conditions in available where the settlor had a general charitable intent. 

One characteristic of a charitable trust is that the beneficiaries are not clearly defined. Intended beneficiaries might not be aware of their status or even of the existence of the trust itself. Hence, legislation designed for the enforcement of ordinary trusts is not effective to enforce charitable trusts. 

Lack of knowledge and lack of informed self-interest makes the typical individual beneficiary ineffective in seeking the redress of abuses. George Bogert, in 

Trusts and Trustees, notes that the society is the real beneficiary of every charitable trust, and that the people are the mere conduits of the social benefits to the public.10 The important element in finding a manifestation of intent to dedicate the property to a purpose that is accepted as charitable, thus imposing the judiciary duties of the trustee. 11 B-2
