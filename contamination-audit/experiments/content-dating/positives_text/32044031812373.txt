CHAPTER I Searches 1-1: The Search in General 

A-The doctrine of caveat emptor applies to the purchase of real estate, 1. It is the duty of the purchaser or mortgagee to search the public records and to make a complete examination of such records, for such information as they may contain regarding the validity of the title of such real estate. 

2. The registry and recording acts and therefore, the search, are public data giving constructive data. 

3. The system of searching is to trace the line of record title to that particularly described tract or parcel of land and search against each owner during the period he held title. 

It is upon this that titles to real estate are based. 

4. To make the title complete every succeeding line in the chain of title should be complete of record for the purchaser's protection. 

5. Judgments, however, act as liens on the real estate of the judgment debtor whether title is acquired before or after the judgment is entered and are good for 20 years. 

6. Judgment searches should cover a period of 

20 years before title is acquired as against everyone in the chain of title, and for 20

years before the property is conveyed to another grantee. 

7. Where a judgment is found in the prior chain of title, executions should be searched to forestall the possibility of a sale having been made of the property between the date of the entering of the judgment and a subsequent sale by the sheriff on execution.

A-Requirements prior to filing complaint 

1. Complaint cannot be filed unless: (a) 2 years has expired since the date of the issuance of the tax sales certificate. (b) 4 complete calendar years of taxes remain due and unpaid including year suit instituted. (c) Governing body of municipality must pass resolution to foreclose and said resolution must contain following: (1) Schedule number. (2) Description of the land as it appears on the tax duplicate and in the certificate of tax sale. (3) Serial number, or, if no serial number, other identification of the certificate of tax sale. (4) Date of tax sale. (5) Book and page or date and instrument number of the record of the certificate in the office of the county recording officer, if such certificate has been recorded at the time of the adoption of such resolution, and the prior recording of the tax certificate shall not be a prerequisite to the adoption of the resolution. 41

(6) The amount of the sale as set forth in the certificate. (7) The amount of all tax liens accuring subsequent to the tax sale, including interest, penalties and costs. (8) The total amount required to redeem. HISTORY OF TAXATION IN NEW JERSEY 

In the early history of New Jersey, in what is known as the colonial period and dating back to around 1670, the machinery for levying and assessing taxes was comparatively simple. The population was small with few urban areas, and the administration of the property tax was a simple affair. 

The property which was subject to taxation was chiefly land, buildings, horses, certain types of cattle, and a limited number of industries and commercial enterprises. 

In these colonial days the only one who received the tax was the provincial government, and the only tax official in existence at that time was the local constable. When counties were created in 1682 they were given taxing powers, and in 1686 towns were permitted to levy taxes for the support of highways, bridges and other public purposes. The first constitution of the State of New Jersey, which was adopted in 1776, made no fundamental change in the tax structure or pattern. Some years later, in 1829, townships were authorized to raise money for school purposes. There seemed to be no limit to the amount that could be levied, and when a township proceeded to act under the authority of the law they became entitled to share in state aid. The unlimited. ability to levy this tax apparently ran into abuse and in 1838 the Legislature limited the amount which a township could raise for school purposes. This amount was limited to a sum not to exceed double the amount of the State aid. Apparently this arrangement did not work out because a few years later, in 1846, the law was changed and required the township to raise a sum equal but not more than double the amount apportioned or granted to it by the State. Around this period of time in our early history, there was no tax levy for State purposes but with the growth of the population and urban areas, the demands of the counties, townships and school districts on property were constantly and steadily 81

increasing, and State and local officials were being forced into a keener awareness of their tax problems. That this was so was indicated by Governor Daniel Haines in a message to the State Legislature in 1851 in which he said that: "The passage of a law equalizing taxation seems to be imperatively demanded by the people and I respectfully but earnestly commend it to your early consideration and prompt and efficient action."

It should be pointed out that the New Jersey Constitution of 1844 was silent on the subject of taxation. 

Prior to 1851, the property tax system in New Jersey consisted of a combination of: 

1. Ad valorem taxes (which is a tax on property and measured by the value of the taxed property). 

2. Specific taxes (which imposes a specific tax by head or number, or some standard of measurement other than the value of the property).

In 1951, a really important change was made in our tax In 1851, a really important change was made in our tax structure. The legislature by an act of that year (P.L. 1851, p. 271) wiped out specific property taxes and all property, real and personal was required to be taxed upon an equal ratio according to "actual value." This new law, however, failed to define a standard of value and this omission caused so much trouble that the law makers soon established "market value" or "true value" as the legal basis for assessment. 

In the early development of the tax system in the State of New Jersey, the railroads played a very important part.

Therefore, to prevent the injustice of this inequality and to equalize the public burdens, the New Jersey legislature in 1873 passed an act to establish just rules for the taxation of railroad property. 83

About the same period of time, in 1875, the New Jersey Constitution of 1844 was amended to provide that "property shall be assessed for taxes under general laws and by uni form rules according to its true value." In a further attempt to improve the tax structure, through centralization and control of local assessments, the legislature, in 1891, passed a law creating the State Board of Taxation. This Board was given statutory power to revise, review and equalize assessments within and among the taxing districts and counties. This State Board of Taxation functioned until 1905 when it was abolished and replaced by a

As the State of New Jersey grew and the population increased, the need of more governmental services naturally increased. As more highways, roads, courthouses, jails and other public buildings were being built, new taxes and better methods of administration of these taxes became a necessity. 

The old machinery for the assessment and collection of taxes was breaking down.

This weakening of the assessment process was noted by Governor George F. Fort in his annual message of 1854 in which he said: "There would be a propriety of devising some better mode to obtain a fair and equal assessment. 

As now exercised, there is no doubt that, through favoritism, lack of energy, and other causes, equitable valuations of property are often evaded. To correct this evil, I would recommend that the commissioners of appeal in each township be required to sit as a board of revision and appeal on the appraisement made by the assessor, with power to examine and correct the duplicate, before it is delivered to the collector. In such case, the assessor should give notice to the citizen, of the tax assessed, as soon as he completes the assessment; so that the whole assessment shall pass the inspection of the board, be revised and corrected before the collector commences his duties. "These suggestions are made with the confident belief, that by judicious legislation on this important subject, much improvement may be made, without doing injustice to any, or infringing in the 85

least upon the great and vital principle of equal taxation."

There were attempts made from time to time to improve the local assessment process.

In 1866 a provision was made (Law 1866, page 108) for a yearly meeting of the County Assessors to equalize the basis of valuations upon which state and county taxes were to be apportioned. 

In an attempt to centralize and control local assessment and inter-district equalization, the Legislature in 1891 passed a law creating the State Board of Taxation. This Board was given statutory power to revise, review and equalize assessments within and among taxing districts and counties. (Law 

1891, Chapt. CXIV.) This State Board of Taxation func tioned until 1905, when it was abolished and replaced by a 

Board of Equalization of Taxes (Laws of 1905, Chapter 67). 

The powers and duties of the State Board of Taxation were transferred and taken over by the new Board of Equalization, and the new law also added a further jurisdiction to equalize railroad and canal assessments with assessments of other property. To supplement and strengthen the tax machinery, the legislature in 1906 (Laws 1906, Chapter 120) established County Boards of Taxation. This was done because supervision of local taxation by State Boards was apparently unsatisfactory.

At this stage of development, the Tax Structure in New Jersey consisted primarily of the Board of Equalization of Taxes, the County Boards of Taxation and the State Board of Assessors, which was established in 1884 to assess railroad and canal property. 86

HEARINGS AND INVESTIGATIONS 

In cases where the Director is authorized by law to make investigations or hold hearings, he has the power to subpoena witnesses and compel the production of books, records and documents. (R.S. 54:1-16, 54:50-4 and 54:50-5.)

The statute apparently does not grant a similar power to the taxpayer, but if the taxpayer wishes the use of the agency's subpoena power, it is granted.

The Director, or any of his delegated employees who have been deputized to sit for him, have the power to administer oaths to any persons sworn as witnesses, and they also have the authority to apply to the Superior Court for an order to compel any person to submit to an examination for the purpose of ascertaining any facts which are pertinent to the proper performance of the duties of the Director. 

Generally speaking, any taxpayer aggrieved by any findings or assessment by the Division of Taxation is entitled to a hearing thereon. (R.S. 54:49-18.) The taxpayer or his duly authorized agent has thirty days from the giving of the notice of the findings or assessment in which to request a hearing, and the method for doing so is to file a written protest, stating the reason therefore, under oath and together with a request for the hearing. (R.S. 54:49-18.)

Usually this type of hearing is technically informal in nature, and the testimony given at these hearings may or may not be under oath nor necessarily recorded. The rules of evidence are generally applied, but in their applications contested evidence is usually admitted, probably because the hearing officer is not a lawyer and not trained in the strictly technical rules of the evidence. Decisions and orders resulting from these hearings are usually accompanied by some findings of facts and conclusions. However, in view of the 95

fact that the taxpayer has a right to a trial de novo in the Division of Appeals, his substantive rights are protected. 

From any decision of the Director, except as to transfer, inheritance and estate taxes, appeal may be taken to the 

Division of Tax Appeals as a matter of right. (R.S. 54:1-44, 

54:2-34 and 54:51-1.) In the case of transfer, inheritance and estate taxes, the appeal is directed to the Appellate Division of the Superior Court. (R.S. 54:34-13 and 54:38-10.)

CHAPTER DISCUSSION OF TAX EQUALIZATION Nature and Purpose 

Equalization of taxes, in the language of Mr. Justice 

Dixon when speaking on the subject, "requires that the tax exacted from each person owning or possessing property of the class assessed, shall bear the same proportion to the whole amount of taxes exacted, from all persons having property of that class, as the true value of each one's classified property, bears to the true value of all of the property. 

(See Central Railroad Company vs. State Board of Assessors, 

19 Vroom, Page 146.)

In Cooley Taxation, 4th Edition, Volume 3, Section 

1195, it is stated: "Equalization of assessments has, for its general purpose, to bring the assessments of different parts of a taxing district to the same relative standard, so that no one of the parts may be compelled to pay a disproportionate part of the tax. . . . This is not done by changing individual assessments, but by fixing the aggregate sums for the several districts at what, in the opinion of the board, they should be, . . . 

In New Jersey the important principle of equalization is recognized by the fact that the statutes give equalization powers to the county boards of taxation, the Division of Tax 

Appeals and the Director of the Division of Taxation. The general purpose of these statutory powers is to equalize and justly apportion the county tax burden among the several taxing districts in the county, and further to supervise and review this process of equalization by the Division of Tax 

Appeals and the Director of the Division of Taxation. (See 

97

City of New Brunswick vs. Middlesex County Equalization. 

Table, 53 A. 2nd Page 641.) 

This principal of equalization had an early application in the State of New Jersey. Under the act of 1905, (P.L. 

1905, Page 124, Section 3) the Board of Equalization of 

Taxes had power to equalize the assessments between districts, or between counties bearing a common burden of taxation, and to equalize the valuations throughout the territory which contributed taxes to the common burden. 

This board was consolidated with the State Board of Taxes and Assessments under a law passed in 1915. (P.L. 

1915, Page 438.)

In 1917 (P.L. 1917, Page 61, as amended by P.L. 1927, Page 216) an equalization act was passed and by the act of P.L. 1931, Page 823, the duties of preparing a State Equalization Table and equalizing assessments passed to the State Tax Commission, and on appeal to the State Board of Tax Appeals. These acts were superseded by the act passed under 

P.L. 1934, Page 465, in which the County Boards of Taxation are now required to prepare a County Equalization Table, and on the state level the Director of the Division of Taxation is required to prepare a State Equalization Table from which, in each case, there is an appeal to the Division of Tax Appeals. 

By virtue of N.J.S.A. 54:3-17 R.S., a copy of the Equal ization Table is mailed by the County Boards of Taxation to the assessor of each taxing district, and annually on January 98

EQUALIZATION OF ASSESSMENTS 

25th the county boards of taxation meet for the purpose of equalizing the assessment of real property between the taxing districts of the county. (R.S. 54:3-17.)

By March 10th of each year the County Boards of Taxa tion conclude hearings on the equalization tables and after the equalization table is finally confirmed by the county boards, a copy of the same is transmitted to the Director of the Division of Taxation, the Division of Tax Appeals, the State Comptroller, and each taxing district in the county.

In McEnaney vs. Spedick 13 N. J. Super. page 39. 

Sept. 27, 1947. 

Received from R.S.M. One Thousand Dollars ($1,000.00) deposit, on property known as The White Birch Cottages consisting of 13 Furnished houses. Full price $30,000. "A counterpart of this writing, retained by the defendant H.C.S. was signed by R.M. and had added thereto, in R.S.M.'s handwriting, 'deed of property to be made out in the name of M. and 106

FORMS 

R.M.' The court further said, "The first contention is that the writing is a mere receipt and not a contract. The fact that a writing, which embodies the essential elements of a contract, acknowledged the receipt of certain money or property does not, standing alone, make the entire writing a mere receipt subject to contradiction by parol evidence. 

It is admitted that this writing is certain as to parties, description of the premises, price and terms. 

Therefore, it contains the essential elements of a contract for the sale of land. Since it is contractual in nature, it is a contract and not a mere receipt.'
