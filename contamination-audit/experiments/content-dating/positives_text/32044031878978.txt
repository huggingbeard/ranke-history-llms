SUBJECT: Report on the Possibilities for Redevelopment of Niagara Falls, Niagara River, New York for

Hydroelectric Power. 

1. This report on the possibilities for a greater utilization of the water resources of the Niagara River at Niagara Falls for the production of hydroelectric power, consistent with other necessary and desirable uses is submitted pursuant to the request of the Federal Power Commission, and has been prepared under authority of Section 4(a) of the Federal Power Act. In accordance with the Commission's instructions the informal assistance of The Power Authority of the State of New York and The Niagara Falls Power Company was utilized as offered by these two interested agencies. 

2. Before proceeding with the study, a conference with Mr. 

Gerald V. Cruise, Trustee and Chief Engineer of The Power Authority of the State of New York and Mr. Edwin S. Bundy, Vice-President and Chief Engineer of The Niagara Falls Power Company was held on March 26, 1947 in the office of the Chief, Bureau of Power, Federal Power Commission, Washington, D. C. to discuss the scope of the investigation. It was the consensus that the following basic assumptions should be adopted, subject to any changes which might be found desirable as the study progressed:

(a) For purposes of present consideration of the problem, the amount of diversion of waters for power production should be based upon the latest official recommendation, to wit: the recommendations of the Special International

Niagara Board as to diversions around the Falls and around the Lower Rapids, except that flows in excess of those required for scenic purposes should also be developed to the extent feasible. (b) Water passages for full head development should be designed to carry at least 120 percent of normal average daily diversions in accordance with existing provisions affecting diversions, and to permit day use of night flows. (c) Consideration of possibilities for providing regulation of the Niagara River by means of a control works in the Niagara River in the vicinity of Buffalo. (d) The assumption that one-half of the total water available for power be developed on each side of the International Border. (e) Existing plants should remain in operation as part of the redevelopment if: (1) The most efficient use is being made of water considering full head development, or (2) The most efficient use is being made of head available above the

Lower Rapids resulting from water required for preservation of scenic effects in the Lower Rapids and 

Whirlpool and for purposes of assisting in handling ice conditions in the Gorge. (f) Existing plants which do not meet the requirements of (e) above should be retired as more efficient plants become available. Consideration should be given to preserving such plants as stand-bys for emergency defense or war use. (g) The scheme of redevelopment should be such as to permit construction by stages as load requirements might indicate.

3 .

The need for such a study is indicated by the following 

(a) A great natural water resource capable of meeting these needs is not being fully or efficiently utilized. (b) The availability of low cost power in northeastern United States is insufficient to meet existing and potential requirements. (c) There exists, in general, a need for additional power throughout the entire country. (d) The northeast is largely dependent for supplies of electric energy produced from fuel plants, the fuel for which must be imported into the area from other parts of the country. (e) Fuel costs are high. (f) The Power Authority of the State of New York has officially advised the Federal Power Commission of its recommendations for public redevelopment of the Niagara River, in coordination with the St. 

Lawrence power project in the same watershed and of its opposition to increased use of Niagara water under license to private interests. (8) The Niagara Falls Power Company, licensee for Project No. 16 which includes existing developments for power at Niagara Falls on the United States side, has advised the Federal Power Commission that it wishes to expand its power developments along the Niagara River. (h) The United States and Canadian Governments concurrently recognized the need for remedial works, as envisaged by the Special

4. 

International Niagara Board, to insure the best use of the diversions for power purposes and to protect the scenic beauty of the Falls. The Niagara River Submerged 

Weir was constructed in the Canadian channel about one mile above the cataract and is the first constructed of the remedial works proposed by the Board. It is reported by the Construction Subcommittee that the weir has increased the output of power by restoration of water levels in Grass Island Pool and has nearly doubled the flow over the American Falls improving its scenic appearance. (1) The study could serve a useful purpose in any negotiations which may be undertaken to amend the International Boundary Waters

Treaty of 1909-10 concerning equitable division of the waters and to authorize additional permanent diversions for power purposes. 

4. Since the Niagara River is part of the International boundary between the United States and Canada, the utilization of its waters is subject to the international treaty with Great Britain and to agreements with Canada. Although the apportionment of water by treaty or agreement as between the nations is not a responsibility of the Federal Power Commission, the Commission has jurisdiction over such waters as may be apportioned for power purposes to the United States, and it is empowered, under Section 4(a) of the Federal Power Act to make investigation and report thereon.

SYLLABUS 5(a). The International Boundary Waters Treaty of 1909-10 

between the United States and Great Britain permitted the authorization of a permanent daily diversion from the Niagara River above Niagara Falls for power purposes of 20,000 cubic feet per second in the United States and of 36,000 cubic feet per second in Canada. In 

1940, 1941 and 1944, temporary additional diversions totalling 

12,500 cubic feet per second in United States and 18,000 cubic feet per second in Canada were agreed to, and, of the latter amount 5,000 cubic feet per second are compensated for by the diversion of water from the Hudson Bay drainage basin to Lake Superior. A further agreement in 1948 renewed until April 1951 the temporary agreement of 1944 with respect to water being diverted by Canada (4,000 cubic feet per second) and, in addition, permitted the diversion during the nonnavigation season and until April 1951 of an additional 2,500 cubic feet per second through the Welland Canal for power use. Further additional diversions for power purposes in Canada include 1,430 cubic feet per second for power purposes and 370 cubic feet per second for power and sanitation purposes totalling 1,800 cubic feet per second 

diverted prior to the 1909-10 Treaty. The present authorized diversions for power purposes total 32,500 cubic feet per second in the United States and 50,430 cubic feet per second in Canada, (52,930 cubic feet per second in non-navigation season) exclusive of the 5,000 cubic feet per second compensated for by the

diversion from the Hudson Bay basin and the 370 cubic feet per second used for sanitation and power along the Second Welland Canal. 

5(b). The diversions in the United States are used through 

The Niagara Falls Power Company Schoellkopf and Adams stations, with total rated capacity of 445,000 kilowatts. Neither of these plants develops the full head available from above Niagara Falls to Lake Ontario level. The diversions in Canada are used through the Queenston, Ontario, Toronto and DeCew stations of The Hydro 

Electric Power Commission of Ontario and Canadian Niagara Power 

Company Rankine station, with a total rated capacity of 848,000 kilowatts in the five plants. More than a fourth of the water diverted in Canada is used through practically the full head from above the Falls to nearly Lake Ontario level. The power (35,062 kilowatts) from about one-half the water used through the Rankine station is delivered into the United States under a contract (expiring 

September 30, 1974) between the Canadian Niagara Power Company and the Buffalo Niagara Electric Corporation subject to annual renewal of an export license from the Canadian Government. In addition, the power (about 26,000 kilowatts) generated in Canada from the 4,000 cubic feet per second diversion authorized in 1944 and renewed in 1948 until 1951 is exported into the United States. 

5(c). In accordance with the Special Niagara Board's recommendations and considerations not less than 100,000 cubic feet per second

will be made available for scenic purposes for the Falls and Rapids during the tourist season in the daytime. Flows in excess of 100,000 cubic feet per second and up to a maximum of 100,000 cubic feet per second will be available for diversion for power purposes. During the night hours in the tourist season all flows in excess of 50,000 cubic feet per second will be available for diversion for power purposes. In the non-tourist season, except where required for ice flushing in the Gorge, all flows in excess of 50,000 cubic feet per second will be available for diversion for power purposes. It is assumed that water available for power purposes would be divided equally between the United States and Canada which assumption seems consistent with Article VIII of the 1909-10 International Boundary Waters Treaty. Thus each country would develop its half of the water available for power purposes in accordance with its needs, with appropriate adjustment in power now exported from Canada to the United States under private contracts. 

5(d). A feasible plan for redevelopment in the United States would consist of: a Lower Niagara unit in conjunction with the existing Schoellkopf plant with diversion works in the Lower Niagara below Schoellkopf tailrace and a tunnel serving 120,000 kilowatts of generating capacity in a power plant at Lewiston; a Conners 

Island-Lewiston unit with an intake in the Niagara River near Conners Island, waterways (either covered canals or tunnels) serving 1,080,000 kilowatts of generating capacity in the Lewiston power

· 8plant; a pump storage unit near the Lewiston plant with a generating capability of 130,000 kilowatts; and control works in the Niagara 

River near the outlet of Lake Erie. The control works should not be finally determined until an international engineering board has investigated all possible effects of such a structure, its best location, and other pertinent factors. Such a redevelopment in the 

United States would provide a total installation of 1,695,000 kilowatts, including Schoellkopf and the new capacity but exclusive of the Adams plant which would be used for voltage regulation and as stand-by reserve. The redevelopment would increase the total dependable capacity from the existing 440,000 kilowatts to 1,572,000 kilowatts, or a net increase of 1,132,000 kilowatts. The average annual energy would be increased from 3.7 billion kilowatt-hours to 11.6 billion kilowatt-hours, or an increase of 7.9 billion kilowatt-hours. 

The corresponding increases, after losses, delivered to load centers amount to about 1,115,000 kilowatts in dependable capacity and about 7.78 billion kilowatt-hours of annual energy. Power market studies indicate that this increase in power could be absorbed in either central and western New York or in that area and northwestern Pennsylvania and northeastern Ohio in less than ten years after giving consideration to power which might be made available by the concurrent development of the International Rapids section of the 

St. Lawrence River, 5(e). The estimated cost of the complete redevelopment is

$308,700,000 using covered canals as waterways from near Conners Island to Lewiston and $350,300,000 if tunnels are used in lieu of canals. With canals, the estimated average costs per kilowatthour are 2.08 mills and 5.57 mills for public and private financing, respectively, at the load centers in central and western New York. 

Using tunnels instead of canals, the estimated average costs per kilowatt-hour are 2.30 mills and 6.22 mills for public and private financing, respectively, at the same load centers. The estimated average cost per kilowatt-hour of equivalent steam-electric generation is 7.88 mills based on private financing. 

CONCLUSIONS 

6. As a result of this study the following general conclusions are drawn with respect to the possibilities for the comprehensive redevelopment for power on the United States side at Niagara Falls: (a) The amount of water now being diverted on the United States side of the Falls, including permanent and temporary diversions, is less than that on the Canadian side. However, a substantial amount of power generated on the Canadian side is transmitted to the United States. (b) The amount of water being diverted on the Canadian side, including permanent and temporary diversions, is roughly equal to half of the total amount of water which may be diverted for power purposes. (c) The construction of the submerged weir has resulted in improvement of scenic conditions at the American Falls and has increased the

- 

output of power from those plants which have their intakes upstream from the weir; and the remainder of the remedial works should be completed along with redevelopment for power purposes. (d) Water for power purposes is being diverted in excess of the amounts suggested as a starting point by the Special International

Niagara Board without apparent detriment to scenic effects at the Falls. (e) Experience in the operation of large hydroelectric power plants at Niagara Falls extends back for over 50 years. (f) Ice conditions have been exhaustively studied by various parties. (g) It now appears that decision as to the amounts of water needed for purposes of scenic effects, ice alleviation, navigation and other nonpower uses could be made with reasonable confidence, and the amounts of water suggested in this report could be made available for power use. (h) The total permanent diversions of water around the Falls and Lower Rapids for power purposes (in United States and Canada) should be provided substantially as follows: 

1. During the tourist season (April 1 to October 31): (a) Daytime (sunrise to sunset). 

Flows in excess of 100,000 cubic feet per second and in an amount not exceeding 100,000 cubic feet per second. (b) Nighttime (sunset to sunrise). 

Flows in excess of 50,000 cubic feet per second. 

2. During the non-tourist season (November 1 to March 31):

. 

All flows in excess of 50,000 cubic feet per second except when necessary for flushing ice in the Gorge below the Falls. (1) of the total diversions which may be authorized for power purposes (exclusive of the "Ogoki" diversion), onehalf should be authorized for use in the United States and one-half for use in Canada, thus permitting each country to develop its share in accordance with its needs. (j) A final division between the United States and Canada of the waters of 

Niagara River available for power (excluding the "Ogoki" diversion from the Hudson Bay basin) should be made immediately. (k) Appropriate adjustments in the power now exported from Canada to the United States should be made as the redevelopment progresses. (1) Present facilities at Niagara Falls in the United States do not develop fully the existing diversions of water and as a result there is a continuing waste of a great and magnificant natural resource. (m) The existing Schoellkopf station is valuable and adapted to any comprehensive plan for the redevelopment of Niagara Falls for power in the United States; the existing 

Adams station should be retired from normal generating service but can be used for voltage regulation and kept in stand-by reserve for generating use in event of a grave emergency. (1) Under the assumptions of water availability proposed in this report the dependable capacity at Niagara Falls in the United

States could be increased from 440,000

- kilowatts to 1,572,000 kilowatts with increase in energy at the project site from 

3.7 billion kilowatt-hours to 11.6 billion kilowatt-hours. (o) Such power will be lower in cost than power from alternative fuel-electric plants under either public or private financing. (p) With full allowance for any power that might be available from the proposed St. Lawrence project at Barnhart Island the power which could be obtained from a redevelopment at Niagara Falls can be utilized as soon as it can be constructed. (q) With respect to control works at the outlet of Lake Erie a special international board of engineers composed of representatives from the United States and Canada should be appointed to make field investigations, model tests and other studies to determine the advisability of such works and their effect on existing conditions. However, the other units of redevelopment can be constructed independently and in advance of the control works. (r) Selection of a final over-all plan for redevelopment for power in the United States at Niagara Falls is not advisable until the total diversions of water for power purposes are permanently authorized and any restrictions in use with respect thereto agreed upon. 

SCOPE OF THIS REPORT 

7. It is sought in this report to review the existing situation with respect to diversions of water at Niagara Falls for power purposes, and to present results of studies of the possibilities for more complete utilization of the water resources of the Niagara River for

power purposes in the United States consistent with the preservation of the scenic beauty of Niagara Falls and the interests of navigation.

Included within the scope of the report are investigations and/or considerations of: (a) Availability of water for power purposes over and above that required, as indicated by the 

Special International Niagara Board, for the preservation of the scenic spectacle at the Falls and Lower Rapids and for the alleviation of ice conditions below the Falls. (b) Assumption of division of water as between the two countries. (c) The engineering, operating and economic aspects of a comprehensive scheme of development. (d) Efficiency of use of water by existing plants. (e) Possible markets in the United States for 

Niagara power. (f) Costs for transmission facilities to deliver the power to load centers in such markets. (g) Cost of Niagara power at the load centers. (h) Cost of producing power from alternative steam plants located at or near load centers. 

EXISTING WORKS 

Pover Development on the United States Side 8.

The Federal Power Commission on March 2, 1921, issued to The Niagara Falls Power Company a 50-year license for the development of power on the United States side of The Niagara River at 

Niagara Falls, New York, designated as Project No. 16. The existing

--  project under license consists of the Schoellkopf station located about 3,000 feet downstream from the American Falls, containing 

19 turbines rated at 365,000 kilowatts (aggregate name plate ratings 336,050 kilowatts) operating under about 215 feet head, the Adams station comprising Powerhouses No. 1 and No. 2 located on a canal intake about 7,000 feet above the American Falls and containing 21 turbines with aggregate installed and rated capacity of 80,000 kilowatts operating under a net head of 135 feet, water conduits, substations, transmission lines and other facilities. 

Water is diverted to the Schoellkopf station from the Niagara River at Port Day above the Falls by means of a tunnel and canal, each about 4,500 feet long and having a maximum combined capacity of about 23,500 cubic feet per second. The turbines of the Adams station are set in common pits about 177 feet below the surface of the ground. 

These pits discharge into a flow-line tunnel of about 10,000 cubic feet per second maximum capacity extending about 7,000 feet to a point in the Gorge 1,200 feet below the American Falls. 

9. The Niagara Falls Power Company is authorized to divert 

19,725 cubic feet per second, during the terms of the license, for power purposes and an additional 12,500 cubic feet per second for temporary use during the national emergency. The company also has temporary authorization to continue to divert and use an additional 

275 cubic feet per second originally granted October 1, 1928. The total diversion now utilized through both plants is 32,500 cubic

feet per second which amount of water is subject to downward revision to conform to the 20,000 cubic feet per second provided for in the treaty between the United States of America and Great Britain relating to boundary waters between the United States and Canada, signed at Washington, January 11, 1909, and proclaimed by the President of the 

United States on May 13, 1910.

Power Development on the Canadian Side 

10. On the Canadian side of the Niagara River there are four hydroelectric plants, namely, Queenston, Ontario, Toronto and Rankine developments. The Queenston, Ontario and Toronto plants are operated by The Hydro-Electric Power Commission of Ontario. The Rankine plant is operated by the Canadian Niagara Power Company, a corporation controlled by The Niagara Falls Power Company of New York. 

11. The Queenston development, the largest power plant on the 

Niagara River, was completed with the installation of the tenth unit in 1930. The development comprises an intake structure in the Niagara 

River at Chippawa; the deepening and enlarging of the Welland River with a reversal of its flow for 4 miles; a canal about 8-3/4 miles long from Montrose on the Welland River to the fore bay and screen house which are situated on the cliff above the powerhouse; and penstocks and a powerhouse about one mile south of the Village of Queenston. 

The total installed capacity is about 373,000 kilowatts. At maximum load the plant operates under a head of about 294 feet. The normal gross head is 315 feet.

-. The Ontario development consists of an intake in the 

Niagara River at a point about a mile above the crest of the 

Horseshoe Falls, three water conduits about 6,500 feet long, sixteen steel penstocks varying in length from 225 to 317 feet, and a powerhouse located below the Canadian Falls having fifteen generating units with a total installed capacity of about 138,000 kilowatts. 

The normal gross head is 202 feet and the normal net operating head under full load condition is 180 feet. 

13. The Toronto development located in the vicinity of the Falls consists of an intake in Niagara River, a wheel pit 416 feet long, eleven penstocks 127 feet long, a tailrace tunnel about 2,690 feet long, and a powerhouse containing eleven units having a total installed capacity of about 108,000 kilowatts. The plant operates under an average effective head of about 137 feet. 

14. The Rankine development consists of intake turbines located in a wheel pit 567 feet long and 171 feet below the generator floor, a tailrace tunnel 2,164 feet long and a powerhouse containing 11 units with a total installed capacity of about 80,000 kilowatts. 

The eighth annual report of The Power Authority of the State of New York states that the Canadian Niagara Power Company is licensed by Canada to produce 100,000 horsepower (75,000 kilowatts) which requires about 10,000 cubic feet per second in the Rankine plant, about 5,000 cubic feet per second of which is applicable to the 35,062 kilowatt contract with the present Buffalo Niagara Electric Corporation which

-. expires September 30, 1974. 

15. The DeCew Falls plant of The Hydro-Electric Power Commission of Ontario receives its water supply from Lake Erie through the Welland Canal. Large storage basins, principally Gibson Lake, enable the uniform supply from the Canal to be used at a varying rate through the plant. The old station has an installed capacity of about 37,000 kilowatts. Work commenced in September 1941 on the enlargement of the DeCew Falls development and the initial installation was completed in 1943. The plant is situated in the vicinity of St. Catherines, with the power canal intake at Allanburg on the Welland Canal and the outlet at Port Dalhousie on Lake Ontario. Twelve Mile Creek, which has been enlarged to its junction with the old Welland Canal, forms the tailrace of the plant through which the water flows to Lake Ontario through Port Dalhousie Harbor. The enlargement to the existing 

DeCew Falls development provided for the installation of one 48,000 kilowatt unit when operating under an ultimate head of 280 feet with provision for a second unit. The initial operation was under a head of 265 feet. 

16. The second unit in the new DeCew Falls plant was brought into service in September 1947 with a capacity of 64,000 kilowatts. 

The total capacity of the new plant is about 112,000 kilowatts. 

Gibson Lake has been enlarged by having the upper limit of its level raised, the reservoir canal widened and the tailrace enlarged, thereby increasing the operating head for both mmits in the new plant from 265 to 280 feet.

Submerged Weir 

18. The Special International Niagara Board proposed remedial works to redistribute the flow in the American and Canadian channels and Falls. The program recommended by the Board with respect to the construction of remedial works in conjunction with additional withdrawals will be found in Appendix D. The submerged weir was the first of the remedial works completed and was constructed during the period 1942-1944 for the purpose of (1) increasing the output of power by the restoration of water levels in Grass Island Pool and (2) increasing the flow over the American Falls and thereby improve its scenic appearance. By an exchange of notes dated October 27, 1941, the Governments of the United States and Canada agreed to commence the construction of the submerged weir in the Niagara River above Niagara Falls in 1942. The United States Senate approved this agreement on November 

27, 1941. The task of construction was assigned jointly to the 

United States St. Lawrence Advisory Committee and the Canadian

Temporary Great Lakes-St. Lawrence Basin Committee. On January 23, 

1942 the Committees submitted a joint report recommending the construction of a submerged weir in the Chippawa-Grass Island pool to raise the water surface in the pool one foot more or less at Standard Low Water, as envisaged in the report of the Special International Niagara Board dated June 22, 1928. The Committees report was approved by the President on January 27, 1942, and by the Prime Minister on 

February 28, 1942. On March 13, 1942, at a meeting of representatives

- · of the two Governments in Niagara Falls, Ontario, a committee known as the Construction Subcommittee was formed. The Construction Subcommittee was charged with the responsibility of supervision of the construction of the weir. Work was commenced in 1942 and completed in 1944. 

19. The instructions to the Construction Subcommittee were to build a weir that would raise the water level at Grass Island one foot more or less at Standard Lower Water, which corresponds to a discharge of 124,000 cubic feet per second over the Upper Cascades. 

With the diversions in effect during 1947 and 1948, this corresponds to a total river discharge of 170,000 cubic feet per second from Lake Erie. Pertinent information with respect to the design and construction of the weir and its effect on the water levels in Niagara River is contained in the Final Progress Report of the Construction Subcommittee dated September 1, 1948. The Final Progress

Report of the Construction Subcommittee states that the construction phase of the Niagara submerged weir project ended on January 7, 1947 when the removal of the causeway was completed, however, a small portion of the causeway in deep water adjacent to Tower Island was retained to supplement the effect of the weir, and that the net total cost of construction of the submerged weir was $785,446.41, or an amount less than the estimated cost of $879,429.38. The report concludes that the work of the Construction Subcommittee has attained the objectives of the two Governments, that no adverse effects

- have resulted and lake levels have not been appreciably affected, and states (a) "The rise in water level due to the construction of the weir has increased the power output of those plants on both sides of the river that have their intakes upstream from the weir and has reduced trouble from ice. (b) The weir has nearly doubled the flow over the American Falls and thereby has improved its appearance. (c) This is the final report of the Subcommittee and reflects the final costs of the submerged weir project."

PHYSIOGRAPHY 

22. The region of the Great Lakes is underlain with sandstone, shale, conglomerate, and limestone deposited during the early Paleozoic Age. At the close of the Paleozoic Age the interior of the continent was uplifted causing new drainage systems. During the 

Pleistocene Age which followed, the identity of the old watersheds was obliterated by the continental ice sheets which covered the present Great Lakes area south to the Ohio River. As the ice sheet retreated to the north, the melting ice formed in successive stages the new Great Lakes drainage system. When the ice front receded north of the Great Lakes area, Lake Erie was separated and flowed over the Niagara escarpment to Lake Ontario. The Niagara River was then without Falls. When the Mohawk outlet became available, the waters in the Ontario basin were withdrawn to a lower level. As the waters fell below the top of the Niagara escarpment, in the vicinity of Lewiston, the Falls came into existence.

23. The Niagara limestone which is underlain by soft shales and sandstone is responsible for the escarpment. Undercutting at the base of the Falls has resulted in retreat of the Falls. The 

Falls are now about 160 feet high, but by the time they have retreated two miles farther upstream they will be less than 100 feet in height. At that place they will be entirely in the Niagara limestone. Undercutting will cease, and the Falls will change slowly into rapids. A fuller discussion of the physiography will be found in Appendix A.

REDEVELOPMENT IN UNITED STATES 

35. In any consideration of possible scheme of redevelopment at Niagara Falls for power purposes there are certain particular limitations and advantages peculiar to this resource which must be considered. Among the important limitations in addition to the obvious ones of total water availability and total drop (about 315 feet) from above the Falls to Lake Ontario, are those imposed by scenic consideration and ice difficulties. Navigation is an important consideration but is not a serious limiting factor. Municipal water supply, industries, water needs and other possible uses are of negligible effect. 

36. Undoubtedly, insofar as power production is concerned, limitations imposed on the use of water by scenic needs are the most important. A great deal of attention has been given this matter over a long number of years by many different boards, agencies and experts. It would now appear that conclusion as to the minimum needs of water for scenic purposes should be made with reasonable confidence. 

37. The second most important limitation on power production is ice conditions, although not second in point of physical difficulty with respect to operation of power plants and appurtenant work. 

38. Navigation limitations relate mostly to controlling depths of water and they were provided for in the assumptions for water available for power purposes. 

39. The natural advantages of the site are the concentration of potential head in a short river distance, a nearby right angle

· bend in the river and the large volume of flow of relatively uniform character.

Another potential advantage of the site lies in the possibility of storing water in Lake Erie for stream flow regulation and the availability of a pumped storage site near Lewiston. 

40. Existing power developments do not fully utilize the possibilities at the site either from the standpoint of use of presently authorized or temporary diversions, or in consideration of the maximum possible amounts of water available which may be diverted for power use. 

41. The scheme of development herein, shown on the Location 

Plan, provides for the full use of the water which might be available on the United States side and full exploitation of the advantages of the natural site. It includes consideration of water presently being diverted, permanent and temporary, and not fully utilizing the head available; additional water which might be used; and the regulating possibilities afforded by Lake Erie and pumped storage.

During periods of extra large flows when water is available it could be used to generate steam replacement energy. It could be placed in service in event of extreme need occurring after the redevelopment although it would take water away from the Falls and thus reduce the scenic spectacle to some undetermined degree.

The redevelopment of power under consideration in this report provides for construction of a new plant of 1,080,000 kilowatts capacity at Lewiston, a Pumped Storage plant having generating capacity of 130,000 kilowatts and a new Lower Niagara plant of 120,000 kilowatts utilizing the additional head from water which has passed through the Schoellkopf plant. Thus, the capacity from the redevelopment of Niagara could be utilized in less than ten years in central and western New York State. It is very probable, however, that additional Niagara output would be absorbed in less time in the western portion of the state since such low cost hydroelectric power would encourage greater use per customer and would encourage expansion of electrochemical and electrometallurgical loads. It is understood that many of such industries located in the Niagara Falls area have desired for several years to increase their consumption of electric energy.

They have been unable to do so, due to the lack of additional amounts of low cost hydroelectric power for expansion since the entire output of the existing hydroelectric plants has long since been allocated. Some of these types of industries have left the New York area to reestablish in areas where less expensive electric energy is available or have transferred all plant expansions to such areas.

1,660,000 kilowatts will be needed in the period from 1951 to 1960. Cost of Power from Alternative Sources 

109. In general, the value of power that may be developed at a new hydroelectric project may be measured by the cost of obtaining equivalent power from alternative sources. In the Niagara region it is expected that new steam-electric plants would constitute the principal alternative source of power supply, Therefore, the cost of producing power at modern steam-electric plants that could have been constructed at January 1949 costs to meet average conditions in the Niagara market areas has been estimated and is presented herein. 

110. The total annual cost of power supply available from alternative new steam-electric plants is considered to be made up of two components: (1) a "capacity cost" which is composed of fixed charges on the capital investment and that portion of the operating, maintenance, and fuel costs that varies with the size of the installation rather than with the amount of energy that is produced; and (2) an "energy cost" which is that portion of the cost of generation that varies with the volume of production and which is composed of the incremental costs for fuel, labor, supplies and maintenance.

Capacity Cost at Site of Steam-electric Plants in Central and Western New York

APPENDIX A PHYSIOGRAPHY 

1. The Niagara River, which forms a part of the boundary line between the State of New York and the Province of Ontario, is the natural outlet from the 263,500 square miles of the Great Lakes watershed above Lake Ontario. In its length of 36 miles from Lake Erie to 

Lake Ontario, the Niagara River drops 326 feet. The Niagara River begins at the outlet of Lake Erie where the river falls about four feet in three miles of restricted river section at Buffalo, New York. Here the river is 1500 feet wide and shallow and functions as a natural submerged weir which discharges in accordance with the level of Lake Erie. For the next 

17 miles the river falls four feet where the flow is divided by Grand Island into the Chippawa and Tonawanda Channels. At present water is diverted from the Niagara River in the vicinity of the Chippawa-Grass 

Island pool for power purposes.

This pool, located at the foot of Grand Island, 562 feet above mean sea level, is about three miles long and discharges over a submerged weir across part of the River. The flow is then through rapids for about a mile to the crest of Niagara Falls, 23 miles from Lake Erie. Niagara Falls plunges vertically to elevation 

340 at the Maid-of-the Mist pool, and thence for seven miles of rapids and whirlpool to Lewiston, New York where the river level is about elevation 245. The six mile reach of the river from Lewiston to Lake 

Ontario is deep and falls about one half a foot. 

2. The region of the Great Lakes is underlain with sandstone, shale, conglomerate and limestone deposited during the early Palezoic Age, during the time when the interior of the continent was uplifted causing new drainage

systems. During the Pleistocene Age which followed, the identity of the old watersheds was obliterated by the continental ice sheets which scoured and changed the earth's surface, and left the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence River as we know them today. 

3. During the Pleistocene Age the ice sheet covered the present 

Great Lakes area south to the Ohio River. Land surfaces previously above sea level were scoured by the moving ice to depths of 200 or 300 feet below sea level as evidenced by the present bottom of Lakes Superior, Michigan, Huron and Ontario. As the late Wisconsin ice sheet retreated to the North, the melting ice formed in successive stages the new Great Lakes drainage system. Morainal lakes were formed enlarging in area as the ice front moved North. The continental ice sheet caused changes in drainage as follows: (a) When the ice front reached the south shore of the present lakes the water drained by way of the Wisconsin, Rock, Illinois, Wabash, Susquehanna and Hudson Rivers. (b) After further retreat, Lake Whittlesey occupied the Lake Erie Basin and drained westerly to Lake Chicago and the Illinois River. (c) When the ice front receded north of Niagara River the water drained to the West through the Illinois River and easterly by way of the Finger Lakes to the Susquehanna River. (d) Further retreat caused lower lake levels and separate drainage; Lake Superior by way of the St. Croix River, Lake Chicago by way of the Illinois and Lake Erie along the edge of the ice front to the 

Mohawk and Hudson Rivers. (e) When the ice front receded North of the Great Lakes area,

- Lake Algonquin covered the present basins of Lakes Superior, Michigan and Huron. Drainage was by way of the Illinois River and the Ottawa River to the St. Lawrence Valley which, including Lake Ontario, was below sea level. 

Lake Erie was separated and flowed over the Niagara escarpment to Lake Ontario. The Niagara River was then without falls. (f) When the ice front had receded farther North into the Hudson Bay watershed, and the weight of ice was removed from the Great Lakes area, the land was uplifted, the Ottawa outlet abandoned and the present drainage system of the Great Lakes was established. 

4. The geological history of Niagara River and the Great Lakes is well described in "The Physiographic Provinces of North America" by Wallace W. Atwood published in 1940. 

5. "Niagara Falls came into existence in the midst of the history of the Great Lakes. When the waters from the basin of Lake Erie first flowed northward, the surface of the ancestral waters of Lake Ontario was 150 feet higher than the surface of the present lake, and the Niagara River was without falls. When the Mohawk outlet became availble, the waters in the Ontario basin were withdrawn to a lower level. At that time they built the beach ridge on which the present Ridge Road from Lewiston to Rochester is located. When the waters fell below the top of the 

Niagara escarpment, which is just south of Lewiston, the falls came into existence." 

6, "The Niagara limestone, which is underlain by soft shales 

Undercutting at the base and sandstone, is responsible for the escarpment. of the falls, such as is going on today, has caused block after block of the limestone to give way. As a result, the falls have retreated upstream

-- ·

for a distance of nearly seven miles, leaving a vertically walled gorge downstream from the Falls. The depth and width of that gorge represent the dimensions of the Falls at various stages in their retreat. 

At the Whirlpool Rapids the stream found soft glacial debris in an ancient valley, and the gorge was cut unusually wide in this unresisting material. A large round basin was excavated at one side of the main gorge, and today the river waters flow in a circular eddy in this basin before moving farther downstream." 

7. "The retreat of the Falls is now most rapid in the Canadian, or Horseshoe Falls, where it amounts to about four feet a year. Estimates based on historical records indicate that the time consumed in the retreat from Lewiston to the present site may have been as short as 10,000 years or as great as 25,000 years. Since the surface of the 

Niagara limestone declines to the southward about 35 feet per mile, the Falls are certain to have less height as they move upstream. They are now about 160 feet high, but by the time they have migrated two miles farther up the valley they will be less than 100 feet in height. 

At that place they will be entirely in the Niagara limestone. Undercutting will cease, and the Falls will change slowly into rapids." 

8. The gradual process of uplift of the land in the Great Lakes region since the ice age has amounted to several hundred feet as shown by ancient beaches along the north shores of the Great Lakes. It is the opinion of geologists that uplift is still taking place. The matter of earth uplift and tilt has been carefully studied in the report dated 1926 by Mr. John R. Freeman, "Regulation of the Great Lakes"

9.

Mr. Freeman concludes on the basis of daily gaging for the past 50 years that earth-tilt has been going on throughout all the Great Lakes region. He attributes this earth-tilt as partial cause for change in relative lake levels and discharges. According to Mr. Freeman the earth has been tilting upward to the north and east during the past 50 years, from 2 to 1 foot per 100 miles. The axis of tilt he places on a line a little south from Cleveland to Chicago. This action has tended to deepen the lake at Chicago possibly 0.74 foot in 50 years. At Georgian 

Bay the water depth is 0.60 foot less than 50 years ago. In Toronto 

Harbor water depth has increased about a foot. The estimate of increase in elevation of lake outlets from 1860 to 1925 is as follows: Lake 

Superior 1.91, Lake Huron 1.07, Lake Erie 1.29 and Lake Ontario 2.03 feet. 

10. The geological facts including earth tilt are similarly described by Mr. F. N. Menefee in his book entitled "The St. Lawrence 

Seaway." Mr. Menefee refers to the prediction of the geologist Mr. Gilbert "that if earth tilt should continue, the discharges of all lakes except Ontario into the Mississippi via Chicago would be re-established at some distantly future date." Mr. Menefee concludes regarding the history of seismic disturbances, "aside from the gradual raising of the northern rim of the basin which has slowly changed the lake shores and outlets over thousands of years, there is nothing to indicate danger to engineering structures from violent quakes.'

APPENDIX C TREATIES AND AGREEMENTS 

1. The treaties and agreements described hereinafter relate in part or solely to the diversion of waters of the Niagara River for power purposes and to the preservation of the scenic spectacle of Niagara 

Falls. According to the Special International Niagara Board Report of 

1928, the American Civic Association in 1905 called attention of both the United States and the Canadian Governments to their duty to the world in controlling and preserving the scenic integrity of the Falls of Niagara against further depletion. Consequently the Congress of the 

United States on June 29, 1906, passed the Burton Act "For the control and regulation of the waters of Niagara River, for the preservation of Niagara Falls, and for other purposes." This act assumed jurisdiction of the Niagara River to the international boundary line, and limited diversions by power plants on the United States side to that required for plants then in operation or under construction, and also limited importation of Canadian power so as to provide for an equitable adjustment of diversions on each side of the Niagara River. The result of this Act was the immediate assumption of control for the United States by the engineering force of the then War Department and the eventual negotiation and ratification of the International Boundary Waters Treaty of 1909-1910. 

2. On May 13, 1910, the International Boundary Waters Treaty between the United States of America and Great Britain became effective. 

This Treaty is included in its entirety at the end of this appendix. 

Article V of this treaty provides that the total withdrawal of water

- for power purposes from above Niagara Falls must not exceed a daily average of 56,000 cubic feet per second, of which 20,000 cubic feet per second was allotted to the United States and 36,000 cubic feet per second to Canada. Article VIII of the Treaty provides that United States and 

Canada "shall have, each on its own side of the boundary, equal and similar rights in the use of the waters hereinbefore defined as boundary waters" subject to a specified order of precedence in which "Uses for power and for irrigation purposes" were third and last in order.

BETWEEN 

THE UNITED STATES AND GREAT BRITAIN RELATING TO BOUNDARY WATERS, AND QUESTIONS ARISING BETWEEN THE UNITED STATES AND CANADA By The President Of The United States Of America A PROCLAMATION WHEREAS a Treaty between the United States of America and His 

Majesty the King of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and of the British Dominions beyond the Seas, Emperor of India, to prevent disputes regarding the use of boundary waters and to settle all questions which are now pending between the United States and the Dominion of Canada involving the rights, obligations, or interests of either in relation to the other or to the inhabitants of the other, along their common frontier, and to make provision for the adjustment and settlement of all such questions as may here-after arise, was concluded and signed by their respective Plenipotentiaries at Washington on the eleventh day of January, one thousand nine hundred and nine, the original of which Treaty is word for word as follows: 

The United States of America and His Majesty the King of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and of the British Dominions beyond the Seas, Emperor of India, being equally desirous to prevent disputes regarding the use of boundary waters and to settle all questions which are now pending between the United States and the Dominion of Canada involving the rights, obligations, or interests of either in relation to the other or to the inhabitants of the other, along their common frontier, and to make provision for the adjustment and settlement of all such questions as may hereafter arise, have resolved to conclude a treaty in furtherance of these ends, and for that purpose have appointed as their respective plenipotentiaries:

The President of the United States of America, Elihu Root, Sec retary of State of the United States; and 

His Britannic Majesty, the Right Honorable, James Bryce, O.M., his Ambassador Extraordinary and Plenipotentiary at Washington; 

Who, after having communicated to one another their full powers, found in good and due form, have agreed upon the following articles: 

PRELIMINARY ARTICLE 

For the purposes of this treaty boundary waters are defined as the waters from main shore to main shore of the lakes and rivers and connecting waterways, or the portions thereof, along which the international boundary between the United States and the Dominion of Canada passes, including all bays, arms, and inlets thereof, but not including tributary waters which in their natural channels would flow into such lakes, rivers, and waterways, or waters flowing from such lakes, rivers,

- and waterways, or the waters of rivers flowing across the boundary.

ARTICLE I 

The High Contracting Parties agree that the navigation of all navigable boundary waters shall forever continue free and open for the purposes of commerce to the inhabitants and to the ships, vessels, and boats of both countries equally, subject, however, to any laws and regulations of either country, within its own territory, not inconsistent with such privilege of free navigation and applying equally and without discrimination to the inhabitants, ships, vessels, and boats of both countries.

It is further agreed that so long as this treaty shall remain in force, this same right of navigation shall extend to the waters of Lake Michigan and to all canals connecting boundary waters, and now existing or which may hereafter be constructed on either side of the line. Either of the High Contracting Parties may adopt rules and regulations governing the use of such canals within its own territory and may charge tolls for the use thereof, but all such rules and regulations and all tolls charged shall apply alike to the subjects or citizens of the High Contracting Parties and the ships, vessels, and boats of both of the High Contracting Parties, and they shall be placed on terms of equality in the use thereof. 

ARTICLE II 

Each of the High Contracting Parties reserves to itself or to the several State Governments on the one side and the Dominion or Provincial Governments on the other as the case may be, subject to any treaty provisions now existing with respect thereto, the exclusive jurisdiction and control over the use and diversion, whether temporary or permanent, of all waters on its own side of the line which in their natural channels would flow across the boundary or into boundary waters; but it is agreed that any interference with or diversion from their natural channel of such waters on either side of the boundary, resulting in any injury on the other side of the boundary, shall give rise to the same rights and entitle the injured parties to the same legal remedies as if such injury took place in the country where such diversion or interference occurs; but this provision shall not apply to cases already existing or to cases expressly covered by special agreement between the parties hereto. 

It is understood, however, that neither of the High Contracting Parties intends by the foregoing provision to surrender any right, which it may have, to object to any interference with or diversions of waters on the other side of the boundary the effect of which would be productive of material injury to the navigation interests on its own side of the boundary.

ARTICLE III 

It is agreed that, in addition to the uses, obstructions, and diversions heretofore permitted or hereafter provided for by special agreement between the Parties, hereto, no further or other uses or obstructions or diversions, whether temporary or permanent, of boundary waters on either side of the line, affecting the natural level or flow of boundary waters on the other side of the line, shall be made except by authority

- 

of the United States or the Dominion of Canada within their respective jurisdictions and with the approval, as hereinafter provided, of a joint commission, to be known as the International Joint Commission. 

The foregoing provisions are not intended to limit or interfere with the existing rights of the Government of the United States on the one side and the Government of the Dominion of Canada on the other, to undertake and carry on governmental works in boundary waters for the deepening of channels, the construction of break-waters, the improvement of harbors, and other government works for the benefit of commerce and navigation, provided that such works are wholly on its own side of the line and do not materially affect the level or flow of the boundary waters on the other, nor are such provisions intended to interfere with the ordinary use of such waters for domestic and sanitary purposes. 

ARTICLE IV 

The High Contracting Parties agree that, except in cases provided for by special agreement between them, they will not permit the construction or maintenance on their respective sides of the boundary of any remedial or protective works or any dams or other obstructions in waters flowing from boundary waters or in waters at a lower level than the boundary in rivers flowing across the boundary, the effect of which is to raise the natural level of waters on the other side of the boundary unless the construction or maintenance thereof is approved by the aforesaid International 

Joint Commission.

It is further agreed that the waters herein defined as boundary waters and waters flowing across the boundary shall not be polluted on either side to the injury of health or property on the other. 

ARTICLE V 

The High Contracting Parties agree that it is expedient to limit the diversion of waters from the Niagara River so that the level of Lake Erie and the flow of the stream shall not be appreciably affected. It is the desire of both Parties to accomplish this object with the least possible injury to investments which have already been made in the construction of power plants on the United States side of the river under grants of authority from the State of New York, and on the Canadian side of the river under licenses authorized by the Dominion of Canada and the Province of Ontario. 

So long as this treaty shall remain in force, no diversion of the waters of the Niagara River above the Falls from the natural course and stream thereof shall be permitted except for the purposes and to the extent hereinafter provided. 

The United States may authorize and permit the diversion within the State of New York of the waters of said river above the Falls of Niagara, for power purposes, not exceeding in the aggregate a daily diversion at the rate of twenty thousand cubic feet of water per second. 

The United Kingdom, by the Dominion of Canada, or the Province of Ontario, may authorize and permit the diversion with the Province of Ontario of the waters of said river above the Falls of Niagara, for power purposes, not exceeding in the aggregate a daily diversion at the rate of thirty-six thousand cubic feet of water per second.

The prohibitions of this article shall not apply to the diversion of water for sanitary or domestic purposes, or for the service of canals for the purposes of navigation.

ARTICLE VI 

The High Contracting Parties agree that the St. Mary and Milk 

Rivers and their tributaries (in the State of Montana and the Provinces of Alberta and Saskatchewan) are to be treated as one stream for the purposes of irrigation and power, and the waters thereof shall be apportioned equally between the two countries, but in making such equal apportionment more than half may be taken from one river and less than half from the other by either country so as to afford a more beneficial use to each. It is further agreed that in the division of such waters during the irrigation season, between the 1st of April and 31st of October, inclusive, annually, the United States is entitled to a prior appropriation of 500 cubic feet per second of the waters of the Milk River, or so much of such amount as constitutes three-fourths of its natural flow, and that Canada is entitled to a prior appropriation of 500 cubic feet per second of the flow of St. Mary River, or so much of such amount as constitutes three-fourths of its natural flow.

The channel of the Milk River in Canada may be used at the convenience of the United States for the conveyance, while passing through Canadian territory, of waters diverted from the St. Mary River. The provisions of Article II of this treaty shall apply to any injury resulting to property in Canada from the conveyance of such waters through the Milk River.

The measurement and apportionment of the water to be used by each country shall from time to time be made jointly by the properly constituted reclamation officers of the United States and the properly constituted irrigation officers of His Majesty under the direction of the International Joint Commission.

ARTICLE VII 

The High Contracting Parties agree to establish and maintain an International Joint Commission of the United States and Canada composed of six commissioners, three on the part of the United States appointed by the President thereof, and three on the part of the United Kingdom appointed by His Majesty on the recommendation of the Governor in Council of the 

Dominion of Canada.

ARTICLE VIII 

This International Joint Commission shall have jurisdiction over and shall pass upon all cases involving the use or obstruction or diversion of the waters with respect to which under Articles III and IV of this treaty the approval of this Commission is required, and in passing upon such cases the Commission shall be governed by the following rules or principles which are adopted by the High Contracting Parties for this purpose:

-The High Contracting Parties shall have, each on its own side of the boundary, equal and similar rights in the use of the waters hereinbefore defined as boundary waters. 

The following order of precedence shall be observed among the various uses enumerated hereinafter for these waters, and no use shall be permitted which tends materially to conflict with or restrain any other use which is given preference over it in this order of precedence: (1) Uses for domestic and sanitary purposes; (2) Uses for navigation, including the service of canals for the purposes of navigation; (3) Uses for power and for irrigation purposes.

The foregoing provisions shall not apply to or disturb any existing uses of boundary waters on either side of the boundary. 

The requirement for an equal division may in the discretion of the Commission be suspended in cases of temporary diversions along boundary waters at points where such equal division can not be made advantageously on account of local conditions, and where such diversion does not diminish elsewhere the amount available for use on the other side. 

The Commission in its discretion may make its approval in any case conditional upon the construction of remedial or protective works to compensate so far as possible for the particular use or diversion proposed, and in such cases may require that suitable and adequate provision, approved by the Commission, be made for the protection and indemnity against injury of any interests on either side of the boundary. 

In cases involving the elevation of the natural level of waters on either side of the line as a result of the construction or maintenance on the other side of remedial or protective works or dams or other obstructions in boundary waters or in waters flowing therefrom or in waters below the boundary in rivers flowing across the boundary, the Commission shall require, as a condition of its approval thereof, that suitable and adequate provision, approved by it, be made for the protection and indemnity of all interests on the other side of the line which may be injured thereby. 

The majority of the Commissioners shall have power to render a decision. In case the Commission is evenly divided upon any question or matter presented to it for decision, separate reports shall be made by the Commissioners on each side to their own Government. The High Contracting 

Parties shall thereupon endeavor to agree upon an adjustment of the question or matter of difference, and if an agreement is reached between them it shall be reduced to writing in the form of a protocol, and shall be communicated to the Commissioners, who shall take such further proceedings as may be necessary to carry out such agreement. 

ARTICLE IX 

The High Contracting Parties further agree that any other questions or matters of difference arising between them involving the rights, obligations, or interests of either in relation to the other or to the inhabitants of the other, along the common frontier between the United States and the Dominion of Canada, shall be referred from time to time to the International Joint Commission for examination and report, whenever

- either the Government of the United States or the Government of the Dominion of Canada shall request that such questions or matters of difference be so referred.

The International Joint Commission is authorized in each case so referred to examine into and report upon the facts and circumstances of the particular questions and matters referred, together with such conclusions and recommendations as may be appropriate, subject, however, to any restrictions or exceptions which may be imposed with respect thereto by the terms of the reference. 

Such reports of the Commission shall not be regarded as decisions of the questions or matters so submitted either on the facts or the law, and shall in no way have the character of an arbitral award. 

The Commission shall make a joint report to both Governments in all cases in which all or a majority of the Commissioners agree, and in case of disagreement the minority may make a joint report to both Governments, or separate reports to their respective Governments. 

In case the Commission is evenly divided upon any question or matter referred to it for report, separate reports shall be made by the Commissioners on each side to their own Government. 

ARTICLE X 

Any questions or matters of difference arising between the High Contracting Parties involving the rights, obligations, or interests of the United States or of the Dominion of Canada either in relation to each other or to their respective inhabitants, may be referred for decision to the International Joint Commission by the consent of the two Parties, it being understood that on the part of the United States any such action will be by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, and on the part of His Majesty's Government with the consent of the Governor General in Council. In each case so referred, the said Commission is authorized to examine into and report upon the facts and circumstances of the particular questions and matters referred, together with such conclusions and recommendations as may be appropriate, subject, however, to any restrictions or exceptions which may be imposed with respect thereto by the terms of the reference.

A majority of the said Commission shall have power to render a decision or finding upon any of the questions or matters so referred. 

If the said Commission is equally divided or otherwise unable to render a decision or finding as to any questions or matters so referred, it shall be the duty of the Commissioners to make a joint report to both Governments, or separate reports to their respective Governments, showing the different conclusions arrived at with regard to the matters or questions so referred, which questions or matters shall thereupon be referred for decision by the High Contracting Parties to an umpire chosen in accordance with the procedure prescribed in the fourth, fifth and sixth paragraphs of Article XLV of The Hague Convention for the pacific settlement of international disputes, dated October 18, 1907. Such umpire shall have power to render a final decision with respect to those matters

and questions so referred on which the Commission failed to agree.

ARTICLE XI 

A duplicate original of all decisions rendered and joint reports made by the Commission shall be transmitted to and filed with the Secretary of State of the United States and the Governor General of the Dominion of Canada, and to them shall be addressed all communcations of the Commission.

ARTICLE XII 

The International Joint Commission shall meet and organize at Washington promptly after the members thereof are appointed, and when organized the Commission may fix such times and places for its meetings as may be necessary, subject at all times to special call or direction by the two Governments. Each Commissioner, upon the first joint meeting of the Commission after his appointment, shall, before proceeding with the work of the Commission, make and subscribe a solemn declaration in writing that he will faithfully and impartially perform the duties imposed upon him under this treaty, and such declaration shall be entered on the records of the proceedings of the Commission. 

The United States and Canadian sections of the Commission may each appoint a secretary, and these shall act as joint secretaries of the Commission at its joint sessions, and the Commission may employ engineers and clerical assistants from time to time as it may deem advisable. The salaries and personal expenses of the Commission and of the secretaries shall be paid by their respective Governments, and all reasonable and necessary joint expenses of the Commission, incurred by it, shall be paid in equal moieties by the High Contracting Parties. 

The Commission shall have power to administer oaths to witnesses, and to take evidence on oath whenever deemed necessary in any proceeding, or inquiry, or matter within its jurisdiction under this treaty, and all parties interested therein shall be given convenient opportunity to be heard, and the High Contracting Parties agree to adopt such legislation as may be appropriate and necessary to give the Commission the powers above mentioned on each side of the boundary, and to provide for the issue of subpoenas and for compelling the attendance of witnesses in proceedings before the Commission. The Commission may adopt such rules of procedure as shall be in accordance with justice and equity, and may make such examination in person and through agents or employees as may be deemed advisable.

ARTICLE XIII 

In all cases where special agreements between the High Contracting Parties hereto are referred to in the foregoing articles, such agreements are understood and intended to include not only direct agreements between the High Contracting Parties, but also any mutual arrangement between the United States and the Dominion of Canada expressed by concurrent or reciprocal legislation on the part of Congress and the Parliament of the Dominion.

ARTICLE XIV 

The present treaty shall be ratified by the President of the United States of America, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate thereof, and by His Britannic Majesty. The ratifications shall be exchanged at Washington as soon as possible and the treaty shall take effect on the date of the exchange of its ratifications. It shall remain in force for five years, dating from the day of exchange of ratifications, and thereafter until terminated by twelve months written notice given by either High Contracting Party to the other.

APPENDIX G ICE CONDITIONS in the NIAGARA RIVER 

Available data and reports on ice conditions in the Niagara River were collected and analyzed by the Special International Niagara Board and the results of its analyses are set forth in detail in Senate 

Document No. 128, 71st Congress, 2nd Session. Information is contained 

1918", in "Niagara Power, History of Niagara Falls Power Company, 1886 

by Edward Dean Adams, and in a paper "Effect of Winds and Ice on Niagara 

River Flow" by Mr. Harland C. Woods, Principal Engineer, Buffalo District

Engineer Office, Corps of Engineers. In connection with this study Mr. 

Woods has further commented concerning ice conditions particularly during the last 20 years. 

1. Ice on Lake Erie generally starts to form during the latter part of December. The Lake Erie ice field extends usually from the harbor of Buffalo to the neighborhood of Dunkirk. Early in the season thin ice begins to form in the bays and lower end of the lake in the vicinity of Buffalo and with continued cold weather it gradually thickens and extends unless broken up by storms or sudden variations in lake level, in which case a considerable portion passes down the rapids where it is broken up into comparatively small pieces. In the early winter there is also a considerable amount of frazil formed near the outlet of the lake and in the rapids, which also passes down the river. As the winter season progresses the thickening lake ice is blown toward the outlet by southwest winds, and is piled up upon the shallow Horseshoe Reef at the inlet to the Niagara River, where it forms an ice jam extending from near the bottom to several feet above the surface of the water. In some instances

-- 
- the bergs have been noted 20 to 30 feet in height. These ice jams seriously interfere with the opening of navigation, the average date of opening in the spring, at Buffalo being about April 9. During December and January frazil and slush ice form in the river, especially after snow storms. This kind of ice frequently forms in enormous volumes and packs to depths of 6 to 8 feet. 

2. From time to time during the winter ice which has been lodged on 

Horseshoe Reef is loosened due to the effects of storms and variations in the lake level. The loosened sections pass down the river and are generally broken up into small pieces by the action of the rapids and through contact with bridge piers. Sometimes cakes of ice lodge against the Buffalo waterworks intake crib and the bridge piers, but only for short periods. 

Between Black Rock and Chip- 3. There does not appear to be any great tendency on the part of the ice from Lake Erie to form jams in the river between Horseshoe 

Reef and the Chippawa-Grass Island Pool. pawa, ice forms in the different bays and remains there until disturbed by sudden variations in river level or by strong winds, in which case the ice cover of a bay may be swept ut and pass down the river or may be lodged against either shore due to cross winds. A considerable accumulation of ice sometimes takes place at Chippawa due to a strong southeast wind, or in the vicinity of Grass Island and down to the Three 

Sister Islands when there is a strong west or southwest wind. When there are no strong winds to force the ice to either shore it passes down the channel and over the Falls without causing any obstruction in the upper river.

1184. The amount of ice formed and the amount passing down the river depends entirely on the meteorological conditions throughout any partic ular season. During times of heavy runs of ice in the river there may be considerable accumulations of ice in the bays on either side due to wind from the opposite shore, and jams form in the lower river due to an on-the-shore wind from Lake Ontario. In some years it has been noted that due to frequent east winds at the end of the season the ice in Lake Erie has been held there until melted, no particularly heavy run being released from the lake, although throughout the season there may have been small quantities passing down the river from time to time. 

5. In general, the ice problem in the Niagara River is a serious one from the standpoint of power development, but ways and means have been developed for combating the troubles and maintaining practically continuous output from the power plants. Usually the river is able to clear itself of ice and only when a combination of low levels, low temperatures, wind, and large volumes of ice cause abnormal conditions are jams formed for at most a few days. Ice floes going down the river have caused damage to real property, cut communications and reduced output at the power plants, thus causing losses to the public, the power companies and to industries. 

6. Ice jams sometimes occur at the head of the rapids above the 

Falls due to ice floes grounding in the shallow water above the head of Goat Island. The worst condition occurs when west or southwest winds on Lake Erie increase both the outflow from the lake and the amount of ice in the river. The high water piles up the ice at the jam and when the

wind and flow subside the jam settles, and this tends to shut off the flow to the American Fall as well as to the intake of the Niagara Falls Power Company plant. While ice jams are frequent at this point, it is seldom that they seriously affect the flow with the preventative measures used. 

Immediately afterwards 7. In the lower Niagara River an ice jam or bridge forms almost every year in the Maid-of-the-Mist Pool at the upper bridge, but below this the ice as a rule does not jam in the river. There are, however, two or three occasions on record on which jams of considerable extent have been caused by a congestion at the shoals where the river enters Lake Ontario. Below the escarpment the current in the river is comparatively slow and when a large amount of ice is running and the weather is severe, any retardation or holding up of the ice due to winds from Lake Ontario will give it time to freeze into a solid mass. the jam will start to build up, as it has on two or three recorded occasions, to a point above the Lewiston Bridge, causing a considerable rise in the water level in that reach and for some distance farther upstream. During the winter of 1925 the backwater from the jam attained a height of about 23 feet near the Queenston plant of The Hydro-Electric Power Commission of Ontario and extended as far upstream as Devil's Hole. 

The extent of ice jams in the lower Niagara River depends upon the amount of ice brought down and upcn climatological conditions. 

In this connection the power plants located at the Falls help to alleviate ice jams by providing additional flow to flush ice through the lower Gorge. 

8. Ice in the Niagara River may be one of two origins, river ice or lake ice. River ice forms only in the more quiet bays and backwater areas and is carried away by the current at the next change in wind

or fluctuation in water elevation. It is thin and causes no trouble. 

Lake ice may be of two types, thin surface ice or windrowed ice. Lake surface ice forms when there is quite cold weather and may be as much as 9 inches or a foot thick, Any of this ice that gets into the river normally causes little difficulty. 

9. Windrowed ice is formed by storms breaking up the surface ice and blowing it to the vicinity of the shore, usually the Buffalo end of the lake and piling it up there. It is packed together and on edge and may extend from the bottom of the lake to 20 or 30 feet above the water surface. The waves breaking over the piled up ice furnishes water that freezes and binds the cakes together. Then as the wind changes these ice masses may be carried out into the lake and back again, so that some get into the Niagara River. Or, if it is in the spring, after a few warm days, ice that is driven back up the lake may melt there and not come back to the outlet. This is the normal manner of disposal for Lake Erie ice. 

10. Prevailing winds in the Lake Eric area are southwesterly ones and blow the major dimension of the lake. The lake is relatively shallow and the water level in the easterly end of the lake is materially raised above the normal lake level by such winds and lowered by the less frequent northerly or easterly winds. In the winter, ice in Lake Erie is blown into the river by prevailing winds and away from the river by northeasters. 

11. The ice nearest the outlet is carried into the Niagara River but the greater part jams against the sides and bottom and arches across from the United States to the Canadian side and is unable to move as long

- as the wind pressure remains. A slight change in the direction or intensity of the wind may change the pressure lines in the floating mass so that some ice may be released from the jam to move down the river. 

A complete reversal of the wind would carry the whole ice field out into the lake, there to be melted if the lake water has been warmed. If it has not, when the wind again shifts to the southwest, the ice will be brought back to the outlet end. If it is brought back, some of the ice goes down the Niagara River, but the remainder is again windrowed and piled higher. 

12. If the ice flow into the river is composed of thin surface ice, even though large in extent, it is easily broken up in the rapids and falls and causes little inconvenience or damage. If, however, it is windrowed ice, the masses may be large enough to resist fracture when riding through the rapids and they may arrive at the intakes to the power plants in masses extending several feet above the water surface, which means they probably extend 18 or 20 feet below that surface. It is when runs of this type of ice occur that damage is done, especially if the temperature is low so that the river water is close to or below the freezing point of quiet water. Under these conditions, frazil ice forms on the bottom or on metal. The ice floes freeze to anything they touch and the operating personnel at the power plants may have trouble keeping their intakes clear. Whether they are successful or not depends greatly on the direction of the wind, and the tomperatures. Winds that blow the ice toward the outlet of Lake Eric also blow most of the ice in the river near Strawberry Island into the cast branch of the river around Grand Island and that which does go into the Canadian channel is blown to the Grand 

Island shore of the west branch.

- 

13. A recent example of ice troubles at power plant intakes occurred with a storm beginning on January 7, 1945, when with low temperatures, strong southwesterly winds, and a large fall of snow, the river was carrying a heavy load of ice from Lake Erie, and slush ice formed in the river from snow driven into it and the cooling action of the cold winds. Accumulation of this ice at the booms protecting the intake to the power plants on the United States side, was sufficient to cause a maximum drop of head estimated at 6 to 8 feet and correspondingly cut down the flow into the canals. 

14. When the ice arrives in the Grass Island Pool, prevailing winds blow so as to slow up the movement of the ice and drive it toward the United States shore and away from the Canadian shore. Consequently, when it comes within the influence of the flow in the channel to the Niagara Falls Power Company's intake the resultant movement is directly toward the intake and it is especially difficult to dislodge it and keep it moving down the river. More especially because the immediate down river area is shallow and ice floes ground there. 

15. At times when ice obstructs the entrance to the canals, however, it is impossible to run the plants at full capacity, tugs are used to push the ice away from the booms and keep it moving down the river, and it is not always possible to keep the entrance clear. On the occasion of the storm, January 7, 1945, the power output, of the United States power plant was substantially reduced whereas the Canadian plants was reduced a relatively small amount. 

16. With deep water at the booms, the tugs can more easily keep the ice brought in by westerly winds away from the protecting booms when the river is low, if the ice is floe or windrowed ice only. If,

· - 

however, the current is carrying slush as well as windrowed ice, the booms intercept the windrowed ice, which in turn intercepts the slush and the tugs cannot clear it as fast as it collects. It is then that serious reduction in power at the United States plants occurs. With easterly winds the ice will be carried to the intakes of the Canadian plants but the trouble generally lasts only a short while, as those winds prevont ice from the lake entering the river and what ice may be in the river from conditions prior to the easterly wind is carried past the intakes in about 

4 or 5 hours usually.

The final progress report of the Construction Subcommittee on the Niagara submerged weir states the following with regard to the effect of the submerged weir on ice conditions in the Niagara River: "The run of ice in the Niagara River during the winter and spring of 1947 was the heaviest of any year since work started on the weir in 1942. There were two separate periods of ice activity: the first during January and February, while ice was forming and before there was a complete cover on Lake Erie; the second during the spring break-up, from the middle of April to the middle of May. 

The various conditions that developed during these two periods will be described here in order to illustrate the effect of the weir during ice runs. "During the last week in January and the first week in February, large quantities of ice came down from the lake; however, the ice floes were not thick and little ice lodged on the weir. 

There was no ice trouble on the Canadian side of the river but because of the prevailing south and southwest winds, most of the ice gathered

- 

along the United States shore in front of the entrance to the power canals and at the head of the channel leading to the American Falls. There was considerable reduction in the flow over the American Falls and at times almost a complete stoppage. "The customary practice of the Niagara Falls Power Company is to open and maintain a channel through such an ice field by using ice breaking tugs and dynamite but so much ice accumulated at times that the channel could not be kept open. On the worst day, 6 February, the flow of water into the intakes was reduced 50 percent, with a corresponding reduction in the amount of electric power generated and the output was not back to normal for nearly a week; however, it is generally agreed that the reduction would have been still greater, and that it would have lasted longer, had it not been for the extra depth of water duc to the weir. "The spring ice run started early in April and continued intermittently until 15 May. The ice flocs were thick, many lodged for a time on the crest of the weir and many others struck the crest in passing over. At times there was ice along the whole 

1,200-foot length of the main part of the weir. but always there was an opening 300 feet wide at the south end of the weir near the 

Canadian shore where the crest was 2 feet lower. "The resulting rise in water lovel was great enough to give an appreciable increase in the output of the plants with intakes upstream from the weir but was never great enough to do any harm. During this period, there was little ice trouble at the intakes of the Niagara Falls Power Company. At times there was some reduction of output at the plants with intakes along the

- Canadian shore below the weir but this appeared to be no worse than usual for such a heavy run of ice and did not seem to be aggravated by the weir. In fact, the temporary storage of ice above the weir way have improved conditions below the weir, particularly in the Maid-of-the-Mist pool. Thus during the spring ice run considerable benefit was derived from the weir without any serious disadvantages."
