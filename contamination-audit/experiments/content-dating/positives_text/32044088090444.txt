Australia and New Zealand 

NE NIGHT near the end of 1941 

shadowy figures streamed down the gangways of troop-laden transports "somewhere in Australia." The first units of what has become a growing stream of American men and equipment had arrived in the island continent "down under." our supply route through the South Pacific to India, China, and Russia. 

That is why Australia and New Zealand are vital to America's defense, vital to the success of our war against fascism. That is why

American troops, planes, and ships have been sent to Australia. That is why General Douglas MacArthur on Bataan Peninsula was ordered by 

President Roosevelt to break through the enemy lines and go to Australia to lead the United Nations' forces in the Southwestern Pacific.

At that time Japan's forces were in the opening stages of their great push southward. They were still about 2,000 miles from Australia. 

Two and a half months later-by the middle of March, 1942—Malaya had been overrun; Singapore had fallen; overwhelming forces had Far Away crushed the United Nations' defenders in the Netherlands Indies.

Japanese troops had invaded the island of New Guinea, just north of 

Australia. Bombs were dropping on the northernmost Australian naval station, Port Darwin.

Australia and New Zealand stood alone, the last major bases in the Western Pacific. If these bases should fall, Japan would control both land and sea from the Arctic to the Antarctic. We should lose a vital base for offensive action against 

Japan. The Axis would sit astride

But for all their importance to 

America, most of us know very little about Australia and New Zealand. What sort of countries are these to which so many thousands of our troops and so much of our equipment are being sent? What sort of people are these allies of ours in far British Dominions?

In the first place, we know that Australia and New Zealand are far away and hard to get to. They are below the equator-in the Southern 

Hemisphere like much of Africa and 1

South America. To narrow it down, they are in the Southwest Pacific. (See the map in the center of this pamphlet.)

Eastern Australia is 7,000 miles from San Francisco, a trip of three weeks by ocean liner. 

Port Darwin on the tropical north coast of Australia is 3,400 miles from Tokyo. The Japanese-domi nated Netherlands Indies and Philippine Islands lie between.

It is more than 4,000 miles from 

Australia to Calcutta, the nearest port in embattled India. From Britain, by way of the Panama Canal, it is 12,500 miles to the great southeastern Australian port of Sydof Sydney. The British call Australia and

New Zealand the Antipodes, which comes from a Greek expression meaning "with the feet opposite," because they are on the opposite side of the globe from the Mother Country.

Newest New World 

Australia was not colonized until fairly recently. It was far from the world's main trade routes. The parts of its coast explored by Portuguese, Dutch, and English navigators during the seventeenth century were either too barren or too tropical to encourage settlers. Oddly enough, the American Revolution was partly responsible for the decision of the British government to send the first colonists. Here is how it happened.

Britain had been in the habit of transporting convicts to her American colonies. When we won our independence, she could no longer do this, and her jails became overcrowded. Consequently, the British authorities looked around for another place to send the inmates of their prisons. These were not all criminals by our present-day standards, but poor men in debt and out spoken champions of democracy. 

It so happened that the British explorer, Captain James Cook, with a party of scientists, had sailed around New Zealand and up the eastern coast of Australia in 1770. 

The eastern coast, which had been missed by previous explorers, is fertile and well-watered. Cook's party, on their return to England, gave the government a favorable report of what they had found. Therefore, when looking around for a good place to start a prison colony, the British decided to try the eastern coast of the great unknown continent. 

In 1787 Captain Arthur Phillip, who had been named governor of the new colony, set out from England with a party of 1,500 men, almost 800 of whom were convicts. 

They established the first permanent colony on Australian soil in January, 1788, on the spot where Sydney now stands. 2

Gradually, more and more free settlers arrived. The convicts who had served their terms got farms and grazing land, and struck out to build a new life for themselves in this newest New World.

New Zealand was not settled until somewhat later. Toward the close of the eighteenth century traders from many countries landed in New Zealand to get flax and timber from the native Maoris. In 1814 missionaries arrived from England. But it was not until 1840, when it looked as though the French were about to claim New Zealand, that the British government gave official backing to efforts to colonize the country on a large scale.

Some Contrasts 

Although we are in the habit of speaking of Australia and New

Zealand together, they are actually not very close to each other. Nor are they very much alike in their geography. 

New Zealand is more than 1,200 miles southeast of Australia-about as far as New York City is from 

Miami, Florida.

The whole of Australia, except for the island state of Tasmania, is a continent almost three million square miles in area, about the same size as the United States. Tasmania, with an area of 26,000 square miles (about the size of Scotland), is separated from the southeastern part of the continent by shallow Bass Strait, 200 miles in width. 

New Zealand, on the other hand, is a group of islands. By far the largest and most important are North 

Island and South Island, with an area of a little over 100,000 square miles, as big as New York, New 

Jersey, and Pennsylvania put together. They are separated from each other by Cook Strait, only 16 miles wide at its narrowest point.

There are still other points of contrast between Australia and New 

Zealand: 

Except for the Eastern Highlands, running north and south, parallel to the east coast, Australia has no real mountain ranges. New Zealand, particularly South Island, is extremely mountainous. There is a sharp contrast between the rugged peaks and glaciers of New Zealand and the flat Australian bush country with its red and yellow grasses and scattered, grey-green eucalyptus trees.

Australia is a dry country, except in Tasmania and the coastal regions of the north, east, and south. Much of the western interior is uninhabited desert. New Zealand's rainfall, on the other hand, is plentiful.

Later on we shall see the effect of geography and climate on the lives of the people, on the way they govern themselves, and on the part that Australia and New Zealand are playing in the war. 3

The "Fossil Continent" 

Until the settlement of Australia began in 1788, she had been cut off from the other continents for thousands of years. As a result, kinds of animals and plants which have long been extinct and exist only as fossils in other lands, still live and breed in Australia. 

The "fossil continent" is proud of its wild life. The kangaroo- "the animal that jumps when it walks, sits down when it stands up, and carries its young in its pocket"-shares a place on the national coat of arms with the emu, a huge, flightless bird almost as big as an ostrich. 

Perhaps the most popular Australian animal is the koala. The koala, which looks like a child's 

Teddy bear, carries its young in a pouch like the kangaroo and the

American opossum. This quaint, clumsy, furry little creature goes in for night life and spends its days dozing in the fork of a gum tree. 

One of the real wonders of Australia-and of the world is the platypus. This strange animal seems to be part bird, part reptile, and part mammal. It has a bill like a duck and webbed feet-but the toes end in claws. It has a tail like a beaver, is covered with thick fur, and gathers its food in streams. The female lays eggs, but suckles her young like other mammals.

One of the strangest of Australian birds is the lyrebird, who dances on a specially prepared mound, imitates the barking of a dog or the sound of an automobile horn, has a song unequalled by birds of other lands, and spreads his tail feathers out to look like a lyre. The kookaburra, or "laughing jackass," has a call like human laughter. 

New Zealand, too, has strange and attractive birds. But she has no native mammals at all. The ancestors of all the mammals in New

Zealand were brought from other 

Pacific islands, from Europe, and elsewhere.

Land and People 

The picture most of us have of the people down under has been formed by the newspaper photographs of two wars. The Anzacs (the initials of the Australia and 

New Zealand Army Corps of World 

War days), tall, lank, fighting men, their broad hat brims turned up at a jaunty angle, are familiar to us both by picture and reputation. At 

Gallipoli and in France during the last war, in Greece, Crete, Libya, and Malaya in the present war, the 

Anzacs became known as stubborn and resourceful fighters. And in the intervals between battles they won another reputation-as a wild and sometimes rowdy gang behind the lines.

The Anzacs appeal to us because in many of their faults and virtues they remind us of the pioneer farmers and cowhands who played such a large part in opening up our own West. And, actually, the Australians and New Zealanders have had to do a pioneering job like our own, and under even harder conditions. 

The obstacles they have had to overcome in building up a civilization in a far-off wilderness have done much to make the people and institutions down under what they are today. The job of creating homes and industries in their plains, mountains, forests, and deserts has made new nations-of Australians and New Zealanders-from the 

English, Scotch, and Irish immigrants who settled there. 

Not until twenty-five years after Captain Phillip's party of convicts and soldiers had landed in New South Wales did settlers cross the mountain barrier which separates the eastern seaboard from the rest of the Australian continent. On the western slopes of the mountains and the flat plains beyond they found excellent pasture for their sheep and cattle-particularly for their sheep. 

Where the rainfall was too light and the pasture too poor for cattle, sheep were able to thrive. And as settlers passed westward with their flocks, they found the country increasingly dry.

In fact, starting from almost any point on the 12,000 mile coastline of Australia, a traveller going inland for a few hundred miles will encounter desert. In many parts of the continent, particularly just west of the Eastern Highlands, life is made possible by wells which tap great underground artesian water supplies. Almost 9,000 of these wells have been bored. The early settlers on the east coast thought that there was a great inland sea across the mountains, but they did not suspect that it was underground. 

Australia's "dead heart”—the desert and semi-desert in the central, south-central, and western parts of the continent-is one reason why only seven million people live in this vast land. Another reason is that the northern part of the continent is in the tropics.

Colored laborers work the plantations of other tropical lands in the western Pacific-Malaya and the 

Netherlands Indies, for example.

But they are not allowed to come to Australia. And it is hard for white men to live and work in hot, wet climates.

In spite of this fact, a quarter of a million white men and women have made their homes in the northern tropics-mostly in Queensland—during the past twenty years.

They produce large quantities of sugar on the only sugar plantations in the world being worked 100 per cent by white labor.

Sheep, Gold, and

Railroads 

Cattle, gold, and railroads played a large part in opening up western 

America. Sheep, gold, and railroads-plus camels and, later, auto trucks for desert travel-played a similar part in expanding Australian settlement along and beyond the eastern and southeastern seaboards.

An enormous part of central Australia was first explored by sheep raisers hunting new pasture for their flocks.

But furnishing wool for English factories, profitable as it was, did not hold the dramatic promise of overnight wealth that can attract adventurous people by the thousands. Gold brought a real upsurge in Australian settlement. A year after our own gold rush to California in 1849, gold was discovered in 

New South Wales. Soon after, the richest mines in the world at that time were opened in Victoria. Prospectors from all over the world flocked to the gold fields. In 1850

Australia's population was less than half a million. By 1860, it had more than doubled. 

The gold rush brought not only settlers, but railroads. In 1854 the first trains began running in the 

Southeast. Soon they spread into the back country, helping to open up the land for agriculture and making it easier to transport wool to the ports of Sydney and Melbourne. 

In 1892 and 1893 the gold fever broke out again, this time in western Australia, where the continent's richest gold fields were discovered.

This helped in the settlement and development of the West much as the earlier gold strikes had done in the East. 

The railroads run to whatever parts of Australia have enough water and a good enough climate or rich enough mining areas-to be developed. In the East, railroads cover southern Queensland, most of New 

South Wales, all Victoria, and the southeastern part of South Australia. In the West, only the southwestern corner of Western Australia is well served by rail. Within these areas are the best land, most of the people, the bulk of the manufacturing industries, and all the large cities of Australia. 

Unfortunately, the railroads of the various states differ in gauge. 

They were built at different times, independently of one another. A traveller going by train from Cairns in northern Queensland to Perth in 

Western Australia must ride on track of three different gauges in the course of his journey. He has to change trains each time the gauge of the track changes. For this reason, and because of the great distances in Australia, coastal shipping and, recently, airplanes have become

important in transportation within and between the states.

Problems of Settlement 

An important fact about the railroads of Australia helps us to understand the people and their government. It is this: Australian railroads were built, and are owned and operated, by the various state governments. Private enterprise couldn't do the job. The traffic in freight and passengers with the thinly settled interior was not great enough to pay dividends. Therefore, the state governments have had to take over rail transportation. 

And what is true of the railroads is true also of much of the settlement and development of Australia: the active help of the government was absolutely necessary to bring it about. For example: ce During the present century assisted immigration" programs have been carried out by the Australian state and Commonwealth governments, in cooperation with the British government. These programs have helped to people the country by providing land and financial and other aid to carefully selected settlers.

In order to encourage closer settlement of the land, large land holdings are often broken up by state governments and leased or sold in small blocks. 

During the last sixty years large irrigation projects have been undertaken, mainly in Victoria, South Australia, and New South 

Wales. They have changed stretches of arid, almost useless land into orchards, wheat fields, and dairy farms. The greatest of these projects, capable of irrigating two million acres, gets its water supply from the Hume Dam on the 

River Murray between Victoria and New South Wales. 

Graziers, farmers, and manufacturers have looked to the government for protection against the ravages of drought, falling prices of farm products, and foreign competition. "White Australia" 

All these and other-governmental activities have been undertaken partly to help the people of Australia build up a modern, prosperous civilization in their dry and remote continent, and partly to help increase the population of the country as fast as possible. Estimates of the number of people Australia will some day be able to support run all the way from twenty million to forty million.

A great Australian statesman, W.

Labor Rules 

Especially insistent on the socalled "White Australia Policy" is the Dominion's powerful labor movement. Workers do not wish their high wage scales broken down by competition from Asiatics who are used to a much lower standard of living.

Organized labor has been a gradually increasing power in Australian life since the first unions were formed in the 1850's. It organized its own political parties, first on a state-wide, later on a national, basis. 

In the fall of 1941 the conservatives handed over responsibility for Australia's war effort to a government headed by Prime Minister John 

Curtin, leader of the Labor Party.

Labor has looked to government for help as much as, and perhaps more than, other groups in the Commonwealth. 

A series of unsuccessful strikes in the 1890's among miners, sheep shearers, seamen, and waterside workers greatly weakened the unions. As a result, labor turned to a belief in arbitration courts as a way of settling disputes with employers. 

With the support of small farmers and liberal middle-class people, labor got enough political power to put this idea into effect. One after another the state governments, and then the Commonwealth government, established courts to rule on such issues as wages, hours, and working conditions. These courts have been highly successful. Strikes have been cut down and the membership and influence of the unions have increased. Today about half of Australia's wage earners are union members. 

Social Democracy 

Turning to New Zealand, we find the same two big facts that we have found in Australia: 

1. The government has taken a most active part in helping the people to develop the country and establish a high standard of living. 

2. Labor is at the present timeand has been for some time pastthe ruling power in the country. 11

These two facts make a big difference in the kind of people our allies down under are. Something has been added to the independent self-reliance and democratic spirit of pioneer peoples. That "something" is a sense of dependence on one another, a spirit of cooperation. 

They are practised in getting together to solve their common problems, in making use of their governments to better the conditions of life of the masses of the people. 

This care by the government for the social conditions of the people is the reason why Australia and New Zealand are sometimes called social democracies.

Some of the things the New Zealanders have provided for themselves through their government are: free medical services of every kind; free milk, apples, and dental care for all school children; and a decent standard of living guaranteed for everyone.

Dominion Status 

Australia and New Zealand are not British colonies. They are not ruled by His Majesty's Government in the United Kingdom. They are ruled by their own governments, and only their own representatives in their own parliaments can make laws for them. They are connected with Britain as members of the British Commonwealth through allegiance to the same king. George VI is King of Australia and King of 

New Zealand, as well as King of England. 

King George is represented in

Australia and New Zealand by governors-general, and he appoints governors for each of the Australian states. But these governors do no more governing than the king does in England. They are figureheads only, symbols of the kinship to the 

British Commonwealth of Nations. 

Although Australia and New

Zealand are independent politically, they have strong cultural, economic, and military connections with Britain.

Many Australians and New Zealanders go to Britain to be educated.

Much of the wealth down under is owned by British businessmen. (But this situation is gradually changing. A greater proportion of Australia's industry is owned by Australians now than ever was the case before.) 

These two dominions sell most of their wool and meat exports to Britain. ¶ They depended-until the Japanese victories in the Far Eastupon British ships and bases for their defense. Now they must rely mainly on the ever-increasing flow of troops and weapons from America, and on their own production and manpower. 12

Production for Peace and War 

What, exactly, do Australia and 

New Zealand produce? (See products map on page 10.) What can their production contribute to a total war effort?

Australia, as we know, is famous as the greatest sheep raising and wool exporting country in the world. She has millions of cattle, millions of acres of wheat and other grains, sugar, some rice, some tobacco, a little cotton, dairy products, and all kinds of fruits. We think of Australia mainly as a pastoral and agricultural country. And we think of her in this way because her exports are mostly products of her farms and ranches.

Actually, however, more of Australia's workers are engaged in manufacturing than in rural occupations. Australia is not only a land of vast spaces. She is also a land of great cities. Sydney and Melbourne have each over a million inhabitants, and nearly half the population live in the capital cities of Australia's six states. Altogether, more than two-thirds of the people are town and city dwellers.

During the present century Australia has gradually built up her industries to supply a growing percentage of her needs in manufactured products. Now her factories have been converted to the production of war materials, and output is being expanded as fast as possible.

She has plenty of iron, the largest single steel mill in the British Commonwealth at Newcastle, New South Wales, and large supplies of coal near her southeastern industrial centers. In machinery and chemicals, large import items in the past, Australia is now trying to gain self-sufficiency. Oil, rubber, and aluminum must be imported.

In proportion to her population, Australia's production of war materials is large in amount and variety-and it is growing daily. 

Destroyers, corvettes, mine sweepers, merchant ships, and freighters are being built in Australian shipyards. From four to six types of planes are in mass production. Thousands of army trucks, ambulances, and gun carriers are produced every month. In 1942 tanks came into mass production. Rifles, machine guns, anti-aircraft guns, howitzers, and other artillery are being turned out, as well as the bombs, shells, and bullets required for ammunition.

And besides weapons and ammunition, Australia has gone far with her program of producing in her own territory all the many other items of equipment that are necessary for a modern army.

New Zealand, with a population of only 1,600,000, is far less industrialized than her neighbor. She 13

is producing small arms, ammunition, uniforms, and other items of equipment, but her real importance is as a source of food supplies. In spite of her small size, New Zealand exports more mutton, lamb, butter, and cheese than other any country in the world. Manpower

The fighting men of Australia and New Zealand have fought the Axis on all Britain's war fronts. In 

1942 most of them came back tralia and New Zealand strictly control the use of their limited labor supply. This is necessary to make sure that the most important war work has enough of the right kind of labor, when and where it is needed. In Australia compulsory labor battalions are at work building roads, air fields, fortifications, and war plants. Men and women and youth are working at top speed to meet the threat of Japanese invasion in the only way it can be met successfully-with total war. home-to help ward off the first Defense and Offense attack that has ever threatened their homelands.

The Australian army, including home defense forces especially trained for guerrilla warfare, numbers well over 500,000 men. Her 

Navy has over 20,000 men. There are over 60,000 men in the air force, more than Britain had at the outbreak of the war.

New Zealand, with only about a quarter of Australia's population, has mobilized armed forces, including army, navy, and air corps, of over 250,000 men. Of New Zealand's troops, 5,000 are native Maoris, the brown-skinned, intelligent natives of the South Seas who inhabited New Zealand when the white men first arrived.

The conquest of Australia by Ja pan would be a tremendous, perhaps an impossible, task. The heart of the continent, as far as population, industry, and food supplies are concerned, is the Southeast-Victoria and New South Wales. Each possible invasion route is beset by colossal difficulties.

The whole northeastern coast of the continent is protected by the 

Great Barrier Reef-a coral reef running for 1,250 miles along the coast of Queensland almost to the Australian-held territory of Papua in southern New Guinea. A fleet sailing down the coast in the narrow, mined waters between the reef and the shore would be dangerously exposed to air bombardment. And coast artillery could shell the Japanese warships. To sail around the 14

reef means a long route, a route continuously exposed to attack, over which to transport, re-inforce, and supply the large invasion army that would be needed. 

Strategists have argued that a seaborne invasion of southeastern Australia could not be successful until the enemy had first conquered New Zealand and the groups of islands lying north of New Zealand. These islands include the Free French col ony of New Caledonia. American troops landed there in the spring of 

1942 to help in its defense.

A land invasion-starting from 

Darwin on the northern coast of

Australia-would mean a march of almost 2,000 miles through jungles, deserts, and finally mountains, before the vital Southeast could be reached. 

And we must not forget that an invasion must start from bases. 

Troops, planes, ships, and supplies must be massed at certain starting points. Bases for a possible assault on Australia or New Zealand are

Japanese-occupied Timor, northeast of Darwin; New Guinea, which is not yet conquered by Japan; and 

New Britain, east of New Guinea. 

Almost every day American and 

Australian heavy bombers rain death and destruction on these Japanese outposts. The invader is not being allowed to gather his forces in peace. 

And if he should be able to establish a base on Australia itself, he would have an even harder job to hold it against land and air attacks. 

Meanwhile, General MacArthur is not only preparing to smash a 

Japanese assault. He and his staff are preparing their own offensive. 

The enemy can only guess where and when it will strike.

After Our Victory Peace is likely to find Australia and New Zealand changed in many ways. Their sources of raw materials will be more developed. Their industries will have expanded. Their men and women workers will be more efficient and better organized than ever before. 

But beyond all material changes, a change will have been made in the outlook of the people and their governments. The struggle for survival against Japan should bring home to

Australians and New Zealanders the fact that their countries are part of the Far East. Observers have pointed out that Australia and New Zealand, because of their race and culture, have been more interested in British and European affairs than in what went on in the Western Pacific. And even when they took a part in Far Eastern affairs, their outlook was influenced by their dependence on Britain. They followed 

Britain's lead rather than playing an independent role in their own part of the world. 15

The war is rapidly changing all this. Australia's safety today depends much more on cooperation with America than on cooperation with Britain. The fact that Australia is necessarily most concerned about her own defense, while Britain must be concerned with all the threatened points in her far-flung empire, has caused the island continent to play a more independent part in foreign relations than ever before.

This does not mean that Australia will cease to be part of the British 

Commonwealth of Nations. Nor does it mean that she feels able to face Japan without outside aid. It does mean, however, that she realizes that responsibility for her safety must lie in Australia, not in 

London. And she also realizes that she must look to the United States for most of her outside help.

Writing in the Melbourne Herald in December, 1941, Prime Minister 

John Curtin declared that Australia could not regard the war in the Pacific as a side issue of the war in 

Europe. The United States and Aus tralia, he went on, should have the fullest say in the direction of the fighting plan in the South Pacific theater of war.`

Prime Minister Curtin's wish has come true. The Pacific War Council, in charge of the United Nations' grand strategy in that section of the world, has been moved from London to Washington.

Increased United States-Austral ian collaboration is a natural development which the war has only speeded up. Both are Pacific nations, and each has an equal interest in seeing to it that fascist aggression is ended forever in the Pacific area. 

Until that is done, neither we nor the people down under can draw an easy breath. 

The common interest which links

America and Australia in war has been well expressed by the Australian Prime Minister: "The battle for 

America," Mr. Curtin said, "may well be won or lost by the way the battle for Australia goes. Americans 

Ican and will save America if Australia is saved." 

And the common interest which links America and Australia in the future time of peace is their desire for peace and security throughout the Pacific area. That peace and security can only be attained through collaboration with one another and with a free India, a free China, a free Russia-and a free Japan. 16
