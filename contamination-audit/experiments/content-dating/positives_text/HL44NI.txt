WORK RELEASE-STUDY RELEASE PROGRAM 1970 and the First Five Years I. Introduction 

Work release is one facet of a total correctional program which permits selected inmates to hold jobs in the community while confined in a correctional facility during non-working hours. It is a step between full-time confinement and regular parole, providing a trial period for evaluating the man's ability to maintain employment, assume financial obligations and function on a part-time basis in the community. The program offers the participant the opportunity to demonstrate his ability, to resist temptations, relate with authority and demonstrate his capacity for self-discipline. 

The Work Release Law was enacted in October 1965; approximately 40 inmates. from the Wisconsin State Reformatory were immediately employed on an emergency basis to help harvest the apple crop in Door County. However, the first meaningful placement more closely related to the inmates' vocational plans occurred six months later in the Wisconsin Correctional Camp System on April 11, 1966. This was followed by a placement of ten on April 18, 1966 from the Wisconsin 

Correctional Institution. 

Experience soon indicated the need for changes in the law and a less rigid approach to selection criteria. The law initially permitted the State Department of Health and Social Services (DHSS) to grant work release privileges to any person incarcerated within the state prisons who had not been sentenced to a life term, providing the sentencing court had been notified and did not object within ten days of receipt of notice.

The law was amended in August 1966 to permit county boards to contract with the DHSS for the quartering in the county jail of inmates under the work release law. The amendment also authorized the DHSS to arrange and contract for other facilities for inmates employed in the area. 

This made it possible to combine parole planning with work release in the community. The parole agent becomes the work release and community coordinator, involving the man's family, community agencies and the employer in a total pre-release and work release program.

The first jail placement was for eight months with housing in the Rusk County Jail. The placement, for work in an auto-body shop, began in April 1967. 

The man kept this employment when he was released on parole. Although he eventually changed employment he has not been a client of the Division of 

Corrections since his discharge from supervision. The second jail placement (in 1968) used the Dane County Jail for housing and permitted work as a computer programmer with the State Department of Administration. This placement permitted utilization of skills acquired in computer programming courses at the Wisconsin State Reformatory. He continues as a valuable and respected civil service employee. 

By the end of 1970, six jails had been utilized for 12 placements. 

A further amendment in 1967 authorized the DHSS to approve work release placements in universities, colleges, technical, vocational or trade schools or in sheltered workshops and training programs designed to improve the skills and ability of the inmate. This amendment led to the beginning of the study release portion of the work release program and the placement of three clients from the Wisconsin Correctional Institution through the cooperation of the 

Fond du Lac Vocational, Technical and Adult Education District at the Beaver Dam

Campus. All three successfully completed the program. Since that time study release placements rapidly expanded to include both full and part-time placement opportunities leading to the attainment of the High School Equivalency diploma, attendance at the University Extension, state universities and private colleges. 

Every effort is made to permit clients to make a logical progression to a meaningful study release assignment in keeping with individual academic or training goals and his readiness for the placement as demonstrated by achievement in education or training programs made available to him in the institutions. 

The 1967 amendment also provided that an inmate with work release privileges placed in facilities outside a state prison shall be liable for the cost of his room, board, clothing, and other necessary expenses incident to his employment or placement unless other means of payment are provided by the DHSS. 

The law was again changed in November 1969 to permit the DHSS to place persons serving life sentences after they attain parole eligibility. As a result of this amendment, during 1970 six persons serving life sentences were placed. 

One was removed from the program for inability to meet work standards demanded by the

· 3 · -M employer. The remaining five successfully completed their placement. An amendment to eliminate the necessity for those serving life sentences to serve until parole eligibility has been presented for legislative consideration. This would permit earlier placement consideration for a group of offenders whose rehabilitative needs might best be met by longer program participation. 

The law was further amended to eliminate the need to contact the sentencing court for placement approval. This avoided delays in approving and placing persons on the program and the time involved in correspondence and related follow-up activities.

The program owes its progress to enlightened legislation and the support and direction of the Health and Social Services Board, the Department Secretary and the Division Administrator. The cooperation of the wardens and superintendents and the various institution work release coordinators has contributed in no small measure in enabling the program to assist in meeting the rehabilitative needs of an expanding number of clients. At the present time the Division Work Release 

Supervisor is the only budgeted position devoting full time to the program. 

Recognition must be given to the diligence of the various institution personnel who, in addition to their regularly assigned duties, participate in the selection process, placement, administrative details, employment search and public relations. 

The response by many county sheriffs has also made a positive impact. The progress and success of the program also reflects the ability of law offenders to accept additional responsibilities. Their ability to do so has contributed to the continual expansion of the program and has permitted a departure from rigid selection criteria.

In 1966 the Division of Vocational Rehabilitation expanded its services to include the public offender. Through their cooperation obstacles and problems such as additional transportation, initial work or dress clothing, initial tool needs, and interim financing made it possible to expand employment placements. Arrangements to underwrite the costs of school supplies, books, tuition and intensive client evaluation by the Division of Vocational Rehabilitation expanded educational and training opportunities commensurate with individualized client aspirations, abilities and rehabilitative needs.

- 4The work release program has progressed in spite of limited finances, adverse economic conditions, and severe unemployment problems. The location of many correctional facilities in smaller communities and rural areas has posed additional problems with respect to transportation and limited employment opportunities. 

Program Future 

Research is being formulated to explore various facets of the program on a more intensive basis. While the work and study release program is not considered to be a rehabilitative panacea, it has added a new dimension to the treatment process. Certainly, there are individual cases which document the relationship of work or study release to successful community adjustment. 

Selection criteria will continue to emphasize placement of the individual on work or study release at the most propitious time commensurate with his progress and adjustment, treatment needs and security requirements. Experience has adequately demonstrated that an individual's positive progress through educational treatment and training programs can be a valuable predictive factor for successful. work and study release placement. Increasing the number of participants in the program will, however, necessitate less rigid selection criteria and undoubtedly will result in an increasing number of removals from the program for inability to respond positively to the program. 

The possibility of increases in the number of program participants will be further enhanced with the opening of a work release center in the Milwaukee Metropolitan Area. A new dormitory at Winnebago Farm will permit expansion of education and work placements in Oshkosh and the Fox River Valley. Decisions regarding further housing and program arrangements could lead to expansion of the program in other geographical areas. The opportunity to have full-time work release coordinators at selected facilities would strengthen the program. 

Every effort will be made to expand the use of county jails to house selected persons where they will participate in a combined work and pre-release program in communities in which they will ultimately be paroled. This will permit parole

agents, the sheriffs, employers and community resources to combine efforts in meeting the clients' rehabilitative needs. 

A furlough privilege permitting qualified inmates to leave correctional institutions on a temporary basis to seek employment would enhance the work release program and lead to additional placements on a selected basis with housing in county jails, half-way houses, or in community work release centers. 

The Division of Corrections will consider housing work releasees in selected half-way houses whenever such arrangement is deemed to be appropriate for the individual and controls are consistent with legal and Division directives.

III. 

1970 Work Release-Study Release Program Characteristics of the Work Releasee 

The ages of the work releasees are presented in Appendix Table 1. Slightly more than 4 out of 10 were between 21 and 29 years of age with the greatest number being 24 to 26. The median age of the work releasees was approximately 27.

Race, sex, and marital status of the work releasees appears in Appendix Table 2. Males represent 95 percent of the work release population. Seventyone percent of the work releasees were white. Almost 4 out of every 10 were single and about 3 out of every 10 were married. 

Appendix Table 3 shows the type of offense and length of sentence of the 1970 work releasees. Four out of every 7 offenses were against property and fewer than 2 out of 7 were against persons. One out of every 4 sentences was for burglary. The average (median) length of sentence was four years. 

Information for first-admitted work releasees only is presented in Appendix Tables 4 through 7. Over half (57.3 percent) of the first admissions had previous felony convictions. About 7 out of 10 had previous penal experiences. However, fewer than 3 out of 10 had previous juvenile institution experience. Fewer than 1 in 5 (18.6 percent) of the first admissions had completed high school, and the average (median) grade completed was 10th grade. 

About 4 out of 10 had completed no more than 8th grade. 

1970 Work Release Experience-Job Placement 

Total financial data appears in Appendix Table 8. Work releasees in 1970 earned an average (mean) of $1,031.43. At the end of the program, after disbursements, each work releasee had an average balance of $473.72. About two-thirds of all the work placements are supervised by the Wisconsin Correctional Camp System, as shown in Appendix Table 9. Nearly all of the adult correctional facilities supervise and house work releasees. In areas where state facilities do not exist, attempts are made to utilize county jails for housing releasees.

Appendix Table 10 presents the reason for termination and the number of days worked. In 1970 only 12 percent of the terminations could be considered in-program failures. Of those failures over one-half occurred within the first 30 days of the placement. The average (median) length of placement was about three months.

Appendix Tables 11 and 12 relate to the relevancy of the placement in terms of its relation to prior work skills and the use of skills learned while incarcerated. Almost 1 of every 4 placements made use, at least partially, of skills learned while incarcerated. Over half (54 percent) of the placements were in jobs at least partially related to prior work skills. 

Only 11 percent continued their work release placements upon reaching parole. 

Appendix Table 13 shows that for those continuing on parole, 83 percent of the placements were related to prior work skills of the inmate. The trend seems to be that the more related the placement is to the inmate's prior work skills, the more likely that he will continue the job on parole. 

The employer's reaction to the work releasee is reported in Appendix Table 

14. About 70 percent of all employers indicated that they would be willing to rehire the inmate when he was released on parole.

LIFT-An Experimental Program 

In 1970 an experimental program called LIFT (Learning Incentives for Training) was conceptualized combining the Walworth pre-release program with work release. The pre-release program is a four week classroom-type course designed to prepare inmates for the transition back into his home community. 

The LIFT program enabled the inmate to be placed on work release or in training through the efforts of Manpower Development and Training Act or the Division of Vocational Rehabilitation during the first two weeks of pre-release, after which he was released and subsequently completed the second two weeks of the pre-release program on parole status while continuing his work release job or training in the community. The LIFT program combined the efforts of the Wisconsin State Employment Service, Division of Vocational Rehabilitation and the Division of Corrections. It was designed to (1) discover employment difficulties, (2) match interests, aptitudes, and abilities with requirements of various jobs, and (3) set realistic goals and make detailed plans to achieve those goals. Only 16 men had an opportunity to benefit from the program before funding was discontinued, 14 of whom completed the program successfully. is believed, however, that some of the concepts gleaned from LIFT will be incorporated in future programs at work release centers in metropolitan areas.
