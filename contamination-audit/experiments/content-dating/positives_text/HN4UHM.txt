The Continued Study Unit 

This is a new educational tool. It represents the cement that binds together the diverse elements or topics which together comprise the Social Science fabric of our educational program. 

These Units must not be thought of as isolated topics which have no connection with the subject matter of textbooks. They do, however, represent a correlated, integrated, and cumulative program. They represent a study of the whole social science field which has resulted for the first time in a solution of the problems involved. 

No textbook can possibly give all that is known about any character, any event, or situation, and yet much more information is needed on every such topic than the textbook affords. In place of printing many paper-bound booklets on various subjects, this program has resulted in a survey of the factual material needed to supplement the textbooks in history, geography, civics and government, and allied topics. Over five hundred topics have been selected as an objective- not that they may all be published in the immediate future. 

Each of the topics selected will be tied into subject material in the textbooks. In this way, additional material on a subject may be added from time to time. A fuller knowledge of the subject under discussion in the textbook is thus made possible. Herein is offered not only factual material correlated with the textbooks, but supplementary reading that is automatically directed to a construcive end. The pupil may be daily exposed to what is really research material, without the labor of digging it out of reference books written for adults. 

Another advantage of the Continued Study Unit idea is the possibility of keeping textbooks up-to-date, as well as the opportunity of securing Units on new and modern problems as they arise. In the past, the teacher has been forced to give assigned readings from books which are beyond the mental age of the child. CHAPTER ONE A Strange Stream 

Rivers and lakes were the roads of the early settlers. In some instances, mountains stood in the way of western expansion and it was easier to follow a river around them, even if it meant a longer journey, than it was to blaze a trail through the unknown mountains.

Rivers and lakes have made possible some of our larger cities as they furnished natural transportation routes. It is true that the coming of the railroads lessened the commercial value of the rivers as means of transportation, but some of them, the Ohio, the Mississippi, and the Missouri, made possible early explorations and discoveries. 

These rivers are still carrying the products of the country and they are growing in importance every year.

The study of our history cannot well be separated from the story of our rivers, lakes and canals. 

They are not only linked with our history but their geography has given much to our commerce and economic development.

We also must remember that large rivers were an obstacle to the early settlers; as much so, perhaps, as some of the mountains, for it was not easy to cross these swiftrunning streams. Not until bridges began to appear did travel across the rivers become easy. It was the railroad that alone warranted the building of the great bridges necessary to span these wide streams.

Some of these bridges with their approaches are from one to three miles in length, and each represents an investment of at least a million dollars or more. In this way man outwits nature. Although the early western settlers had no bridges to cross the Mississippi and the Missouri rivers, it was the bridges and the railroads that finally made possible the great far western expansion.

Jolliet, the trader, and Father Marquette, the Jesuit priest, were exploring together. At the spot where 

Alton, Illinois, now stands they saw, high up on a rocky cliff, some pictures of dragons, devils, or "thunder-birds," painted there by the Indians. Those images, once blasted away by quarrymen, have been restored by Boy Scouts to their former terrifying brightness.

For awhile, the brown waves tossed the pair of little craft about like chips, but the Frenchmen, calling upon all their skill, managed to pass that dangerous spot in safety.

It was the water of the great Missouri which rushed to meet the upper Mississippi. 

If the explorers could have known then what we have learned since, they would have hailed the Missouri as the longest river in the United States, extending nearly three thousand miles between its mouth near St. Louis and its farthest source in the mountains of the West. They would have called it a river of wonderful changes-able in the high water of spring and early summer to float fair-sized steamboats for twenty-three hundred miles, yet during the latter part of the open season wandering in shallow channels among a wilderness of sandy islands. They would have seen it as a mountain stream for hundreds and hundreds of miles, yet flowing through the Great Plains during most of its long course. They would have greeted it as the only stream that offered navigation up to the base of the Rockies. 

If you will look at your map you will see the James River indicated. This was the original bed of the Missouri river until diverted by the glaciers when the river cut its present channel. Most of the year the greater part of the socalled James River is dry, or little more than a creek.

The Character of the River: The 

Missouri has often been called the 

Big Muddy. Many writers claim that the Missouri and the Mississippi should be considered as one stream; but from many points of view, especially that of history, they are different channels. True, they are both very muddy. Senator 

Thomas Hart Benton, who lived near its mouth, is credited with saying, "The Missouri is a little too thick to swim in, and not quite thick enough to walk on." A glass of Missouri water, left to stand for five minutes, will deposit a layer of mud an eighth of an inch thick. 

In the prairies of the Dakotas, Nebraska, and Kansas, the river runs through soil that is loose and sandy, dissolving like sugar when it falls into the water. Quicksand makes the river very dangerous for those who might fall into its waters. 

The Missouri is forever cutting into its banks, changing its channel forty or fifty yards within a week, swinging from side to side of a valley that is often five miles wide, "constant only in its inconstancy."

Yes, the Missouri and the Mississippi were alike in ability to change their channels, but from the viewpoint of an American of the year 1800, the Missouri had a character all its own among Western streams. The Ohio, late as it was in being developed, guarded as it was by Indians, offered a good waterway down which the new settler could easily float to a new home. He was setting his face toward French and Spanish settlements which had been there for some time. All he had to do was to drift, and in the end, if he kept on drifting, he would reach a civilized community. 

On the other hand, the explorer of the Missouri had to fight the river in going farther West, and every mile he traveled took him away from places of help and supply. No one knew just where the Missouri took its rise. The 

French had spread far along the river, so had also a very few English from Canada, but they had not told the Americans much about it. The country through which the 

Missouri ran was different from that of the East. It was a land so strange, so wild, so new to all civilized experience, that to plunge into it seemed unthinkable for the 

American of President Washing ton's day. He would have said, "The Mississippi is our border; the Missouri is a river of the desert and the wilderness, and what have we to do with it?" Soon, however, the Americans were to have much to do with the Missouri.

CHAPTER TWO Early French Explorers 

Kaskaskians Profit by the Mis- of the settlers in this little island souri: Between twenty-five and thirty years after Jolliet and Marquette had passed that way, two priests came to settle at the village of the Kaskaskia (Kǎs-kǎs'kē-ǎ Indians in Illinois not far from the mouth of the Missouri. Father 

Marquette, though long since passed away, had interested himself in these natives, and the good priests were carrying on his faithful work. French trappers and hunters, some of whom had served 

Henri de Tonty, "Iron Hand," as told in The Mighty Mississippi, came to build their huts at the mission station. Thus the town of 

Kaskaskia grew up. Other French settled at Cahokia (Ka-ho'-ki-ă), on the Mississippi shore north of Kaskaskia.

Somehow, Kaskaskia always remained the larger of the two settlements. It stood along a little river surrounded by wide stretches of rich land. Before long, French families came from Detroit to use the land for farming and the raising of farm animals. A couple of small villages grew up in the same fertile plain or "bottom" between Kaskaskia and Cahokia. After awhile, a strong fort, Fort Chartres (shart) was built to watch over the safety of French homes and French life. 

Naturally, while the French hunters and trappers paddled their canoes and their dugouts up all the streams, great and small, in that region, they did not neglect the Missouri, which they found rich in fur. They tried to make friends. with the Indians of the Missouri, and sometimes settled among them. 

The skins of buffalo, beaver, deer, and bear which they brought back were bought by merchants or traders in Kaskaskia, and from the stores of these men the hunters outfitted for new expeditions. Long before the Ohio was well known to the whites, a large part of the lower Missouri was explored by the French.

De Bourgmont's Cavalcade: 

One of the earliest Frenchmen to make his home on the Missouri was Etienne (eh-te-ĕn), or Stephen, de Bourgmont (Bōōrzh'mon), who had been a trusted officer at Detroit. Unfortunately, he provoked a war with the Indians, was severely reproved, and in a fury deserted the service, taking a few of his men with him. To avoid being captured and punished he traveled south and took refuge with the

When time, as de Bourgmont thought, had brought forgetfulness or forgiveness, he appeared one day at New Orleans, where Governor de Bienville received him kindly and made use of his knowledge.

Bourgmont, with a fort to build along the Missouri. He did build it in 1723, three hundred miles up the stream, and called it Fort Orleans. So well did he please the

Governor that two years later he was allowed to visit France, taking with him not only his second in command, Sergeant Dubois, but also the daughter of an Indian chief and eight warriors. During the voyage Dubois paid much attention to the "princess" and they became engaged to be married. 

When the party arrived, clever de Bourgmont presented his respects to the royal court, and offered to stage a show. As there was nothing that could win the hearts of these light-hearted lords and ladies like some new play or sport, his proposal made a great hit. In one of the royal hunting parks de

Bourgmont mounted his eight Indians on good horses; they dashed through the forest, demonstrating on the herds of harmless deer how their arrows brought down the buffalo. Everyone was delighted with this novel spectacle. 

A Second Pocahontas: But that was not all. There remained the "princess," who must have been rather attractive. She made even more sensation in Paris than little Pocahontas had produced in London more than a century before, as in this case her wedding was to take place in Europe. She was loaded with gifts, she was presented to the king, she was invited everywhere. Sergeant Dubois was promoted to the rank of captain, and the wedding took place with great state in the cathedral of Notre Dame, with the whole court present. 

Then the happy pair sailed back to America, where Dubois took charge of both Fort Chartres and 

Fort Orleans. De Bourgmont had "rung the bell." For his services as soldier and entertainer he was 

Imade a nobleman. He married a rich widow and passed the rest of his life in fashionable surroundings, remote from any wilderness. 

Verendrye and His Sons: Only a few years later, explorers came to the Missouri from an entirely different direction. Pierre de la Verendrye (Vāy-ron-drē′), an important man in the Canadian fur trade, had one of his stations on the big "Lake of the Woods," between 

Minnesota and Canada. Having heard of the furs to be had on the 

Missouri, he decided to find that stream in its upper course. With

one of his sons he rode westward across the Great Plains until he came to the desired river near its extreme northern bend, not far below the mouth of the Yellowstone.

Here stood the villages of the 

Mandans far to the northwest of the present village of Mandan. They were sufficiently different from other tribes to make certain explorers think there was white blood in them. They did not wander about, but lived in big round huts framed with wood and covered with thatch or clay, and were friendly, pleasant people. Moreover, they were much lighter in color than the average redman, and some among them had hair that was almost blonde. Today a "national monument" preserves the spot where the Frenchmen discovered the Mandans and the Missouri.

At another time, two of the Verendrye sons explored from the 

Mandan villages up the Yellowstone River and its branches, and went so far that they could see the outline of mountains against the sky. When they returned to the 

Missouri, far below the Mandan villages, the explorers buried a lead plate like those which Celoron de 

Blainville, as told in The Ohio 

River, would leave along the Allegheny and the Ohio six years later. 

One hundred and seventy years afterward, in 1913, a school girl, walking on a river-bluff near Pierre, South Dakota, saw a corner of this very plate projecting from the ground, and uncovered the relic.

CHAPTER THREE 

Pierre Laclede and Daniel Boone

Laclede Hears Bad News: At the end of the struggle between England and France in America, when in 1763 France had to give up her great province of Louisiana, England took the part east of the Mississippi, and Spain was given the land on the western side of the "Father of Waters." Just before the end of the war the French government granted to the New Orleans firm of Maxent, Laclede and Company the sole right to trade with "the savages of the Missouri."

While the elder partner stayed comfortably at home, it fell to the lot of Pierre Laclede, the younger partner, to trade with the Indians and send the furs to New Orleans. 

With his wife, five children, and a crew of thirty men, Laclede finally set off in a big barge for the up-river trip as the summer heat hung heavily over New Orleans.

Though he did not know it, France had signed away Louisiana six months before. Not a word of the great news had yet reached the 

Gulf coast. The barge cheerfully pursued its slow progress against the current, until, at the end of three months, it reached Fort

Chartres. 

Here the serious news met Laclede. Although the French flag still waved there, that was only because hostile tribes, stirred up by 

Pontiac, prevented English troops from coming so far west. Kaskaskia and its traders would be under British control before long. Laclede was not the sort of man to turn back, but he would not accept the 

English as his rulers. He would just find a place out of their reach. 

The Founding of St. Louis: For tunately for Laclede's purpose, he stood at the boundary line. St. 

Ange (Anzh) de Bellerive (bellreevé), who commanded Fort Chartres, let him store his goods safely in the fort, and in the December weather, Laclede took the barge for a scouting trip along the Mississippi He found a spot on the Spanish side, eighteen miles below the 

Missouri's mouth, where the land rose high in shelves or terraces from the water, so that floods would never reach the upper levels. By making a road across the base of the triangle between the two rivers, the people on this spot could reach the Missouri without having to go all the way around and against the current of both streams. Laclede decided that this was just the right place for his trading-post, especially as it was almost across the river from Cahokia. 

In February, as soon as the ice in the river broke up and it was (9)

safe to cross, Laclede sent his stepson, Auguste Chouteau (shoo-toe), not yet fifteen years old, to direct the man in cutting down big trees and building log cabins for the settlement. This was a big responsibility for the lad, but he proved equal to it. Meanwhile Laclede traveled about, urging the French to join with him and preserve their freedom. 

By April everything was ready, and Laclede brought over his family and goods. He named the settlement "St. Louis," after a celebrated French king who took part in the Crusades during the Middle 

Ages. The trader had thought that he still would be on French land, but when he found out that he was wrong, he preferred to be in territory which was Spanish rather than 

English. About a hundred French families thought so too, and came. to join him. In less than a year 

St. Louis could boast of five hundred inhabitants. The salt licks and the lead mines had already attracted some French to cross the river. There was a settlement of 

St. Geneviève (jen-e-vee-ev) on the 

Mississippi and one at St. Charles on the Missouri.

For fifteen years, Pierre Laclede was the leading citizen of St. Louis.

He developed the fur-trade so that no rival did so much business as he. 

Every year a host of Indians came to camp along the river-front in order to trade with his firm. Through him St. Louis became the great fur town of the West, and although so many years have gone by, and so many changes have taken place, it still remains the fur center of the

Trading Along the River: La clede was not a good business man. 

He was too trusting, and so his bad debts piled up. When the "Father of St. Louis" died, only a month before George Rogers Clark came to Kaskasia, Auguste Chouteau became manager. Auguste had just as big a heart as his stepfather, but he did not let himself be cheated. Soon he put affairs on a profitable footing. He sent his young half-brother Pierre to trade with the Osages up the Missouri, and matters went so well that the Chouteaus were granted the sole right of trading with that powerful tribe who lived so near. 

Rival traders had to go farther up-stream in order to reach the other tribes, and they did so. They passed the mouth of the Platte, they discovered the Kansas or Kaw

River, and still they kept on. Before many years there were French trappers making their homes among the Indians of the Missouri. They learned the Indian speech, followed 

Indian ways, and married Indian girls. These were "free" or independent trappers, but a Spanish company, "The Commercial Company for the Discovery of the Nations of the Upper Missouri"-the Spaniards liked high-sounding titles -was organized in the year when 

Washington's second term began, 1793. It employed men of education to explore and to draw maps, but its three expeditions were robbed and threatened by the Indians, and brought back few furs. 

Still, all this activity made the river fairly well known to the Spaniards. 

The frontiersmen of Kentucky and Tennessee regarded the Mississippi as a God-given road of trade to float their produce to market. Every once in awhile the

Spaniards tried to stop the American flatboats from coming down to 

New Orleans, or else they taxed theem highly. Because the government at Philadelphia was weak and far away, the backwoods dwellers often felt rather neglected. Many of them crossed to Missouri, where they could trade freely down the river, if they became Spanish citizens.

Boone Moves to Spanish Land: 

Spanish governors were glad to see the "brave Americans" come, for they wished to see Upper Louisiana grow. Among a group of these adventurers was Daniel Morgan 

Boone, son of famous Daniel Boone.

Four families formed the group in which he came. They were granted land along Femme Osage Creek, which flows into the Missouri about twenty-five miles above St. Charles, Missouri. Three years later, Daniel Boone himself, with his wife Rebecca and his son Nathan, moved from the Kanawha river, where he

had been living, to leave the United 

States and join his boy. 

Wherever Boone's flatboats stopped along the Ohio, crowds of people came to see this well-known man. At Cincinnati some one questioned, "Daniel, at your time of life, when you're nearly sixty-five, why do you pull up stakes and go to Missouri?" "Why," "answered the old hunter, "it's getting too crowded back where I came from. I'm looking for elbow-room!"

There was plenty of elbow-room on the Missouri, and Boone received nearly nine hundred acres of land adjoining the tract which his son Daniel had "homesteaded."

Here in the new region he found peace and the elbow-room he craved. The settlers were mostly 

French, with just enough Americans to keep Boone from growing homesick for the United States.

They were easy-going people, living comfortably on the products of their flocks and herds and did little farming. "Why be rich when you can be happy?" they said.

The Syndic of Femme Osage: 

As Boone could read and write, which few of his French neighbors could do, and as he was so highly thought of among the Americans, the Spanish governor soon appointed him "syndic" or magistrate of his district. The syndic combined in one person a number of offices and was the only representative of the government among the settlers. 

Daniel Boone had to arrest criminals if there were any, to adjust disputes, to lead the military force if there should be fighting; but he never had to think about collecting taxes, for the happy settlers were not taxed.

Boone knew no law except the law of common sense, but that worked very well. When a rascal was brought before him, Syndic Boone sometimes tried to cure him by ordering a dozen lashes on the bare back. When neither side seemed to have a good reason for complaint, Boone divided the costs between the two parties. If his rheumatism was bad, or his duties interfered with his hunting, Boone thought he would like to resign, but neither his neighbors nor the Governor would agree to that; so he kept on settling cases and showing American newcomers the best spots in which to make their homes.

Once More on American Soil: 

After four years of "just and impartial" service, Boone's office came to an end; not because the Governor wished it, but because the 

United States crossed the Mississippi. Louisiana became an American purchase and the flag of Spain gave place to the Stars and Stripes in 1804. Governor Delassus had told

CHAPTER FOUR The Two Leaders 

Jefferson's Young Explorers: For he was captain in the regular army years and years Thomas Jefferson, who was himself the son of a frontier settler, had been anxious to know more about the Great West beyond the Mississippi. He had hoped to induce George Rogers 

Clark to do some exploring, and we know that would have been a good choice; he had made two other attempts to send explorers who should bring back good accounts, but had not succeeded. At last, when he became President, Jefferson persuaded Congress to furnish money for a small expedition which should pass through 

Spanish territory, cross the Rocky

Mountains, and find a land route to the land along the Columbia River, which we already claimed under the name of the Oregon

Country. 

At that time there were no steamboats, no railroads, and the only path that seemed possible to follow was the Missouri River. "This is the only line of easy communication across the continent," said the President. As head of the party Jefferson chose his own private secretary, Meriwether Lewis, a young Virginian from the President's home county. Tall and handsome, Meriwether had always loved the outdoors. At twenty-three under Anthony Wayne.¹ He knew something about dealing with Indians; he knew how to command men. "Of courage undaunted; possessing a firmness and perseverance of purpose which nothing but impossibilities could ever divert from its direction"-that was what Jefferson thought about Meriwether at the age of twenty-nine. 

As his aid and companion on the adventure, Meriwether chose his lifelong friend, red-headed "Billy" 

Clark, younger brother of George 

Rogers Clark. Billy and Meriwether had passed together through the battle of Fallen Timbers; in all their army comradeship and on the strenuous journey before them there never came a shadow of quarrel or even of disagreement between them. "One for both and both for one" seemed to be their motto. Though Billy, through some mistake of Congress, held only the rank of lieutenant, Meriwether always called him Captain, and the men of the party thought he was one indeed.

Fighting the River: It was not easy to fight the rushing river.

Every mile or two there was a jam of timber, trees that were always falling into the water as the current cut the shores. The boatmen called such a jam an embarras, an obstacle, hindrance, or tangle. A way around these usually revealed itself, but through the narrow gap that was left, the stream darted with double force, and the struggle to get through was a hard one. 

Where the gap was wide enough, the twenty oars of the craft could be used; if it was too narrow for that, grappling-hooks were thrown out and the men pulled the boat up-stream foot by foot while the "setting-poles" on the other side did their part by pushing. Sometimes, when willows and cottonwood poplars thickly fringed the bank, the crew grasped these and slowly pulled the barge along. It was slow, heart-breaking labor. 

Lisa was everywhere. He would throw the steering-oar to someone else in order to rush to the bow and help with the grappling-irons; then he would jump to the side and cast his strength upon a settingpole. His fierce energy, his shouts of encouragement, his cheerful songs, lifted and drove the men. 

Fortunately, above nearly every embarras spread a long still stretch of water, and here the sails, when the breeze favored, gave the tired crew a chance to sprawl about and rest.

Anxious as Lisa was to get forward, hard and keen as he might be in business, one thing showed that the trader had a big heart. The boat came to a poor ox, strayed far away from home and caught in a quicksand. There he stood, fixed as if in a mold, and there he had evidently been for days, while from the neighboring trees the buzzards,

CHAPTER TEN The Bull-Boat Voyage Boats of Skin: The buffalo, which was of such wonderful use to the Indians in so many ways, even furnished them means of navigation. Stretched over a frame of willow poles was a sheet of rawhide made by sewing together stout pieces from the skins of buffalo bulls. Sinews, the "tendons" or cords from the ends of muscles, were used as thread. This rawhide, with the hair all removed, and soaked in water, was tied firmly to the frame of the boat. 

As it dried it shrank until it was as tight as a drum-head. Then the seams where the skins were sewed received a thick coat of buffalo-tallow and ashes, which made the boat water-proof.

For crossing small streams or for use near an Indian camp, these "bull-boats" often took the shape of a hemisphere or round tub. Of course, they could be very easily upset, but the squaws skillfully moved them about by the use of their hands, small poles, or short paddles. Longer voyages demanded a different style of craft, one that resembled a canoe, and many of the bull-boats reached a length of thirty feet. A boat of that size would be almost half as wide as it was long, or about twelve feet, and looked quite clumsy. 

Such a boat might carry three tons of cargo in furs and men, and yet draw much less than two feet of water. Where the rivers, such as the Platte, the Niobrara, and the Cheyenne, were very shallow, though wide, the bull-boats came into play for transporting the season's catch of fur pelts down to the Missouri. As a rule, the bullboats did not try to struggle with the swift current and strong winds of the "Big Muddy." When need arose, nevertheless, these chubby constructions even ventured out on the great stream; but in so doing they took a big chance.

Of course it continued to grow, until it now stands among the ten largest cities of the United States, and is the largest community between Chicago and the Pacific Coast. Its central situation, its possibilities of transport both by water and by land, make it a vast hive of industry, with a tremendous amount of varied manufactures, which is better than having only certain specialties. Still, however, the city keeps the result of the trade which made it in the beginning the fur business. St. Louis is still the fur center of the country. From Alaska, from the Rockies, from Louisiana, which is now the most important fur producing State, come the pelts which in order to be of final value must go through the skillful hands of St. Louis workmen and be disposed of through St. Louis' business relations. It is an interesting survival from the old days. 

By comparison, the Missouri is now a deserted river. Railroads have taken the freight traffic, as is the story with our other rivers. On the levee, that paved inclined slope which forms the St. Louis waterfront, an excursion steamer and a showboat are more prominent than 

Missouri freightcraft.

The main amount of freight now is carried by Government-owned barges, directed by the Federal Waterways Corporation, and Uncle

Sam pays for any loss in the operation. These barges run only as far as from St. Louis to Kansas City. 

Above the latter point there is really no freight and not a good channel, although the dam at Fort Peck is supposed to keep a constant flow of water in the river. The total freight on the Missouri amounts to less than one-seventh of the amount which the United States engineers estimated it would have today. Interesting as is its history, numerous as are its cities, we must recognize that as a waterway the Missouri has lost much of its glory.
