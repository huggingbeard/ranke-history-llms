Dept Two decades ago a certain Dr. Collins looked at literature. 

He wrote a book so named, and, in a minor way, it became a bestseller. This so gratified the good Doctor that he straightway wrote others The Doctor looks at Marriage and Medicine, The Doctor looks at Love and Life, The Doctor looks at Life and Death, and at Doctors. 

His look at objects outside his profession was not received as well by the public as was his first effort upon the subject with which he was most familiar.

It might be said that my remarks today could well be directed to a look at the administration of justice, a subject more familiar to me than antitrust law, and/or the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice. But I don't think you gentlemen present are especially interested in my views on that subject, although there has been some slight indication that more than one or two of you. present are interested in what I see when I take a Judge's look at "Antitrust." 

Before I specifically get into my own observations on that vexing subject, I think we should first set the stage, and consider the cast of characters.

As a supporting member of the cast, one of the few things I ascertained before agreeing to take up my part was whether or not those two leaders meant what they said. I can assure you they do. 

If anyone had told me six months ago that I would leave the comparative peace and quiet of the bench a life among my friends and neighbors of fifty years; a lifetime job with substantial renot to mention the California climate and smog tirement provisions for the push and pressure of an admitted "hot seat" - a job where no matter what decisions were made they would, to some people, be "wrong" a job working under a man I had never met, on legal problems that had not often been considered by me (either as a lawyer or a Judge), in a nation's capital with its lovely summer humidity, at a substantially lesser salary I think I would have gladly signed my own commitment papers for a psychopathic examination. And sometimes, I confess, at the end of a twelve-hour working day, I find myself wondering if such an examination is still not in order. My lack of specialized knowledge, however, has enabled me, I believe, to approach the problems of my Division in a more objective manner, free from any preconceived notions. 

I have been on the job less than four months. You gentlemen would be the first to agree that that is not a long enough association with any legal subject, and particularly one as complex as antitrust law, to draw either any profoundly original conclusions or to come to any solid convictions.

During these four months I have read a great deal more than I have ever read in my life in any similar period. I can say without exaggeration much help has been offered me! 

Friends whom I have not seen since college days have not been shy in seeing to it that no time was lost in my having the opportunity to read the "right" literature. 

And while this attention makes one feel rather like the man who has just won the jackpot on the television show, or the Irish Sweepstakes winner, it makes the mail deliveries interesting and gives the mail a nostalgic touch.

I have talked to anyone who wanted to talk with me. I have visited each of our branch offices, and talked individually with each of our lawyers in those branches, and gone over each case pending in those area offices. This has

ments, at many levels of speaking publicly at Los Angeles, California, at Columbia, Missouri, and at Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; and privately at four schools of law. In all these contacts, both with books and people, I have endeavored to "soak up" as much information, knowledge, and know-how as is possible in such a limited time. 

From my studies and contacts I have come to some tentative beliefs.

First: That there exists almost universal recognition among the American people that that "charter of freedom," known as the Sherman Act, and, to a lesser or greater extent, its thirty odd supplemental related laws, are "a part of the warp and woof of our economic life.

There are few who advocate its abolition," and "most observers of these laws profess to favor them," though

frequently urging substantial changes. It is the means chosen by the people of America to confirm and preserve private competitive enterprise existing under limitations placed on freedom of commercial action. 

Second: That if there is to be any change with regard to enforcement of fundamental law that approaches in many minds the dignity of Constitutional amendment it should be our constant purpose to accomplish this change by express legislative enactment, sufficiently general in terms to accommodate itself to differing economic pressures, but constant in theory, certain in interpretation, uniform in application, and simple enough to guide industry and commerce, acting in good faith, past courts of law.

Third: That despite our quest for certainty there will and must always be room for judicial, and, to a more limited extent, administrative, interpretation and choice. Those who differ with me as to the desirability of such an approach most frequently tell me that the American businessman is being ambushed by the Department of Justice; that the 

Antitrust Division is lying in wait for the innocent and unwary, and, as one Federal Judge of considerable distinction asked me, at the American Law Institute meeting at Washington, "When is the Department going to stop this ambushing of the innocent businessman?" 

In the first place, let me state that from my brief acquaintance with the prosecution of antitrust I have found some, but not many, of these "innocents." It is true, to be sure, that in almost every case I have instituted since taking office counsel for the defendants, usually with their clients along, have appeared at my office and assured me in

1:

most convincing language that their clients are reputable and respected businessmen in their various communities and that they (both clients and lawyers) are positive that they (the clients) have done nothing morally wrong. (Observe that the emphasis is on the word moral; not on the word wrong.)

One case where such a statement was made to me involved a conspiracy, established by written evidence, of a retail price-fixing agreement among certain retailers for the purpose of eliminating competition. A second case that readily comes to mind is one where certain businessmen, with the help of the union business agents they dealt with, persuaded certain bidders on government contracts to withdraw their bids, on penalty of never being able to complete or comply, because no labor would be supplied. 

When this didn't work, and the outside bidder persisted, this "moral" businessman reduced his bid by 35 per cent to undercut his persistent rival. And in still a third case a similar protestation of innocence was made with regard to a situation where force was alleged to have been used. When violence was denied by defendants' counsel, I asked him what about that certain incident when X's truck was shot at, by certain individuals using rifles, the bullets from which penetrated X's windshield. 

I was told that no bullets were actually fired; that it was a coca-cola bottle that had been thrown through the windshield. It is difficult for me to understand how any case involving such facts can be described as moral, or how those who commit such acts can believe they did no wrong.

I specify these factual situations to illustrate my point, that no matter how clearly illegal actions may be proscribed by statute or decision, there will continue to be violations. And most of the cases we file are in this category; out-and-out violations of well-recognized 

antitrust prohibitions. And this, I think, is where the antitrust bar can be of some considerable help to the administration of justice generally to meet issues squarely; and if one's client has violated the law, in a clear and unequivocal manner, let him be told so. Put the blame for that client's predicament on the law, not on the Department of Justice.

And what about the judge who asked when I would stop the lying-inambush approach? I checked his court record and discovered he had considered four cases; in three of the four, he had held the government's case was sound. And in the fourth case he held against the government on a procedural point.

One more story about the innocent client: After hearing a two-hour explanation of how pure a certain company was, then under investigation for monopolistic practices, and as counsel and its president rose to go, the latter turned to me and said: "And, Judge Barnes, I want you to know our company does not want to monopolize this industry. We have intentionally kept prices high enough to permit our principal competitor to exist." 

It was then I wished that my office had been wired for sound. His counsel was glad it wasn't.

Fourth: That certain cases that have been brought in the past by the Division may not fall within the category of violations of well defined and recognized legal prohibitions, but are on the periphery of the law, and contemplate possible extensions of legal theories, based upon political or sociological doctrines and belief. I repeat that this may or may not have been so. It is for that reason that I propose to continue our review of every one of the one hundred and thirty-nine cases pending in my Division, with the purpose of seeing if there are cases pending which neither I nor my staff would, in the first place, have recommended filing. If there are those that we would not file, we shall consider their present posture; how far they have gone; how much good can be accomplished by them; and whether the conditions sought to be remedied still exist. "That sounds well," said one of my lawyer friends in one of our discussions, "but when are you going to start dismissing cases?"

In the first place, my study of pending cases to date has convinced me that there are very few that will ultimately be terminated by dismissal. But there are some. On July 23, 1953, we dismissed the case of United States v. Columbia Gas & Electric Corporation, et al. That dismissal was overdue. Today I am able to announce the filing of a dismissal, without prejudice, of the eight year old complaint in an important case, that of United States v. Cement Institute, et al., which named as defendants eighty-nine cement companies, a number of which had been previously dismissed. The civil action has been pending

in the District of Colorado since 1945, charging defendants with a conspiracy to fix and maintain identical and noncompetitive prices on cement through the use of a multiple basing point delivered price system, in which the transportation charges included in the price were calculated on the basis of railroad freight rates only. The essence of this system of pricing was alleged to consist of an agreement that the defendants would fix and establish a delivered price at any given destination point by combining the mill price of the nearest base mill and the all-rail freight rate from that mill to said destination point.

The primary purposes for which the suit was filed, as shown by the relief which the court was asked to grant, were to break up the practice of the defendant cement companies refusing to sell cement f.o.b. the mill if the buyers so requested; to permit buyers to use truck transportation without being discriminated against in price if they desired to use truck rather than rail transportation; to have the defendant Cement Institute dissolved; and to prohibit other pricing practices.

Information secured by the Department indicates that there have been such substantial changes in the industry and its selling practices since 1945 that the principal objectives of the Department have been mostly attained and that the most important aspects of the relief sought by the complaint are not now required.

The Cement Institute was dissolved in 1946. A majority of the cement companies now sell cement f.o.b. their mills, and permit 10

transportation by truck when requested to do so by the buyers. Whereas prior to 1945 there was evidence showing almost complete identity in the prices set forth in responses to most invitations to cement companies to bid on government contracts, there has been a very substantial improvement in this respect since that time. Similarly, the degree of uniformity in prices at which sales have been made to commercial buyers, including dealers, has materially decreased.

No substantial additional benefit to the public could be expected to result from the trial of this case, which would require a great deal of time of the court, and the expenditure of much money and effort both by defendants and by the government. Good sense demands its dismissal. 

The same principle holds true in the institution of actions. We not only want to know we have a case but what we will accomplish if we win it. What good can be obtained if the Division files suit, and what harm can be done? In arriving at decisions and conclusions I can assure you that no suit will be filed that is not sound, and in the public interest. I hope that in appropriate cases, we can find the time for around-the-table discussion and explanation, before any lawsuit is filed. And after all the evidence is in, and before any decision, we hope to bring a judicial attitude to the solution of the problem. 

Perhaps we are naive in this hope. But at least we can try. In fact, my Division is already trying. In two present matters we have complaints all ready and prepared for filing. One involves a large area, a large industry, and demands large relief. The second is smaller in scope and in effect. We have notified counsel we are ready to file suit, but have asked them to confer with us looking toward a settlement. We 11

are noting carefully our success, or lack of it, in these two endeavors.

We know already it will take more of our time lawyers work perhaps not better, but certainly faster, under court deadlines. But we should profit by our experiment.

There are a great many other problems I could discuss with you. I have some definite ideas and have reached certain tentative conclusions along both procedural and substantive lines. But, as you know, I cannot discuss or express opinions as to pending cases; nor do I think it best to comment too directly on matters which we expect will be taken under consideration by the Attorney General's National Committee to Study the Antitrust Laws. Not that I won't have a part in that Committee's deliberation, but I don't wish to in any way indicate at this time any official views on the subjects it will consider. I hope you will read and consider carefully the plan of organization already read to you by 

Professor Oppenheim. I think it good.

In conclusion, may I say that I conceive the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice as a part of the Executive Branch of our government, with its purpose being the enforcement of law, as laid down by Congress and interpreted, enlarged, or restricted by our courts in their respective wisdoms; and not to be an agency for the promulgation of political or sociological doctrine or belief. This enforcement applies to the entire American public, individuals, corporations, big business, little business, and even the nonexempt activities of labor unions and farm cooperatives.
