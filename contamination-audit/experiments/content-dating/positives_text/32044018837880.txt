Foreword 

IN THE heart of the city of London, north of St. Paul's 

Cathedral and up against the Wrenish edifice known as Christ Church, Newgate Street, overlooked by the sightless windows of a department of the London General Post Office, is to be found a strip of greensward where the graves of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century citizens have been smoothed to the flatness of a lawn so that the modern citizen may sit there without an undue reminder of mortality.

This city pleasance marks the site of the ancient church of the Grey Friars which was demolished at the Dissolution in 1538. It also marks the spot where on the night of April the 20th, 1534, the mangled remains of two Grey Friars, Richard Risby, Guardian of the 

Canterbury Franciscans, and Hugh Rich, Guardian of

Sheen Friary, were interred after their execution at Tyburn. Along with the friars, so say the records, was interred the headless body of Elizabeth Barton, known as the "Holy Maid of Kent." All three suffered death for setting obstacles in the way of a pronouncement from Rome in favor of an annulment of the the marriage between King Henry VIII and his Queen, Katharine of Aragon, which was never to come. Two Benedictine monks and a secular priest suffered for the same reason. The little garden off Newgate Street, near St. 

Paul's, holds the relics of the first religious men who were offered a "pardon" from the death sentence if ix

Chapter One "BESS! BESS BARTON!" 

Mistress Cobb's voice was not usually shrill although she was a really capable housewife, but when the cakes are burning on the hearth and the children quarreling on the threshold, all at one and the same time, it is well to have a coadjutor, even if it were one less skilled at meeting a domestic emergency than Bess, the kitchen wench.

The big kitchen at Cobb's Farm retained for itself all the importance that the hub of domesticity deserves. 

Mistress Cobb, for all that her husband was steward to His Grace the Archbishop of Canterbury, spent most of her time there. It had a door leading out onto the front of the house - there was nothing snobbish about Cobb's Hall, for all that it was known by that highsounding name. As Mistress Cobb stood there invoking the absent Bess she caught sight of an approaching visitor. He had just dismounted from his horse. It was no other than Mr. Edward Thwaites, the gentleman. who had taken a tenement at Lympne and was said to be courting a relative of the Archbishop's. Mistress 

Cobb stepped back abashed. She was afraid that the visitor would be thinking her a loud-voiced scold. Mr.

Thwaites was a very devout gentleman. Sir Richard 

Masters, the Parson, held him in high esteem. In these evil days it was refreshing to find a layman who still found time to say his Day Hours. 1

Mr. Thwaites, however, greeted her with his customary affability. Tom, the eldest of the Cobb offspring, who was holding the visitor's horse, interpolated a response to the invocation, the echoes of which still haunted the air. "Bess be up at our Lady's chapel," he said. "I saw her there when I took the kettle to Giles a while back." 

Mistress Cobb completely forgot the presence of her distinguished visitor in her vexation. "There!," she exclaimed; "and I sent the kettle by you knowing that if the wench got near the chapel she'd be slipping in to say her prayers. It was to miller Fogg's that I sent her for the barley meal, and here the cakes are burning in the oven and the children trying to kill one another." 

Tom continued, stolidly. "I see her there," he said. "She was talking to our Lady." "Talking to our Lady! Go along with you. You'll be saying that our Lady was talking to her next!"

Mistress Cobb pulled herself up. The visitor intervened. "Suppose I gallop back to our Lady's shrine," he suggested, "and tell Mistress Bess that you have need of her?" 

He had remounted his horse before the amazed lady had fully grasped the practical example of chivalry. "I'm in sore straits, and the girl Bess is my right hand," she murmured, apologetically. A smell of burning and wild shouts of recrimination from the place where the children were at play upheld her statement. 

Tom held the stirrup for Mr. Thwaites. "Beware of the watchdog," he said. "Some say that he bites."

His mother frowned Tom into temporary silence. "He means the dog belonging to the fellow that lives up against the chapel," she said. "The man that does the tinkering and other odd jobs for folks. He used to look after the chapel in the days when there was a Mary Mass said there every day." Mistress Cobb sighed. She remembered the days when Court-up- 

Street was a well-known and much-sought-after shrine. "Well," Mr. Thwaites said, in his pleasant way, "he will not be biting those that come by day - the dog, I mean.' 

Tom let go of the stirrup and stood back." "Tis Giles that is the watchdog," he said, "and you be not going there to pray, you be going there to take away her that's praying." 

Mr. Edward Thwaites rode away smiling to himself at the yokel's gift of repartee. Worthy of the wits who forgathered at Sir Thomas More's house, or even of the great man himself. 

Mistress Cobb returned to her kitchen reassured. "Mistress Bess," the courteous visitor had called her. 

There was not much of the lady about poor Bess. But she was handy to turn the cakes in the oven and get the children disentangled. There they were, at it now, playing at being Lollards, which led to much fighting. 

And Bess away, saying her prayers.

Meanwhile the chivalrous messenger was pursuing his way down the steep lane which led to the little disused chapel-of-ease.

Being a newcomer he had yet to make acquaintance with our Lady's Shrine at Court-up-Street, the tiny hamlet on the roadside bordering the marsh. It was a dreary place was Romney Marsh. The inland line of

cliffs looked across it toward the bleak coast. Very soon he found himself alongside the dilapidated little building which had once been a place frequented by pilgrims. No one seemed to know the origin of the chapel at Court-up-Street or the legend connected with it. The countryside was dotted with such shrines. 

Thwaites dismounted and entered the forlorn little sanctuary. It contained a naked altar, and the roof over the sanctuary was still intact. The other parts had fallen into disrepair. To the left of the tiny chancel there stood a statue of the Blessed Virgin, as pathetic in its dereliction as the ancient place in which it stood. In the days of the pilgrimages miracles had been wrought before the image of our Lady of Court-up-Street. It was the coldness of neglect rather than the hot anger of the iconoclast that shed its atmosphere over the desolate place. It tugged at the heartstrings of the man who stood there gazing round about him, feeling the chill unkindness of it all. It seemed typical of the loveless England in which he was living. 

Suddenly he became conscious of a figure kneeling, motionless, before the statue - as motionless as the Mother herself. It must be the girl of whom he was in search. He stood contemplating the two. Perhaps of the two the girl was the less alive. There was something about the statue that seemed to live. It was the crudely executed work of a native genius. The carved lips might easily move - smile. But as Thwaites watched the face of the other, the living suppliant, his interest turned to awe. It might so well have been carved in stone. The closed eyes gave away no secrets. 

So this was the kitchen wench of whom he had come in search! The damsel who was engaged in baking

cakes and carrying barley meal. Here was a task to give a man - to recall the owner of that upturned face to earth! to return her to Mistress Cobb and her brood 

of squabblers.

Edward Thwaites was what his period called “a ghostly man." He possessed a keen palate for ghostly things. He was ready to meet the supernatural halfway. 

He felt himself being lifted into an unseen world. 

Surely there were angels round about! 

Suddenly there came a sound. A rude sound — the loud clattering caused by pot or pan being flung onto the stone floor. A man had entered from a door near the chancel. He was a big, very soiled man, with begrimed hands, and he was accompanied by a dog who was a canine reproduction of himself. Thwaites guessed at once that it would be the man who mended pots and pans and lived up against the chapel. The latter saw the visitor and made a rough apology for the noise. "I caught sight of the wench from Farmer Cobb's," he said, "and I've got a frying pan here to send back to Mistress Cobb. I thought the wench might take it along for me." He shot a glance at the motionless figure. "But I see she's laden already," he added. "If yon is Mistress Cobb's maid, I have a message for her, too," Thwaites said. “The cakes are burning and the children are murdering one another, and she is needed at home; but you can see that she is occupied." He dropped his voice as he spoke. 

The tinker cast another glance at the kneeling figure. 

His dog trotted briskly across in the direction of Farmer

Cobb's Bess and greeted her cordially by thrusting his nose between her loosely locked fingers. There was no response.

"Call that beast off!" Thwaites cried, angrily. But the tinker was as unseemly a fellow as his dog, or more so. He strode across to the kneeling girl and placing his hands close to her ear clapped them, making pistollike report. There was no response. The clown had been no more successful than his dog. Bess of Cobb's 

Farm knelt there, rigid and oblivious. 

Thwaites felt a thrill of satisfaction at the failure of the uncouth attempt to bring an ecstatic back to earth, for an ecstatic the girl certainly was. He found himself looking up at the face of the Madonna for sympathetic appreciation of the situation.

But our Lady of Court-up-Street made no comment. 

The dog stood watching, his ears cocked, his tail motionless.

The tinker, who was said to be a handyman at any job that came along, tried again. 

Without lifting his voice he addressed the girl in casual tones. "The cakes are burning in the oven," he said, “and the children are squabbling. What be thou paid for doing, Bess Barton?" 

There was a swift, sudden movement. The girl turned and faced the speaker, her bright brown eyes well in focus with the world around her. She struggled to her feet and snatched up her basket containing the miller's meal. The dog sprang forward and frisked round her in joy. "Come along quick, Slouch," she said. "We must race. I must outrun 'ee, or the cakes will be burnt." 

The pair of them were off like the wind, for all that 

Bess had her basket to carry. Mr. Edward Thwaites could only watch them disappear. It had been an

abrupt interruption of a heavenly visitation. The incongruous message had been delivered with unpardonable roughness. Thwaites turned sharply on the man who had delivered it. The latter was watching the retreating figure with no less satisfaction than his dog had shown; in a sense his tail wagging was as evident. "You seem to take singular pleasure," he observed, “in recalling our Lady's clients from Heaven to earth." 

The other kept his tail up. "She's all right," he said. "I had my doubts, but if she had been rapt into the third Paradise of fools she'd have stayed there and not heeded me. Heaven has set her to cook food and mind urchins - that's her business, not orisons." He looked hard at the other. Then he softened and became confidential. "I had a fear," he said, "that it might have been a strange angel that had got hold of her. There are such in search of a habitation, and yon's a curious wench. 

There's a door somewhere that she goes out of; but others may come in." He shook his head. "But she is a good lass, is Bess."

Thwaites eyed the speaker curiously. This rough fellow was shrewd. More handy in his methods than even the manipulation of candle wax and wicks would imply. "She'll need be careful," Giles continued. "The noonday fiend hath a third Heaven all of his own."

He picked up the pan which was lying on the ground and examined the soldered corner. "I must take it along myself," he said. "Give it to me," Thwaites said. "I am going back to

Cobb's. If I overtake the girl I will give her a lift on my saddle."

"You won't overtake her," Giles said; "but I'll be kindly thankful if you will take the frying pan along. 

God reward your charity to a man with a stiff leg."

He handed the article in question to the faultlessly attired gentleman. “I could put an old clout round it, if you liked," he said. 

But Mr. Thwaites was in far too great a hurry; neither did he fancy the clout. He took possession of the pan and rode off at a brisk trot.

It was not until he came in sight of Cobb's Hall that he caught sight of Bess's cloak flying in the air as she sped through the gate. Bess had indeed a fleet foot-with a neglected duty awaiting her. She had left the gate open and Mr. Thwaites rode up to the house. A group of children were gathered on the green patch. In their midst stood the object of his pursuit. Bess had seized a screaming infant whose sense of outrage had started the tumult and seated him on the broad and friendly back of Slouch, a favorite playmate of their's. Benny 

Cobb was having a novel ride. The others watched. 

Peace had been restored for the moment.

Thwaites was conscious that he cut a quaint figure with the frying pan in his gloved hand. It was a little serving wench that he was about to address but he had been proud to make his mare the pack horse of our 

Lady's client.

Bess, who was still panting from her exertions, dropped Benny Cobb and a curtsy at one and the same moment. The gentleman on horseback leant over and presented her with the frying pan and an explanation of how he happened to be in possession of it. "I was in the chapel just now," he said.

Bess made another, this time unimpeded, curtsy.

Thwaites noted that she had a broad face of a peasant type, with a well-molded chin. She might be sixteen, or perhaps older. "I was there praying to our Lady," she said, “and I forgot the time, and -oh! the cakes are burning. I must run!"

She was off into the house, the frying pan in one hand and the basket in the other. Thwaites followed.

His business was with the Archbishop's bailiff but he made for the door leading into the kitchen. He was anxious to see the end of the drama of the cakes.

Mistress Cobb was there, at the further end of the great kitchen. She was bending over the open oven. 

Bess was standing beside her. Thwaites saw her face in profile, as he had seen it in the chapel. It certainly was a remarkable face although the features were homely. 

Mistress Cobb spoke, in tones of amazement. "And I could have declared that they were burning," she said. “The smell was all over the place. I hadn't the heart to look. It must have been something else all the time. They ought to be burnt to cinders." 

But the cakes were evidently not what they ought to have been. According to Mistress Cobb's next remark they were "just done to a turn."

Chapter Two 

RICHARD MASTERS, the Rector of Aldington, a pleasant enough little place in its way, on the borders of Romney Marsh in the county of Kent, was more comfortably housed than most of his kind. Aldington was a plump little benefice in spite of its revenues being depleted to the extent of twenty pounds per annum paid to a certain Master Erasmus who had been made nominal rector in recognition of his eminent scholarship. Richard Masters himself was not a great scholar but he had a respect for learning. He gave it hospitality in his bookcase. He had quite a good collection of books - he liked a well-furnished room, and what a later age would have called "the amenities of life."

He was seated in his library one evening meditating deeply on the question of the best way to keep foxes away from things for which foxes have a fancy when a parishioner was announced. It was tiresome, but parishioners are the fly in the ointment of a parish priest. 

Parson Masters had a conscience, and he recognized the fact that in this case the flies were native to the ointment. He relinquished the fox problem with a sigh. 

The visitor proved to be Farmer Cobb, the Archbishop's bailiff, a parishioner of some standing. Farmer

Cobb was a simple enough fellow. It was evident that he was in a great state of mind over something. He dropped into a chair, and then sat and gazed at the Parson as though the suggestion as to his business might come from him. 10

"Well," the Parson said, "how goes the family?” "The family be all right enough," Farmer Cobb replied. Then he added: "It be Bess that I've come about." "Bess?" the Rector echoed. "What ails Bess?" "That's what I've come to know," was the answer. "She's been taken with a sickness that we's never seen the likes of. She has pains, and goes stiff and is like to choke herself - or be choked by some 'at." "But haven't you fetched the leech?" the other asked. "This is surely his business." "He said it were not," Cobb replied, laconically. "He came, and he said it were the devil." 

Richard Masters frowned. "Tush!" he said, "an ignorant leech is swift to blame the devil. Do you think it's the devil?" 

Thomas Cobb shook his head. "If it's the devil," he said, "then the devil's been converted. The things that yon maid says when she can get her speech are none of the devil's saying. They're more like words sent direct from Heaven." 

Masters deliberated. "Bess is a good maid," he said. "There is no reason why the devil should have truck with her, unless it were to do her a despite. Has Mistress Cobb given her a posset?" 

Cobb shook his head again. "She weeps not for the pains in her stomach," he said, "'tis for the sins of the worldly folk, them that she calls the parents of heresy. 

She is as able of speech as though she were speaking the words of the Holy Spirit Himself." 

Masters frowned again. "Preaching comes amiss from the mouth of a maiden," he said. He could not but feel disturbed. A maiden that would trace the pedigree of a

heretic back to the ill-practicing Catholic would not appear to be speaking of her own conceit. This certainly must be looked into. 

He rose and fetched his hat. "I'll come along with you," he said to Farmer Cobb. "I'll see the maid for myself."

The little maid, Bess Barton, had always been a favorite with the Parson. He had known her ever since 

Farmer Cobb and his wife had given a home to the waif whose parents had abandoned her in the big barn one winter's night some sixteen or seventeen years ago.

He had watched her grow up, and marked the strength underlying her simple, straightforward character. One might expect great things of Bess; but preaching ill became a maid, and especially in these days when the heretics set their womenkind to expound the Scriptures which their scholars had translated into the tongue of the people. Many a quick-witted wench had made ribald fun of the ordinances of Holy Church by picking upon a word that appeared to make them void, and shown no small skill at the unhallowed game.

There was a curious scene in the kitchen of Cobb's Hall when Masters arrived with the farmer. Bess lay on an improvised bed in one corner. The children. huddled together in another, casting frightened glances in her direction. As the priest approached her she opened her eyes and made an attempt to say something but her tones were strangled and indistinct, as though her throat were being held in some invisible grip. Masters sat down beside her. Mistress Cobb moved away.

Bess might be wanting to make her confession. Not that she could have much on her soul - she had spoken of Heaven just before the priest came as though she had

been there. She had been mending little Sam's coat and telling him stories when the strange attack suddenly seized her. She had lain there motionless after the pains subsided, and then she had opened her eyes and she had spoken the wonderful things which she would be telling the priest about. 

Bess's eyes were now fixed upon the priest. She made another attempt to speak and this time her utterance was clearer. "Tell Sammy that it was all right. The little bear got some dinner. He's crying."

Sammy, in good sooth, was sending up a lugubrious wail.

Parson Masters looked across interrogatively at Mistress Cobb who was well within earshot - her retirement had been but a gesture. "The little bear?" he repeated. This was no message from Heaven. "'Tis the story she was telling Sammy," Mistress

Cobb explained. "The story of the three bears. She was telling it to him when she was taken with the pains." 

Richard Masters was relieved. Bess was evidently her normal self again. He had questioned the strange spirit which talked about bears and their dinner. Mistress

Cobb bustled herself to get a posset ready for Bess who was still short of breath and very exhausted. 

"The pain has almost gone," Bess whispered. "But, Father," a frightened look had come into her eyes - "God will not let them hurt our good Queen, will He?" "Tut tut, who's going to hurt the Queen?" Masters said. "Our good King would make short shrift of them if they did." 

He called Sammy over to them. "See," he said, “here

is Sammy waiting to hear the end of the story. Silverlocks did not in sooth rob the baby bear of his dinner, did she?"

Sammy thus addressed, screwed up his queer little face into a knowing smile. He was a sickly little creature, Mistress Cobb's one failure, physically speaking. "I know the end of the story," he said, "The little bear gobbled up Silverlocks as well as his dinner."

The Rector was mightily amused, but Bess shuddered. “That's a bad, cruel ending, Sammy," she said. "Why?" Sammy asked. "Silverlocks went to Heaven, didn't she?" Whereat Richard Masters laughed the louder, and Bess was fain to join in. "Such a pity," Mistress Cobb said, as she accompanied the Rector to the door, "that she should have taken to talking that child's nonsense just when you came along, for it was wonderful what she had been saying things about Heaven; and the devil was holding her throat so that she might not get them out.' "Well," the other answered, "at any rate they can't say that she was saying them for the benefit of the parson.

Tom Cobb, the eldest of the family, came running after him. "Be Bess going to die?" he inquired. "Die? Why, no. Why ever should she?" Richard Masters administered swift comfort to the boy in whose eyes the tears were standing. "She doesn't like this world," Tom said. "I thought that God might be taking her to Heaven for good. She goes there sometimes when she's saying her prayers."

"Stuff and nonsense!" Richard Masters said. "Who's been telling you that?" "It were Mr. Thwaites," Tom said. "Him that's courting Mistress Anne Warham." "Ah, the new squire of Lympne," Masters said. "He goes and says his prayers at the chapel up the lane since he saw Bess praying to our Lady there,” Tom went on. "He says that Bess be a saint." "That would be how Giles would put it," Richard 

Masters said.

He went on his way pondering. All this about the chapel at Court-up-Street was new to him. It had served as a chapel-of-ease to his own church in bygone days. It was somewhat on his conscience that it had been allowed to fall into disrepair. The new young squire of 

Lympne had apparently taken a fancy to the place. He might perhaps be willing to put it into repair and make it fit for use.

The illness which had overtaken Bess Barton proved to be a strange malady which baffled the skill of the physician. She grew steadily worse, day by day. The seizures of pain increased in frequency. In these days

Bess, prostrated and unable to move, lay on her improvised bed in the corner of the kitchen, not even at this stage a useless member of the household for she would gather the children round her and tell them stories, grave or gay as the occasion demanded. When the seizures came on the children crept away, for then Bess said many strange things such as those that were said by the preaching friars about the wickedness of the world - such words as one of the women who had met the Lord on the Via Dolorosa, fulfilling her commission, might have addressed to the denizens of the doomed city. Bess had many visitors in these days. Folk who were in trouble came along to pour their woes into her ready ear. They had a feeling that the sick girl was there to listen to them. The evil-living folk came along, too. Mistress Cobb looked askance at first at these latter, but Bess had a way with them as she had with naughty children. They went away repenting their misdeeds.

There were some, of course, who came from sheer curiosity, for tales were getting about concerning Bess Barton's visits to Heaven. But there was danger in such visits, for Bess had a way of getting at things hidden in their hearts, and she was fearless and unsparing in bringing them to light. It was safer on the whole for such folk to keep their distance from Bess Barton, with her straight gaze and simple speech.

One day Parson Masters received a visit from young Tom Cobb. He had brought a message. He stood there in the Rectory parlor twirling his hat in his hand and hunting for the right words. ""Tis a message from Bess," he said, "and 'tis a message from our Lady as well."

Masters glanced at him, sharply. It was not the first

1 time that Farmer Cobb's Bess had claimed to have had a message from our Lady. “And what might the message be?” he asked. "Our Lady would fain have a Mass said in her honor in the chapel at Court-up-Street for the conversion of sinners," Tom said. "Bess told us so whilst the devil had his thumbs on her throat so that she might not speak it. And she would fain have a taper set up there by Bess herself for she hath promised to cure her of her illness." 

Richard Masters eyed the speaker in perplexity. "But the chapel is not furnished for the saying of Holy 

Mass," he objected. "That be all right," Tom replied. "Mr. Thwaites hath told Giles that the chapel is to be made fit for Mass, and he will pay for the doing of it." "Mr. Thwaites will have to speak to me," Masters said, rather tartly. All the same, to press his own position in regard to the neglected chapel would be to bring reproof on himself. Then he added, "And how, pray, is Bess to get to Mass when she cannot put her feet to the ground?" "We would carry her there," Tom replied. "And she would walk home all right for our Lady is going to cure her." "Well," the Rector said, slowly. "If the chapel can be made decent, and the maid can be brought thither, I see no reason why I should not please her in this respect. Not, mind you, that I set any particular store by her fancies."

So Tom carried the good news back to Cobb's Hall. 

Bess was lying on her bed with Sammy at her feet. 

Quite distinguished visitors had occupied that seat.

Mr. Edward Thwaites was a frequent visitor, and sometimes he brought Mistress Anne Warham, the Archbishop's niece with him. Mistress Anne was very shortly to wed the squire of Lympne. "If our Lady cures 'ee, Bess," Sammy said, "will 'ee still tell me stories?" "Of course I will, Sammy. Why not?" "But if thee be cured," Sammy maintained, "thee won't go to Heaven and back again because thee won't have any more pains." "Who says that I go to Heaven when I have pains?” 

Bess asked him. "Thee dost listen to silly stories, Sammy." 

Sammy replied, crisply. "Him that has a dead wife at home." "Old Wat?" "Aye. He old Tom that 'ee had been to Heaven, and there was a bit of glory clinging to thee. What is a bit of glory, Bess?"

His remark eliciting no answer, Sammy continued. "Perhaps our Lady may let 'ee walk about and still have the pains. Then 'ee can go about and tell people about 

Heaven, like 'ee told Wat's wife before she died." "I might walk away from thee, Sammy."

It was an alarming suggestion. "Or 'ee might stay in 

Heaven," Sammy said. “I think if I went to Heaven I would like to stop there." "I'm sure 'ee would, Sammy, and I'd not fetch 'ee back."

Bess was surveying the little wizened form thoughtfully. 

Tom Cobb had heard enough about Heaven. "Our

His vitality called forth a response from the sick girl. 

Her eyes grew bright, and a quiver ran through her helpless limbs. "Oh, Tom," she cried. "Do'st 'ee think that our Lady will make me walk. Do'st 'ee think that she will take away the pains?" "She said she would," Tom said. "It might not be this time," Bess murmured. “I might have to wait." "Giles is making the biggest taper that ever was,"

Bess, by way of answer, closed her eyes. She lay there motionless, struggling for breath. The symptoms were too familiar. Tom fled to fetch his mother. She still had belief in possets although Mr. Thwaites had shaken his head over them. Sammy remained where he was, gazing with large eyes at Bess. A few minutes elapsed, ere the latter opened her eyes. 

Sammy put out a hand and touched her — a soft, fondling touch. "Hast 'ee been to Heaven, Bess?" he asked.

Bess glanced round. The two were alone. "Aye, Sammy,” she whispered.

Chapter Nineteen 

OTFORD MANOR was a quiet, unpretentious residence. 

The new Archbishop was a man of simple tastes, like his predecessor. The visitors who had left their enclosure at his behest were received with all the respect demanded by their position. Bess Barton was not unfamiliar with the pomp and circumstance, such as it was, of episcopal surroundings. There had been very little of it at Rochester - a great deal on the occasion when she had visited the Lord Cardinal of York. Otford was but a quiet country house where an overworked prelate might hide from his friends as well as his clients. There was nothing to strike awe into the soul of the visitor.

The Archbishop himself, when he appeared on the scene, proved to be a gentle, kindly mannered man, with a suave and, one might say, deferential manner. 

He treated the nun with as much respect as he showed to her Prioress, and when the former had taken stock of him she decided that he would be easy to talk to and not unfatherly. "I would fain speak to thee alone," she told him, casting an eye on the chaplain who was present, and the

The other glanced at him in some surprise. She had 162

Gently he led her on to speak of the high favors which Heaven had condescended to bestow on her, a poor unlettered sinner. The listener was plainly deeply impressed. Your Grace be now speaking," she declared, seeking an illustration near at hand.

She regarded him intently. Had she a message? This was the man who represented the Church in England. 

He had been listening to her with sympathy. She had seen a tear in his eye when she spoke of the sweet things of the spirit. She had warned the Cardinal: she had borne a message to the King. What did Heaven wish her to say to Thomas Cranmer?

Chapter Twenty 

NEVER had the city of Canterbury been in such a ferment since the day when three knights had ridden, red handed, through the city gate. Visitors - terrible official visitors - from the King's Majesty had arrived at the Benedictine House to investigate a matter of high importance; to wit, the alleged writing of seditious books by certain of the monks cloistered there, which same books they had caused to be imprinted and were putting into circulation.

The seditious works, so it was bruited about, related to no other person than Dame Elizabeth Barton, the "Holy Maid of Kent." Never had such an upheaval been known. People listened, and tried to credit their ears with hearing aright. Dame Elizabeth, they were being told, was no holy maid but a hypocrite nun who had been working with certain monks and friars ill affected toward the Crown for the overthrowing of the throne of England.

The chapel at Court-up-Street, so the word went round, had been taken possession of by the King's officers so that no more superstitious and false miracles originated by the nun might take place there. As for the nun herself, she had been committed to the Tower of London to be tried for her imposture, together with her confederates.

The number of the latter increased every day. When 

Friar Risby, Head of the Franciscan House, was ap- 170

prehended and carried off to Canterbury Castle for examination the populace held its breath and wondered what would come next. 

Master Christopher Hales, the King's Attorney-General, had ransacked the cell of Dr. Edward Bocking, the saintliest of the monks, and found there papers dealing with the so-called revelations of the hypocrite nun. Canterbury rubbed its eyes and asked if it were dreaming? It was the first time that "so called" had been applied to the revelations of the Holy Maid, except by heretics and evildoers.

It would seem that holiness and blameless living were the qualities which would bring a man within the danger zone!

By degrees facts leaked out. The great book which 

Dr. Bocking had under the press concerning Dame 

Elizabeth's spiritual favors had been seized at the printer's and every copy carefully destroyed. The book written by Mr. Edward Thwaites had also been condemned and every copy burned, together with the rolls containing the authentic revelations. All that remained on record of the sayings and doings of the Holy Maid were the wild stories that passed from mouth to mouth, gathering in sensation as they traveled. These were being carefully collected and set down to form the archives of the hypocrite nun.

The thunderbolt fell heavily on the little community at St. Sepulchre's. So this was the meaning of the lodging at the King's palace within the Tower! Tom Cobb had brought them that much of news some days before. 

Dame Philippa's heart stood still. So that had indeed been the meaning of the gentle-voiced Archbishop's words, hideous in their irony.

But what - what had Sister Elizabeth been guilty of? 

They were talking of treason! What had Bess done that could possibly be associated with treason? She had been so anxious that the King should be warned of the danger in which he was placing himself. She had not rested until he had been warned - by herself, in spite of all that had been said to her to prevent her from doing so. 

And the holy fathers? The monks who had written books about her. Whatever could it be that involved them in such a charge? If there was treason in the books why destroy them ere they had borne their witness? 

Witnesses they would indeed have been to the holiness of the maid who was being denounced as an impostor.

The Prioress and her community fell to praying, and awaited the development of the waking nightmare. 

Had daytime ever produced such a fantasy in the minds of men? 

Meanwhile Mr. Secretary Cromwell's maneuver was succeeding with the most complete success. All those who had had any truck with the "Hypocrite Nun" were being charged with being her accomplices. Everyone, in short, who was known to be opposed to the annulment of the King's marriage had become involved. 

There was the Bishop of Rochester, a troublesome fellow with a conscience, and the late Lord Chancellor, Thomas More, another of the same kidney. The latter had, it was true, refrained from giving an opinion on the King's marriage until Rome had spoken, but he had been known to have had communication with the nun and on that pretext he could be named as a confederate.

It was a list that made glad the heart of Mr. Secre-

tary Cromwell. Not only ecclesiastics were implicated and men of Sir Thomas More's standing, but a long list of nobility, major and minor, and other wealthy men from whom large sums of money could be extracted in the form of fines, not to mention the sums that passed between an accused person and those who had influence with the King's judges. It was only fastidious men like Thomas More who jibbed at such practices. 

Thomas Cromwell was out to fill his moneybags, and a golden hour had arrived.

Developments were rapid. In the course of a few weeks all those high-born dames who had been ardent clients of the Holy Maid had so far readjusted their opinion of her as to write abject letters to Mr. Secretary Cromwell seeking excuses for having listened to as the terrified Marchioness of Exeter put it — “the blasphemous, execrable, and false prophecies uttered by that most unworthy person, the Hypocrite Nun." 

Such letters would be accompanied by a substantial sum of money and gave great satisfaction to their recipient. But the people who were not in high places, the poor folk who had no money to risk and no possessions to lose, listened in bewilderment to the stories that were going abroad. Had not the Holy Maid prayed them back to health and healed them with the gentle touch of her hand in the old days when she was "Bess of Cobb's Hall"? Had she not turned their hearts to

God and His Holy Mother with words that were heavenly rather than of this earth? She had made rough men weep for their sins long before she had truck with prelates and learned clerks. For those who had known

Heaven. Little disputes had sprung up amongst the

Maid has stirred their souls to action. Cromwell is right. She is the most redoubtable enemy of the King's unholy action; but not," he added, "of the King. Did the King but take her warning he would remain in the hearts of his people; but it would seem to be too late."

"Well, at any rate," Tom said, sturdily, "the Pope hath not given way to the King. Holy Church has not made twain those that were one."

The madness of the moment had nowhere made itself more felt than at Chelsea where the late Lord Chancellor lived in a retirement that brought him little peace. Thomas More's resignation of the chancellorship had been followed by a courtesy on the part of the King - he had received an invitation to the latter's marriage with the Marchioness of Pembroke, together with twenty pounds wherewith to buy himself a wedding garment. Thomas More had declined the invitation. His action had brought upon him the King's displeasure. Preposterous charges of corrupt practices had been brought against the ex-judge of the King's Bench, and when these were disposed of, most absurd of all, it was brought against him that he had implicated himself in the conspiracy of the Hypocrite Nun and her accomplices. For a time it had seemed as though this last charge might lead to real trouble, for it was so obviously a pretext for ensnaring the late Lord Chancellor in the general charge of High Treason brought against those who had had dealings with Elizabeth Barton. Sir Thomas prepared his defense with lawyerlike acumen. He stated exactly what intercourse he had had with the nun, and left her accusers to form their own judgment. 

At that anxious time Dame Alice More let go her opinions with some warmth. Somewhat unexpectedly she took the part of the Holy Maid.

"High treason, forsooth," Mrs. Alice declared. “Did not conspirators go about wearing masks, with their fingers on their lips? The nun had told her High 

Treason to the King's face. 'Twas such a mock charge as Paterson might have made up by way of fooling."

Meg was in full accord with her stepmother. She was in some perplexity as to her father's attitude toward the 

Holy Maid. Sir Thomas had almost appeared ready to acquiesce in the charge of hypocrisy brought against her. Her father, as was his wont, showed her the letter which he had addressed to Mr. Secretary Cromwell. Her father answered her. "Neither does Mr. Secretary. I am not making him wise, save in this, that he shall know that a lawyer cannot easily be ensnared into uttering words which, whilst they in no way add to the sum of another's knowledge, yet may be used for an evil purpose against the writer." 

Meg stretched out her hands with a little tired gesture. "It is such a battle of wits," she said. "But I know well, Father," she added hastily, "how hard you did try to move the Maid from her busy ways with things that you did not consider to be her concern. If she had but listened to you!" 

The other smiled. "I am not an angel," he reminded her. "But perchance she was right in mistrusting a man in fleshly habit. Men, in good sooth, have become more fantastic than the shapes that affright the children after dark."

There was some confusion in the minds of those who tried to follow the proceedings in their legal aspect. 

Elizabeth Barton appeared to be the tool of those who in their turn were "dupes" of her cunning impostures of which they were the instigators; all of which was perplexing, to say the least of it. The charges of heresy and witchcraft, moreover, were being brought forward to further elaborate the complex indictment. Elizabeth 

Barton might very easily be brought to the stake on either of those counts. But the main charge was said to be that of High Treason, arising from the fact that the maid had agitated against the King's second marriage.

But the trial had turned on the charge of treason brought against the maid and her adherents. The crowd heard all this and waited for more. There came a report of Chancellor Audley's speech. He had

Where was Bess?

Chapter Twenty-Two 

THE citizens of London were to be treated to a spectacle - a play on an open-air stage - in which the actors would receive no wage for their pains. The 

Hypocrite Nun and her confederates were to make an open confession of their fraud from a platform erected below Paul's Cross, in Cheapside.

The nun and her accomplices would stand in a row and listen to a sermon to be preached by Dr. Capon, the new Bishop of Bangor, setting forth the deceits of the so-called "Holy Maid." The notion of a holy maid proving to be a hypocrite of the first order, and a conspirator to wit, was even a more savory titbit than the story of the Holy Maid's miracles for a certain type of mind, not necessarily antireligious. The populace was bent on getting its entertainment out of the Paul's 

Cross drama. 

Very early in the morning of the fateful November day Tom Cobb took up his position where the workmen were busy putting up the scaffold. There he waited patiently, in a kind of dull stupor, like one in a dream, watching the crowd assemble, each one eager for a good sight and a good hearing of the coming drama. It was the Papal Nuncio who afterwards grimly alluded to it as "the comedy at Paul's Cross." 

Tom asked himself the perpetual question, what had Bess got to confess? What had the holy men, the monks, and the learned Rector of Aldermary whose 186

nose was always in his books, got to do with conspiracies? 

Poor Mr. Thwaites had been dragged into it, too, and 

Sir Richard Masters, the parson of Aldington. And to add to all, they had called this an act of public penance. 

The prisoners would be assenting to it as such. At that price they would earn their "pardon."

An immense crowd had congregated. Such a number as had not gathered for forty years. Such a spectacle as this had never yet been witnessed in that place of grim pageantry. Kings had been uncrowned there, but never yet had monks and nuns been held up to the ridicule and contumacy on a public scaffold like this! Their cloth had protected them. A mob might murder an archbishop, or an outlaw give short shrift to an overfed abbot, but nothing like this had been enacted in the name of the law.

The crowd stood in tense silence as the "criminals" were led up, one by one, onto the platform. The two 

Benedictine monks, Father Bocking and Father De ring, and the two friars, Rich and Risby, were wearing their habits; Dr. Gold and Richard Masters followed in their gowns. The nun came last. She also was wearing her habit. From a Cromwellian point of view the whole thing was uncommonly well done.

There was for Tom's eyes but one figure. Bess was perfectly self-possessed. No doubt numbers of people were hoping that she would go off into a trance and so provide an additional sensation. It might be even more exciting than a confession, but she remained calm. Her mouth was closed in a firm line. Her eyes inexpressibly sad. She had cast one tense glance at her confederates.

The drawn faces of the friars and their bent forms told their own tale. The "Hypocrite Nun" had given a little

shudder. She was holding in her hand a piece of paper. 

Its written contents would only be known to her through another more learned in the art of reading and lettering than Bess Barton.

Dr. Capon had mounted his pulpit. He was the first 

Bishop of the King's creation. Rome had no hand in his making. He had been Abbot of Hyde and was a strong King's man.

The discourse was a summary of the Lord Chancellor's harangue to the Lords of the Star Chamber. The nun stood convicted by her own words of having prevented Cardinal Wolsey from giving sentence to the

King's divorce. Her adherents had been guilty of levity, credulity, and superstition in believing the nun's prophecies, especially those concerning the King's 

Grace: and of treason in not revealing to the King a matter so deeply concerning his person and honor. 

The crowd listened in silence. A silence not unlike that with which it had greeted the new queen when she had ridden through the streets of the city. Dr. Capon was not persona grata with the crowd.

The time had come for the nun's confession to be read. All eyes turned toward the central figure on the platform. Silently Elizabeth Barton stepped forward and handed the paper in her hand to the preacher. He opened it and in a loud voice read as follows: "I Dame Elizabeth Barton do confess that I, most miserable and wretched person, have been the origin of all this mischief and by my falsehood have deceived all these persons here and many more, by which I have most grievously offended Almighty God and my most noble sovereign the King's Grace."

At this juncture Tom saw Bess glance up quickly at

the reader. Her lips opened and closed again. Her drooped eyelid hid the flash that had come into her eye. 

Was she about to speak? No, her lips remained closed. "Therefore I humbly, and with heart most sorrowful desire you to pray to Almighty God for my most miserable sins and make supplication for me to my most noble Sovereign for his gracious mercy and pardon."

The silence which held the crowd had deepened. 

There was a slight movement amongst the five fellow culprits. It might have been a shudder or a shrug that 

Hugh Rich gave as he listened to the words delivered by the King's bishop. It was the maid's "confession."

No confession had been wrung from Hugh Rich in the Tower dungeons. It remained for her to disclaim it. 

But the woman on the platform remained silent.

The comedy was over. The crowd surged backward into Cheapside. 

"That was no confession of her making," Tom heard one man say to another. It was a dangerous speech a speech that could get a man cast into jail. Tom knew that he was right - he must be right. Bess did not tell lies even to save the lives of others to save them at the expense of their honor. 

It was all a hideous nightmare. 

Bess would presumably receive the royal pardon and return to her convent and the monks to their monastery. Poor Mr. Thwaites had had a narrow escape. 

Tom rode home to Canterbury that same day. He passed the night at Rochester, the city which the Holy Maid had well-nigh orphaned through the steadfast adherance of its father-in-God to her cause. The next day found him wending his way toward Cobb's Hall.

It was not his family that he was anxious to see so much

as Giles. He dreaded facing Dame Philippa with the story of Paul's Cross. He had sent on a message by another hand, the same that was to apprize the Abbot of Christchurch of the baleful proceedings. Giles was a person who had a way of stiffening one up. He was better at that than all the monks and friars. But he did it in his own way.

Court-up-Street presented a sorry spectacle as Tom approached the chapel of our Lady. Once again desolation reigned there. The place had been completely dismantled and indeed, desecrated. No one should ever again set up a candle to our Lady at Court-up-Street. 

The scandal of a disgraceful superstition had been wiped out.

Tom peeped in on the scene of desolation and a pang shot through his heart. He was thinking of the words of the confession. Had Bess herself overturned the statue of our Lady? Of what had she repented?

"It shall not be said that our

What had Bess got to confess to? She had been to visit the King, but the great folk had put her up to it. Mistress Cobb shook her head. She had always been of opinion that Bess should have remained at Cobb's Hall and cooked the dinner. All this flattering by fine ladies and holy monks had been the ruin of Bess. Mistress

He found the city agog with the news of the Holy Maid's confession, which had reached it the night before by means of the riders who changed their post horses at short stages and so journeyed at high speed. Much skepticism was expressed as to the genuineness of the confession. Confessions were extracted from prisoners by means which made them of little account.

St. Sepulchre's was chiefly concerned to know how

soon Sister Elizabeth might be expected home - how soon she would be out of the clutches of the smoothspoken minions of the Crown who had lured her away with such fine speeches and fair promises. 

Sister Alys, the portress, had something to say to 

Tom about "toads with two faces."

Poor Dame Philippa had all but completely collapsed. Her turn might come any moment. The latest news, gleaned through Christchurch Priory, was that the ceremony conducted at Paul's Cross was to be repeated in Canterbury itself, and various local misdemeanants would be added to those who did their penance for the second time. Tom sickened at the news. The devil's hand seemed to be in it all. The touch of the grotesque was so evident. It was all twisted and contorted, like the devil's bodily shape when he appeared to folk. They could laugh at the devil in the mystery plays. But this was no laughing matter.

The silver lining to the black cloud was that, once returned to her convent, Bess would not be allowed to go out any more into the world which had laid its snares so skillfully. 

But Bess had not yet returned.

Chapter Twenty-Three 

THE re-enactment of the scene at Paul's Cross which took place at Canterbury, on Oaten Hill, near to the Nunnery of St. Sepulchre, proved to be what a later century would describe as a complete "washout." The 

King's Commissioners in arranging it had overreached themselves for once. Elizabeth Barton was by no means discredited amongst the simple folk who had been the recipients of her favors in the early days. They were moreover the Queen's henchmen, and the King's hirelings could make but little headway with them. The crowd which assembled outside the Dane John, near to the Riding Gate, was one seething with resentment. A crowd that would have been dangerous had it dared. 

The Commissioners had added to the number of players in the drama by including certain local accomplices of the Hypocrite Nun. The Dominican anchorite, an austere personage who had received visits from the nun, so it appeared, had been dragged there from the cell from which he had never been known to emerge.

The late chaplain at Court-up-Street was likewise on exhibition on the scaffold; and as though to add a touch of ridicule to the nun's following the tinker, who was called "the hermit of Court-up-Street," had been set there with the others, a ragged and mirth-provoking figure. 

The sermon on this occasion was preached by Dr. 

Nicholas Heath whose diatribes were received with a 195

chilling silence that would fain have relieved itself in boos and cat calls. The monks and friars who stood amongst the "penitents" were men well known and respected by the people of Canterbury. All the ranting of the King's hirelings could not change them into monsters of iniquity, or even into the dupes of a Hypocrite Nun. East Kent was not having its saint turned into a hypocrite. London might do as it choose. The rabble who had nothing to pass from its pocket into that of Master Cromwell stood solid for its Holy Maid. 

The confession farce was not repeated. The whole proceeding had but served to form an anticlimax to what had gone before. More harm than good had been done by producing Elizabeth Barton and her accomplices in their native place. 

Tom Cobb had gazed with astonishment at the spectacle of Giles in his ragged leather apron standing with the others on the scaffold. It was evident that Dame 

Philippa had escaped by the skin of her teeth. That poor lady was in a state of semicollapse from the fear of what might happen. When all was over and the prisoners were taken away, the tinker was turned loose to go his way. Tom came across him in the crowd. Giles was looking as proud as a man who had just received the order of Knighthood. ""Twas an honor that I had not hoped for," he told Tom. "Never before have I found myself in such good company. Master Hales was the only blot on the picture." "But what be they going to do with Bess?" Tom asked. "Will she not be free now to return to us?" 

The other's face became grave. "I fear me," he said, "that there be an honor in store for her in which I may not share.”

Giles was right. St. Sepulchre's waited in vain for the return of its disgraced member. Dame Philippa tremblingly listened to each peal that came at the doorbell. "Be it Sister Elizabeth?" her children cried - all but 

Marjorie. Sister Marjorie shared with Tom the dark feeling of apprehension. She minded well that day when Sister Bess's horse had been led home with an empty saddle. 

News of Dame Elizabeth came to them at last. The 

King's Officer rode up to St. Sepulchre's and delivered to the person of Dame Philippa Johns, Lady Prioress, a notification of the fact that a Bill of Attainder for 

High Treason of Elizabeth Barton and her accessories had been presented to the House of Lords and would be duly dealt with. 

The devil had not tripped up over his own tail, after all.

The new development in the affair of the Hypocrite Nun was a staggering blow to those who had imagined that the matter was now done with. The inclusion of the name of Sir Thomas More who up till recently had held the office of Lord Chancellor made many feel that the world was indeed turning upside down. Sir Thomas, however, was, as his daughter had playfully reminded him, a lawyer as well as a saint. He had scented the snare spread for him in the questions asked by My 

Lord Cromwell. "If My Lord Cromwell had proved all these things against the nun then verily she was a busybody." The man to whom Sir Thomas indited his letter of defense possessed a very accurate knowledge of the facts of the case. He knew, moreover, that he was dealing with one whose game was a lawyer's game. He most

certainly received no fresh impression of the character of the nun from that concerning her which was spoken conditionally. It remained for future generations that might come across the record of Sir Thomas More's defense, to take, without the subtle interplay of wits between two men versed in legal methods, the words of the knight of Chelsea concerning the Holy Maid of Kent.

All England, of whatever party, was relieved by the news that the name of Sir Thomas More had been removed from the bill of attainder, together with that of Bishop Fisher, whose defense of the Holy Maid had been both naïve and wholehearted. It had been an utterly unthinkable thing that such great and holy men as these should be tried as traitors. But the unthinkable as well as the unexpected sometimes can happen. Cromwell had shown himself to be resourceful in methods where one failed. 

St. Sepulchre's prayed and hoped. The community at Christchurch prayed and hoped. Surely the House of

Lords would not set aside the findings of the Court of the Star Chamber? Elizabeth Barton and her followers were back in the Tower of London. The age knew well enough of trials that were but the ratifications of private condemnations. The Maid of Kent stood condemned by another maid of Kent, to wit, the woman who called herself Queen of England. Anne Boleyn had not yet lost her power over the King for all that it was on the wane. 

On March the 13th, in the year 1534, a messenger rode posthaste into Canterbury. He rode to the Priory of Christchurch where the monks were assembled at prayer. He delivered his tidings.

Elizabeth Barton and her accomplices, including Dom Edward Bocking and Dom John Dering, had been convicted of High Treason and had been sentenced to death.

The edict was read out by proclamation at the Cattle Market. Tom Cobb carried the news to St. Sepulchre's. "High Treason." The words buzzed in his head. Bess had not been condemned for witchcraft; nor for practicing conjuring tricks and deceiving credulous monks. 

She had been condemned for warning the King of a terrible menace that threatened him. The devil, the archenemy, was at his gate and Bess had warned him. 

High Treason!

And those condemned with her were guilty of High 

Treason! Dom Edward, the holiest monk at the Priory. 

Dom John! These had no truck with royality, save that they held in veneration the good Queen Katharine. 

Father Risby, and Father Rich! They had spoken out their minds freely, true — but - High Treason? 

The knight of Chelsea had not been condemned. 

People were saying that he had denounced the nun as a "lewd housewife." He had satisfied his questioners on that point. Tom's heart sank. How had they contrived to get Sir Thomas More onto their side? He had indeed been opposed to the meddling with the King's matter by the holy folk who had surrounded Bess at Sheen; but of Bess herself he had the highest opinion - save that the good knight was ever watchful for some indiscretion in one of the fair sex. And Bess was sometimes in a hurry. If she had only listened to Sir Thomas!

At St. Sepulchre's the news was received with consternation. Dame Philippa assembled her family and

bade them pray harder than ever for there yet might be a chance of reprieve.

Elizabeth Barton had been condemned to death on the Feast of St. Gregory the Great, the Apostle of England, her followers with her. There was a growing feeling that it was the monks and friars who were the objects of "the royal displeasure" rather than the Holy Maid. They, too, had received sentence on the Feast of 

England's Apostle. A dark cloud was gathering over the 

Religious Houses for their heads had shown themselves opposed to the Royal Supremacy which was a new doctrine that the King was forcing upon the Church. 

There was no knowing what the future might bring forth, with the Crown served by a Primate of whom rumor said that he had married a wife during a sojourn overseas and was only waiting to bring her to his Palace. 

The storm of which the Franciscan friars had seen the clouds gathering seemed to be about to burst over England, haply to scourge those worldly men who had made their lives too comfortable, whose vices had been flayed by the tongue of the Holy Maid, along with those of the heretics. The breath of the winnowing fan was already blowing on the men who would betray the sanctity of Holy Church by soft living and deceitful speech. What would it leave behind? Thomas More and John Fisher? Where else would fortitude be found in high places when Friar Risby and Hugh Rich had been driven from the world? There was Friar Forrest, the good Queen's confessor; and others of his cloth were outspoken. The Prior of the Charterhouse of London, John Houghton, was likewise a man who put God and His Holy Church first. But the times were bad desperately bad.

So far there had been no communication between Elizabeth Barton and her religious family. A notification was received, however, a day or two after the direful news reached them that the Lady Prioress's representative would be permitted to interview the prisoner in the Tower and take from her any messages that she wished to send to her friends. No one knew by whom this concession had been obtained, but it was eagerly taken advantage of by Dame Philippa. Tom Cobb was entrusted with the errand. Who else could undertake it so well?

There was a wild beating in Tom's heart at the thought of seeing Bess yet once again. How would he find her? What change would have taken place in the victim of the subtleties of the King's prelates? On the scaffold at Paul's Cross and at Canterbury she had appeared to him as grown older - set at a greater distance from the Bess of Cobb's Hall who was ever to him the only real and actual Bess.

There was still the faint hope of a reprieve to save him from the full horror of the situation. As he rode down Thames Street and sighted the Tower his heart sickened again. Those cruel gray walls were so loath to yield up the victims within them. Would they have removed the condemned prisoner from the cynical "bower" into the hard heart of the fortress? He dared not ask himself any questions.

Tom's pass gained him entrance through the gateway known as the Byward Tower. Within the precincts the domestic life of the great fortress buzzed and hummed. 

The wives and children of the warders and yeomen of the Guard were about the place. A cold March wind blew across Tower Green, the place where they cut off

people's heads. A couple of ravens, very aged, with broken wings, stalked about the green, a source of pastime to the occupants of the Beauchamp Tower, the State prison, which looked down upon it. The King's quarters, known as the Royal Palace, were attached to the Keep on the east side. Somewhere about there would be the "bower" in which the nun had been detained, receiving the treatment of a lady of high degree up to the time that the Star Chamber had sprung the charge of treason upon her. Tom's blood boiled as he realized that, previous to that Dame Elizabeth Barton had been kept under the impression that it was an ecclesiastical commission that was studying the supernatural problem of her favors from Heaven. All those through whom Heaven worked wonders for the good of His Church were subject to such treatment from the theologians. It was right that it should be so. Had 

Bess been able to see through it?

The present abode of the "Hypocrite Nun" was not in the "Nun's Bower"; but on the other hand, she had not been consigned to the dungeons. Her lodging proved to be a fair-sized apartment in one of the round towers on the inner wall. A warder led Tom up the twisting stairs. 

His heart was benumbed. What was he going to see?

The warder turned his key in the lock and Tom found himself in an octagonal chamber, the walls of which were hung with draperies. What might be behind the draperies? Most probably there was a passage running round in the thickness of the walls lending them ears. It was a grim, an uncanny prison house. 

The furniture consisted of a bed, a table, and a stool. 

On the latter the occupant of the cell was seated, or had

been, for she rose to her feet as the visitor was introduced.

Tom gulped down his emotion with one deep breath of thanksgiving. Yes, it was Bess all right!

Chapter Twenty-Four 

YES, it was Bess. She was no longer the nun. She was wearing a rough gown of brown homespun, and no veil. The past months had reproduced the Bess Barton who had been sick nigh unto death. Who had been carried to our Lady's chapel to be cured. Who had promised to dance with Tom on the green had she been restored to health. Ah, if our Lady had but said no on that second occasion as well! "Oh, Bess!" Tom cried, the old name slipping out, as he took her hands in his. "Oh, Bess, how be it with 'ee?" "I be very well, Tom," was the answer. It was belied by the worn and pale face. “And how be my Mother Prioress?"

The warder who had conducted Tom thither had left them alone, locking the door on them. Tom was surprised. He had been prepared for a witness to the interview. But who could say that they were alone in this diabolical place? "I "And all my sisters?" Bess was inquiring anxiously. pray that no ill hath befallen them?"

Tom made haste to reassure her. No harm had befallen the community at St. Sepulchre's. "Oh, Tom," the other said, "how I have been fearing for them. I have brought evil on so many that deserved well of me." She lowered her voice - "and that by my own fault." 204

The tones were lowered not from fear of listening ears, but in sadness and it would almost seem, shame. 

Tom's heart stood still. What was Bess going to say next? ""Twas no fault of thine, Bess," he muttered, falteringly. "Aye, but it was." He marked the agony in her eyes.

Those eyes that were sometimes so strange, yet always so candid. She fixed them sorrowfully on him. "Tom," she cried, "I have not only been the cause of my own undoing but of the death of all that suffer with me." 

Tom raised a hand to restrain her. Who could doubt but that the walls had ears? She went on, heedless of the gesture: "And yet I was not so greatly to blame for these were learned men and they knew that I was but a poor wench without learning, and they might have perceived that the things done by me were not of their sort but of other kind, and that they might well have been feigned."

Tom made another attempt to check her. He knew well enough what she was meaning. Her knowledge came to her direct, not like that of learned men who arrive at theirs by reasoning, it would be subject to the devil's deceits. Bess had put it in her untutored way. "But they upheld me in all I said," she went on, "because those things were profitable to good living, and so I fell into being puffed up, and I thought I could do of myself that which I judged to be no phantasy, which thing hath brought me to this case." 

She bent her head, and the tears rolled down her cheeks.

"Bess, Bess," Tom cried, "'ee be blaming thyself overmuch. If a strange angel, such as Giles talks about, did speak to 'ee 'twas no fault of thine." 

She shook her head. "It was no strange angel, Tom," she said. "It was myself. I did greatly wish to warn the King of his danger, and I held myself told to do it. I held it to be no phantasy, but it was of my own will. I did greatly wish to warn the King."

She had clasped her hands and was gazing through the slit that admitted the scanty daylight. The tears slowly coursed down her cheeks. "And now I am the cause of the death of all these holy men," she ended. ""Twas a great deed that," Tom whispered - "that you should have dared to warn the King." Then he said it again, out loud, "it was a great deed."

A great exultation had gripped the soul of Tom Cobb. Bess was seated there with a look on her face that he had not seen before. She was disclaiming his words. She had humbled herself to the dust. Bess had always been humble, but now something seemed to have been added to what had been perfection. They had brought her here to ruin her soul by putting pride into her heart, and instead her humility had received its crown. They had practiced against themselves. Tom knew in the marrow of his bones that the Bess who was there before him, albeit that she was wearing no weeds of religion, had achieved the sainthood of the saints. 

They talked very simply after that - about St. 

Sepulchre's. 

"Give them all my love," Bess said, "and ask their pardon and their prayers. I would fain that Sister Marjorie had the mantle and the kirtle that I left there;

and Sister Paula my mattress and sheets. And give my love to the dapple gray." 

Her voice faltered. The tears were pouring down

Tom's cheeks. He thrust his knuckles into his eyes, as he had done when he was a small boy at Cobb's Farm. 

Bess had always had comfort for him. She was speaking now. Dame Elizabeth Barton was standing there. The hands which Tom would fain have knelt down to kiss were thrust into the sleeves of her rough garment, nunwise. "We shall be merry in Heaven, Tom," she said. "Pray for me!”

They heard the sound of the warder turning the key in the lock. "Good-by, Tom," Bess said. "Remember my messages to my Mother and my sisters."

Tom was groping his way down the dark staircase. 

His tears blinded him rather than the darkness. He stumbled. Never mind. In Heaven he would be dancing with all the merry folk - with Bess. 

At the head of the staircase a figure had slipped through a concealed opening in the wall. Mr. Cromwell's agent had succeeded beyond his expectations. He had collected the substance of a "confession."

The making of confessions is part of a politician's business, but a spot of truth is all to the good in making an alleged confession carry conviction when put into circulation.

The wench Anne Boleyn, who was officially known as the Queen of England, had no great liking for the royal lodging in the Tower. The King on occasions took up his residence there when there was State busi-

ness to be attended to and on those occasions his new 

Queen accompanied him. Anne Boleyn preferred to keep her eye on her royal consort. His own was a roving one and the Seymour woman was as like to be round about the Palace in the Tower as anywhere else - a little pale-faced chit, the very opposite of herself. 

It was a year and three months now since her marriage with King Henry and Anne was fighting a doubt as to whether she still retained her ascendancy over the man whom she had turned round on her little finger. The menace of the people's disapproval had been more or less disposed of by the action against their champion, the Hypocrite Nun. Elizabeth Barton lay in a prison opposite her own window awaiting the hurdle and the hangman. Revenge was close upon the heels of the 

Holy Maid. But the people's anger might blaze up any moment.

Anne stood at her casement gazing across the green that had its red stains and thought hard. Might it not be an effective gesture on the part of the new Queen to plead for the life of the nun? A crooked smile flickered across her face. Elizabeth Barton was out to be a popular martyr. Suppose instead her life were spared and she were sent to be a body servant to Anne's mother, the Countess of Wiltshire?

The smile hardened on the new Queen's face. She was still tasting the sweetness of revenge for all that she was playing a magnanimous part toward the woman who had all but frustrated her alliance with the King. 

Magnanimity is always picturesque; and revenge is always sweet.

The program for the morrow had been completed. 

The nun's punishment, it had been decided, was to be

simply hanging and heading. The more barbarous one of quartering would be reserved for her accomplices. 

It were not indeed strictly correct to call them that for the five churchmen were intended to be the central objects in this demonstration of the King's royal might. 

The Hypocrite Nun was, in short, to be regarded merely as the tool of the wily priests who had previously figured as her dupes. Elizabeth Barton was to remain in the background. Indeed there had been some talk of turning her off quietly at St. Thomas a Watering, but Tyburn had been decided upon. The two 

Franciscan friars, and the two Benedictine monks, with the scholarly secular priest, Dr. Henry Gold, were to be made examples of what might befall such churchmen as dared to defy the King. An offer of pardon had indeed been made to them if they were willing to acknowledge the King as Head of the Church. Such would have been a triumph for the Royal cause. Henry had more use for apostates than for martyrs. In any case Tyburn would be an interesting spot on the morrow.

The King had arranged it all with his ministers. He had, as a matter of fact, just disposed of the latter when it was intimated that the Queen wished to speak with him. Anne Boleyn no longer took delight in intruding herself at unseasonable times. The permission was accorded and she presented herself. She was wearing her most demure and deferential air. It was one of the new 

Queen's accomplishments to be able to draw upon a variety of personalities. There were a number of Anne

Boleyns whom she donned and doffed like garments. 

The present one was the wifely wife who realized that her husband was also a monarch. "I am come to ask a favor, My Lord," Anne said. She

peeped up demurely as she pursed up her lips, making her mouth small and round. "Tell it." The King's tone was not too gracious. "It concerns the Hypocrite Nun," Anne said. 

The King shot a swift, rather curious glance at her. "Well?" "It beseems me," Anne said, "that it were a terrible thing for a woman of her former repute to be hanged on the gallows." "What would you do with her then?" Henry asked. 

Anne replied. "I would have you give her to my mother for a body servant."

There was a long silence. The King was surveying the woman before him. He was thinking of another woman, a peasant, who had stood before him asking no favor. He was comparing the two in his mind. 

Anne saw his face harden. Then he smiled; but it was an ugly smile. "The mad nun," he said, "has deserved all that may befall her, but the gallows is revenge enough for me." 

The other had turned a fiery red — then she became deadly pale. The sword had fallen. The sword that had hung over her head for these past weeks. The King had withstood her. Her power was gone - and to whom?

She accepted the covert insult. She bowed her head meekly. Her last and only chance lay in meekness, the

King's tastes had warped that way. The model wife whose husband's wish was her own made a curtsy to her lord. "I will ask it no more, Sir," she said. “Forget that I had the temerity to do so. Why should my poor request change the royal pleasure?"

The King looked after her as she retired from his presence. 

Anne with her tongue in her cheek and her bold eyes belying her words had been rather fascinating at one time. But one can have too much of that kind of thing. 

State affairs are apt to make a man out of conceit with things in general. 

Anne Boleyn returned to her own apartment. Once more she gazed out of the window that looked upon Tower Green. Adjacent to the palace was the building containing the "Nun's Bower." Opposite to it, on the wall above the moat, was the round tower used for prisoners. Elizabeth Barton would be in the guarded room behind the narrow slit. Tomorrow she would be no more. They would cut off her head, but not on 

Tower Green. Tower Green was reserved for persons of high degree. A shudder ran through Anne Boleyn.

She could not guess why. There were idle prophecies about that Anne Boleyn would be burnt alive, but no one had indicated the small mercy of Tower Green. 

The Tower was a horrible, horrible place. The new

Queen wished that she had not followed the King thither.

Chapter Twenty-Five 

THERE was no small hubbub in the precincts of the Tower, and in the streets round about. Never before in the history of the City of London had there been a spectacle of holy monks and friars - four of them and a priest that was no heretic - being borne to Tyburn to undergo the hideous fate of the traitor. 

An immense crowd lined the highway on Tower 

Hill. A whisper had gone round that the Hypocrite

Nun would not be with the others that she was to be turned off over at St. Thomas a Watering, but that plan was not to be carried out. It was intended that the five churchmen should be the central figures. The nun had but been their tool, so it now appeared, in their plotting against the King. But the mob was awaiting a sight of Elizabeth Barton who was still to them "the Holy Maid." It was the grand folk who had eaten their words and renamed her the Hypocrite Nun. 

Within the Tower wall a crowd waited, formed of the wives and children of the warders, and other favored folk who had gained an entrance through the good offices of the members of the garrison. Amongst these there stood a young man in rough country garb. 

By his side was another, an even rougher looking fellow. He was pointed out as the tinker who had done penance at Canterbury. He represented the shattered glories of Court-up-Street.

Tom Cobb took little heed of the eyes round about 212

him. All he knew was that in a moment they would be bringing out Bess.

They had been merciful to Bess. They were going to let her hang until she was dead ere they struck off her head. Her sledge was waiting for her.

They had brought her out. She came forward in charge of a couple of warders. She was wearing the same rough gown that had given back Bess of Cobb's Hall to Tom. The bystanders craned their necks for a better view of the nun. She was walking firmly, with eyes cast down. There was no fear on her countenance, but a profound agony had impressed itself there. 

She stood waiting patiently for the others to be brought out for her confederates. They had gone to fetch them from the dungeons. In a few minutes they appeared. 

Strange, bowed figures, they moved slowly between their guards. Long confinement and savage treatment had reduced them to mere shadows of the men who had passed into the Tower many months back. 

Elizabeth Barton shot a glance at the faces. She scanned them and the agony deepened on her own. 

The two monks came first. Dom Edward Bocking and Dom John Dering. Grave, recollected, serene.

They were followed by the friars. Father Risby and 

Father Rich. Tom Cobb pressed forward to get a view of the latter. Hugh Rich was smiling - at Sister Death. 

Master Henry Gold came last. He represented the secular clergy. There was a new dignity about the parson of Aldermary. He had stepped out of his book world and found a real one in which deeds took the place of things preached. His congregation was an alert one on this occasion.

The procession halted at the side of the row of hurdles. Suddenly Father Hugh Rich stepped a little forward. He lifted his hand. He was evidently about to address the bystanders. A hush fell on the assembly. "Friends," he cried, "I would have you know that this morning the King's Grace hath offered pardon to those whom you see here if so be they will take the oath acknowledging him to be the Head of God's Church in this Kingdom of England." 

He paused. Then he cried out. ""Tis for our faithfulness to God's Church and for no other crime that we die." 

A guard stepped forward and silenced the speaker. 

But he had had his say. Moreover, attention had suddenly been diverted to the nun. 

Dame Elizabeth Barton was indeed well worth looking at. Her face had become transformed - transfigured. The sorrow had left it and in its place there shone an ecstasy of joy. The tears of joy were pouring down her cheeks. She was speaking.

A dead silence was accorded to the Holy Maid, and her words rang out. "Ah! God hath indeed been merciful to me for I have not compassed the death of these holy men. 'Tis for God's Holy Church that they die. He hath given to them the crown of martyrdom."

She was gazing fixedly before her. She gazed past the little group waiting to be bound to the hurdles. "I can see more," she cried. "There be scores and scores of them! They be coming to die for Holy 

Church. They have palms in their hands!" The note of triumph rang out. She swayed. The guards stepped forward and caught her as she fell.

They laid her on the sledge, stiff and rigid as death.

Father Hugh Rich turned to his companion. "She gone to visit Heaven,” he said. "Pray God in His mercy that she returns not ere they send her to Heaven for aye.'

Londoners had had a full day. In the morning the "Hypocrite Nun" and her followers had been executed at Tyburn. In the afternoon the citizens had gone to the Guildhall to swear fealty to the King and his legitimate issue by the Queen's grace, now and hereafter to come. Now they were flocking over London Bridge, for there London City had set up a relic of the people's saint on a high and fearful shrine.

There was more than the usual silence reigning in the cloister at St. Sepulchre's Priory. There are varieties of silence. Monastic silence has a balm for the soul. 

This was the silence of Death.

The community was awaiting news from London. 

Tom Cobb would be bringing it. Even now they cherished the fond hope of a reprieve. 

Sister Alys the portress had just finished the fourth mystery of her Rosary when there was a terrific peal at the doorbell. She had heard no approaching footsteps, although her ears were acutely alert for the sound.

She sprang up, and with her heart beating its hardest hastened to the door.

But there was no one at the door. The peal had sounded through the house. The community came running from all quarters. Dame Philippa was at Sister

Alys's elbow. She peered out and along the path. There was no one in sight.

They stood looking at one another. Then the silence was broken for a second time. The chapel bell had begun to toll. The Sisters listened to the sad note of the passing bell that tells of a soul gone forth from this world. 

Dame Philippa bowed her head. "Let us go to the chapel and pray," she said. "Your sister is with God.”

They obeyed her faltering tones. The sound of soft weeping accompanied the sad procession. Then they heard the voice of Sister Marjorie. She was speaking to them all, not merely to her Mother. "But why should we weep for Sister Bess?" she cried, "or for what has befallen her? It were not for nothing that she has died, for did they not say that she did marvelously hinder the going forward of the King's marriage?"
