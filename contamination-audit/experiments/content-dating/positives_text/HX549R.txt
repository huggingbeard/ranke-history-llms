ITHOUT disturbing Earnest, her young and magisterial brother, Myra silently emerges to the long, half-hidden porch that guards the farthermost view of the Great Valley. She is careful not to rouse her blind mother from her musings or to startle Abigail, her impavid sister. Myra notes that the giant beacon of flame is hastening again to his march behind the ranges of hills, that they now begin to arrange the patterns of concealment, that the volatile mists whose eyes become luminous only in darkness now dare to appear and to prepare their pageants, reminiscent of cool ages of despair.

It is the hour when the cry of disconsolation is heard, when the warnings of the distant gorge become audible as it mumbles in the sonancy of forgotten wrongs. Only the easternmost side of that valley, bounding its universe with vertical façades, continues to glow as with remembered plumages of gold and scarlet. Its tumultuous walls, where eagles descend, where historic avalanches have crushed their rocky buckles, now reverberate with the scowls of misgiving and farewell. Myra recalls the images of subsequent evenings. Receives a pang that 3

these ancestral walls may further crumble. Foresees, in the Great Valley, the coming of tumult. Envies the smug stone its lichen of content. 

Even now the petulant Abigail has been driven to the terrace by an inner darkness that pinches at the soft parts of her eyes. She views Myra brooding, and says, “I succeeded in breaking two of your statues.”

Myra knows that this is the lie of arrogance that takes many forms, that turns its view against useless recreation, that suffers a pain upon the sender equal to that which it gives. Is her answer nonresistant, or is it a bold and almost whimsical subterfuge: "Abigail, you may break them all. They are progressive rehearsals toward a receding ultimate; at some point a figure will arise and you may not put him down. Self-created, he will speak a language containing no antonym of sentiment." 

But Abigail turns her words to more specific remonstrances. "Over all these halls hangs a mood of liquidity and ravail. Vours and freshets come from heaven as warnings that our refuge is tottering, that we are to be pitiable to any one who ever sees us. Yet, our brother is not one who can return tattered stones and beams to their places or make a new wall against which we may hide. While you practice stony contortions, overhung by dilapidation-and our old mother presses her eyelids against the velvet prongs of darkness-Earnest is unseeing of all but those old codes, left us as heritage with our ruins by our father! Long winter is not yet past. His lean farewell has not yet been heard and some resolute pang of spring may whip its storms against our walls. If they sway and fall, shall we seem audacious in our distress and creep like furry claws along the trees, or go to live with snails and black tongues in caves? Oh, although we have never seen their eyes, there must

be beings no wilder than we who, although they could not speak to us, would permit us to seek them and to share their straw. I dream of people like us, dancing on level fields, fresh houses that can be fastened against storms, towers filled with cool water and friends with glances in their eyes. Earnest says the universe is large. 

I prefer that we try to cross it, even to die on its sharp edge, than to wait and pray for our ages and our walls to fall upon us.

Her eyes became troubled with tears. 

"You go forth, Abigail," says Myra. "Let every one in the universe who is not, like our mother, unseeing follow its call. I cannot wish to go. Earnest and I are sedants." 

Myra sustains her vigil, watching each faint suffocation spread its veil toward the lowlands, hearing the palsied cry of vale calves, lighting with her glance the serried and now sternly tinctured mountain walls. But, before there is time for her to finish speaking, she forgets to restrain a cry. The great visage floats from the side of the mountain. Its moment of life has come. Only its profile is turned which is why what it seems to speak cannot be learned. "Doesn't it suffocate you?" Myra cries and Abigail answers. "I will not look again and be accused of misbelief." "It comes always at twilight," says Myra, "creating itself suddenly as my marble does with such penitence of miraculous gropings. Knowing how you deny, I always await its challenge to my longings. It will come without my awaiting it, but not to me. Great 

Stone Face, Earnest is seeking you from his high window. Your partially averted gaze is a prophecy of turgid moments when roofs may fall but no one will heed them." 5

And now it almost seems for an instant that Abigail, too, has felt the presence in the disembodied lineaments of the mountain, but with a shudder, covering her eyes with a skein of Mashawn, she is seen to hurry toward the broken portal, to enter by the vines into an equal or greater darkness within. She is heard singing an odd lament, often used by old prawns to charm diseases, to divert the angry tongues of lightning or to charm newborns who are averse to their virginal hold of the appendage.

Myra continues alone upon the balustrade, seeking still to perceive the Great Stone Face although he has been mercifully obscured by the fringe of twilight.

Slowly she turns in a rapture to scale each faintly visible peak with her glances, to descend to the old stony passageway along which rises the memory of ancient flowers. So light is the touch of her step that the echoes are not heard. She recalls the high path of the moon among the spruces. Howlands call. Stars swing their hinges. A faint spray of reckoning-unlike fear but related to it-causes her to reconsider her wish to scale Highwick and to view the wrinkled forest as it breaks and descends toward the plain. 

While Myra remains to converse with the unseen, to which in some unnamed way she seems related, Abigail finds her way among the mute stumbles-assembled in her wary path by cavernness-and stands, at first, then sits in the presence of the mother. After the usual reserve, she resumes to complain and, in her own way, to bewail. From the silence of the youthful seer overhead, from Myra's exchanges and excursions outside, the luminous murmur of their voices cannot be heard. "I know the intention of your thoughts," says the mother. "But have you noticed that Earnest rarely ever

speaks? That is not, as you may assume, because he is mad and I have never felt that the children's fantasy that a great face appears in the mountain is harmful to them. If our house were strong enough and our lives sufficient for you, we might suffer from no distemper other than that of death and exist here as patiently as any remnant nearing its close. You do not feel the uses of isolation to which the children are so readily adapted. 

You rebel against it, for by nature you require a theatre against which the acts of our lives may be visible. That is your rational cry as you seek to form each syllable of dissent and perhaps in my troubled wisdom I can point out to you that Myra's carvings are symbols and their relations to a world in which, if we wish, we may share. But that will never cause you to lay your marching mood aside. Lately, I have searched with particular care to remember the opinions your father held of other and more numerous settlements. His premise was much like that used in assuring ourselves that resemblance is capable of repetition. His views, together with the exact locations of the colonies, belong to Earnest, although the manuscripts are filled with annotations, growths of his ideas. "My secret plan is this-to use Myra as our furtive instrument in view of the ready access permitted her by your brother to view his treasures, to examine them and to assist in their emendation. If I can excite her by some kind of suggestion to look sharply among the old pages, she will share with us any discovery of a neighboring people. Then, if your spirit weakens, if you do not dare to walk alone into foreign presences, you and I will become two solemn messengers to the tribes, proclaiming with signs that we have come to join them. 

We can leave the children behind to perfect their uni-

verse of design, in which at present they seem to need little assistance. "There is one thought that should be held firmly in reserve. We have been able to assume that your father's conclusions were adequate because we have never needed to test them. While we can continue to respect them in that sense, in principle, we must not be disheartened if we find that his actual locations are inaccurate. You will note that I am not a visionary when practice is required-for if we journeyed long to a promised land and found it populated only with wild antlers your sorrow at the loss of discernment would be too great for you to bear. Immaterial woes would countercap your present ones and you would stagger under a load of innocuity. We must prepare to extend our observations, limiting them only by the likelihood of encounters—until at last we have opened up a visible disc into the center of some group which, as you know, may be related to us. But it is the long impatience and its thorns that hurt you. Your grief is so sharp that it pierces me with it wounds. 

As the old woman swoons, Abigail is stricken with chagrin to have overtaxed her with concentered grains. 

She gathers the old web into her arms, carries her to her couch of stones, bathes her temples from the pitchercooled through its pores brings a pith of loose linen to absorb the tears, arranges the unseeing forehead upon its pillow, feels her own grief quicken in her throat and in the sockets of her eyes. "Were it better to fall namelessly from the great cliff?” Abigail reviews each plane of destruction but slips into a lassitude of cruel sobs. "Even young dawn, when it comes, will be feeble. It totters slowly." 

As the tears of Abigail bring their sharp aura to the

crumbled palace, Myra circles the ruin, as she is wont to do since childhood, mounts to the minor post of Rheinalt from which the last day tints may be seen, excepting when rain or low-descending skeins blur the distant reflective walls.

Until the light rises and is superimposed, the phantoms of dream and of longing continue to perplex him by assuming the natures and forms of being. "It is then, when the darkness is secure, that I see him, by the mountain. First the multitude, groping, confused, weeping and dying. Then he, the Great Stone

Face, seems to speak. Instantly a murmur of silence runs among them. All are intent to hear. All are ready to obey, yet that negligible troupe dissents-unamenable to the major will. It is the confusion of dissonance that tortures me through those hours. I turn again to the volumes of my father, treasured now in memory. They, too, are lost in the discord. Oh, welcome light, you recreate the familiar semblances. . 

Earnest, leaping toward the light, plucks at his tall forehead, watches for the apparition to appear, feels in IO

his throat the painful needle of recognition. He searches with his sharp glances every distant lineament and sways exhausted as it retreats into the shadows of maturer day. Then he becomes firm; his eyes glow again as he views the synthesis of parchment. He enumerates each syllogistic whorl ending in a quaternion of indifference, calmly now moves through the cathedral of books, glances for an instant at the skyline of mountains, traverses the plazas of ancient marble, rings the gaunt bell as is his custom until its dome speaks the vowels of deprecation and of dawn.

He passes as in a trance to the upper portico but does not view the illumined world, fearing that it will rouse him from the contemplation of theistic forms. He reads from memory a doctrinal platitude and renders it in the leafy dialect of the Valley, dwelling upon each syllable of refutation. 

As Elgar, the faithful, brews the morning herbs, arranges the garlands of breakfast ferns and brings the communal bowl of wild figs and curds, the three women march to their places.

The blind wither, traveling with a staff, is now seated at the slab of ancestral onyx, the girls remain standing until they receive the nod from their brother, whom they view divergently. He greets them with the gentle resonance of a saint, reflecting himself in these moments in their thoughts. Abigail's eyes are fresh from the cooling showers of tears that fell in the night, a daring light plays in Myra's glances. Earnest feels these playful elements and leans toward them but they elude him. A puckering solemnity gathers in their stead as the mother asks her usual questions and answers them: "What are those animals who spoke last night? Three wolves, and I know not how many foxes. II

"How would the sheep scamper to their feet if

"What is the meaning of my recurrent dream, that the corner stone was uprooted and rolled into the giant crevasse, whereupon a new peak appeared? The first part refers to your father's death. I do not yet know the meaning of the second. "Why is Abigail cruel to all of us? Because in some way she must find her freedom. "Why do we, all of us, continue to live? Because today is reminiscent of tomorrow?"

Elgar has found slender green stalks of edible lheum and all welcome it. As a special provocative, small briar fishes have been turned in mullein oil and placed on tablets of hornbeam, surrounded by bits of the eggs of a fern hen-who, divorced and whimsical, sings and lays every extra glade day. 

Elgar has prolonged the fern hen's stay with wildoaten cakes and lime gibs, lined her nest with corshair and sung to her as pacification when the full moon awakened its tumult. On such occasions all of the glens ring and many happy throats are timorous with appreciation. Elgar commands the granaries of nature for the subsistence of these various creatures. Trees ripen their berries for her; in turn she places their seeds where the sun may greet them with his gleams.

To seven concealed tree caverns, on an occasion, Elgar invited societies of bees, having cleverly webbed and conisized the fragrant interiors. The arrogant mother bee alighted upon Elgar's bare shoulder, the obedient clusters followed and all found her signals friendly so 12

that at her direction they prepared the comb and brought to it the savors of every dale and darrow. 

Elgar delights in these neighbors and with her blandishments entices the mountain nimbles to give her of their milk for her charges. She chose Myra to show to them who in turn brought them herbage and who ran among them so fearlessly that they followed her with their soft eyes, nibbling as they did so the velvety edibles she had brought them.

An unspoken language has grown up between Elgar and Myra and the wild wives of the mountain. With their ears, they brush their husbands to abeyance. Aboriginal doves give them their eggs. Avosards practice their songs for them. On such days Myra is forced to return so that she may not be missed by her whose ears are sensitive to absences. Elgar continues on as far as the Pool of Palm. How could a callous hand of flinty hold so much water, so many mosses and such an avalanche of fishes? Here, into this salty cauldron where wild beasts come to slake their tastes, she lowers her nets, and wonders whence the mussels draw their cobalt, envies the sea weeds their iodides, carries home roe wrapped in leaves of mountain fern.

Earnest tends to relax while tasting the delectables brought to him. "Sometime," he thinks, "we shall have our last supper. Then we must be about our missionperhaps to destroy. Until then, let us be children of the palatable approach." It is this moment which the mother chooses to tell them of her plan, created slowly through her era of darkness. "I see more clearly than you," she explains. "Myra, bring me the lowered agate sphere." 13

N THE City of the Valley lived Ruford and his daughter, Judith. When Judith was a babe in lapis robes, her mother was seized during a meteor storm and carried away by a carnivorous prophet. Now, only the retinues of lace makers, toasters of sturgeon eggs, wolf wardens, priests of the great whooping swan, weeping loffools, web-weavers, veterinaries of strings, apothecaries with remedies for wens and wax-cluv palates, dialcasters, avicures, psalmists, men who arrange the grain of whetstones, masseurs of muscles rorn in hunting throws, peccadilloists-only such as these remained in addition to the revered Ruford and his daughter, Judith. Ruford, factually as king, and aided by the council ruled the Valley.

Ruford's ships lay spread like a fan in the delta of the river Remille. Ruford presided over the cargoes that came delegated their disposition to the sons of shepherds who had run away from grazing.

Pursued by the memories of nibbles, these lads ran among the sheds pushing their barrows of amphibian pulp, chemisized kelp, rheindeer fettles, tropical bronze boards for the fabrication of tables, walrus beard from 14

the arctics, plebeian gum for instruments, fang knives for killing ewes, dressed silk for veils and numerous other rich goods welcome to the growing population of the Valley.

After the father walked through the tiled street to his tapestry of things gathered from many lands, Judith rose and released the cotillions of songbirds brought from the myriad islands. Every tree and cloud became a medley of transfiguration. As on holy mornings, Judith touched her throat in wonder. Emboldened by so much sadness, she flew on her stallion across the sand to visit the shepherd caves and to admonish the striplings. She taught them the magic of number while hooded mothers fawned. "After all, why cannot I go to the fallen castle?"

It had become Judith's custom, while Ruford sat through the hours in the plodding council, to ride the spotted stallion swiftly as far as the base of Mount Rheinhalt and from there quickly to climb to the primal plateau and from there to reach the crumbling estate of Earnest's ancestors. In Abigail she found worshipful sighs. In Myra she found a capricious stimulant. 

Earnest pierced her with his eyes. The blind mother touched her hand, asked to be led to a corner and there thumbed Judith's words as cyclostomes of wonder. What another world! Abigail hung upon its fringes and the texture and the fragrance of Judith taxed partially fallen stones and wry rooves. Gradually, as these visits became more frequent, Earnest would enter the Eastern Cavern, where Judith was received. Lately, he asked the question, "What do people talk about in the City?" 

And then, as Judith prepared to leave, Abigail followed to the topmost stone before the descent began. 

Myra traveled through trees, noiselessly drifting down- 15

ward through the branches. Abigail, when the form of 

Judith disappeared, began to weep, only to conceal the sounds of her sobs from the ears of the all-hearing. The blind one touched her forehead to the cool wall and resumed her slow to-and-fro. Myra, from a stark limb hanging sheer above the beginning of meadows, saw Judith fly away on the stallion, knew the secret of the visits, resolved never to disclose what she had seen. 

Earnest sought to fasten his attention upon a new rhomboidal structure. 

Judith found her way here first on a translucent morning many months ago, when first spring was disregarding the phillipics of a cloistered year. Tethering her stallion, somewhat mellowed by the exertions of a swift transient, she discovered another tumult. It was a bay of blossoms. Following the dividuals, she at length surrendered—pursuing subsequence as far as the squills, along to the pisiform minutiae, climbing ever toward the tingling mood.

Why should she be frightened at the sight of a turreted ruin? All serpents, all venomous spiders are known to run from collapsing human walls. Rodents find another way to keep. Who from the city fears a few rustics pirouetting on falling porticoes? Judith pressed far enough along to be welcomed by a cry. She paused. 

Never would Judith forget her first adventurous greeting of the ruins and its people. She would remember the threadbare hunger that yet had a lively tissue below the threadness, catastrophic gleams of welcome disguised as a polite meeting with the unexpected, several disappearing fugitives who ran from Elgar's handkinds of rand doe whom she had pacified for milking, a wisp of protecting cloud that rose now that she was there, the purr of the fern hen's wings who rose in a 16

tumult at the first foreign sound within her generation, a note of grandeur in the sky that at last the Vorhees had been discovered, trepidations in the Pool of Palm where species and their varieties are fearful lest the nets of the valley be brought hither-numerous other portents that dwelt in Judith's remembering. 

Something of the early sadness colored each visit. On the last time, she entered silently, paused beneath the dome of the now unwhispering bell, passed quickly through the plazas of marble and entered the cave of books where Earnest, seeming hardly to breathe, pursued a problem. Earnest rose to greet her but all other meaning was lost as they swayed and were shaken by forces that each had only half explored. As fugitives, they fled to the open sky and were joined by Abigail, rising from a moody trial, Myra just returned from a journey of discovery-and by the blind token. No one acknowledged that a new arrangement had now been made in the patterns of concourse. 17

It is a morning when an unnaturally cool wind shivers through the forests of Rheinalt, which logicians will assign as the cause of what Myra accepted as an apparition. As full day approaches, the rumble of the wind increases, of those steeds that ride through the trees snapping dependent infants from their brown mothers and raising a holocaust of groans and frightened sighs. 

Wings escape before their ruthless enemies, dust that has patiently gathered rises and chokes the air with its yellow particles. The spurt of rain-propelled by blasts -drowses all shrunken leaves, flattens all tumid stems, drenches the boles of myriads of fibrous trees. 

Now that the sky is overhung, now that the ecstasy of life is driven from the paths and ridges and from among the trees, Myra expires in solemn dreams. She noted yesterday the perturbation between her apostolic brother and Judith-curious and accidental messenger from the Valley. With envisagement she perceived their swaying reserves. Now her face is luminous in contemplation. The swirling wraiths of rain are unfolded in the ancient clarity between the forest's shoulders. The whispers of corners. The despondent sighs of trees. 18

Rising, she remembers to explore the vault where her father weighted the old manuscripts between heavier sheathes. Entering, she dreads the presence of now unseeing eyes, unmoving hands-yet with adventurous bearing she touches a leaning tower of the treasured documents. From this long-wanted touch the tower swings, severs at an appointed place and an upper segment descends to rest in dust on the ancient floor. On top of the portion that did not fall lies the dim and wrinkled script which-impelled by an ungovernable wish-Myra now must read: "Know all that in this valley, near it or of it will be born a son destined to bring bewilderment, exordiums, strife, entropy and peace. He will be the most unlikely from the poor hills. But he will ring like a bell, which may make the deaf to rise and hear. The scepticals will flash in threatening whirls. Counterstrokes will fail. The all-seeing heavens will flash their eyes. The Valley will be disposed. When he comes, he shall be the semblance of the 

Great Stone Face, who at morning and evening assumes the direction of storms. Woe to those who fail in recognition!" 

Myra's first wish-a hope so devout that it almost seems to be a doubt-is that Earnest may be the fulfilment of the prophecy. Why has she not seen the likeness before of Earnest's cool, proud features to this limned majesty? It is a revelation to her that she recently has not circled his face with her eyes-that she has not observed the maturing, the fulfilment of a dread prophecy, that she has looked at Him rather than at these resemblances to the Great Stone Face which she and Earnest alone have seen. 

Have the people in the Valley seen the Great Face?

Myra feels ashamed that they have not asked Judith the 19

question now shown to be important to them. When will Judith come again? Shall she speak to Earnest about entering the vault of manuscripts against his commands? 

Shall she tell the Blind what she has found? Abigail would destroy the script. Oh, she is not used to indecision. She will study Earnest's face and make her decision final—yet slowly she knows that she has known the inevitable. Why has she not told it to herself as plainly as it has revealed itself to her? 20

O THE rolling of drums through the Valley, to the waking of a new born day's light in the sky, to the crying of early-born lambs, to the early gathering of feet, the Procession of Planting Corn forms and makes its way toward the great circle of plinths. As ordained, the first glinting ray of the sun enters the aperture of morning and strikes the great shaft at the bottom of which sits Bilke, clutching the burnished knives within his huddled sable robes.

In the van of the procession marches the regiment of little children, carrying substantive relics of last year's harvest. Next, in freshly woven robes of white wool, comes the army of juveniles, bearing on their heads the hollow bowls of grain to be blessed for planting. After these come the young sorrowing sacrificial victims, flanked and followed by the hordes of mourners, each one armed with some implement for tearing the earth for the burial of seeds.

At the approach of the throngs, the choir of trumpets and wailing voices fills the monstrous court with the mirage of grief. Assistant priests rush to the great slaughtering columns, purify them with water blessed 21

by Bilke, return to him for the sacred knives and resume their positions ready for the symbolic massacre. 

When the theatre and amphitheatre are filled, when the multitude is drawn in from all files of approach, when silence is proclaimed by a signal from Bilke as a stroke of the holy gong, then the sacrificial victims are led to the altars and made ready for the pageant. 

Following the mimicry of killing, a concert of sobs shakes the temple, transmitting its quiver of grief to the fields, carrying its echo of sorrow to the farthest hills. The sanctified grain is carried away in all directions and the planting proceeds according to the ancient custom.

Those who have been sacrificed must presume to be dead until another planting. They must live under no roof and eat no food that has been raised by human hands. In a life of death they must hide in the abandoned quarries, old disused mines or infested caves. 

Ruford and Judith, the temporal rulers of the Valley, are the last to leave the sacred precincts, wondering at the mysteries of priestcraft. All day they keep to their altar in the palace, observing the usual rites, that all conditions for a coming bountiful harvest may be fulfilled.

All the people must fast for seven days, keep a painful wake and lie down to sleep on uncovered stones. 

The rules of the priests are explicit and no one would dream of breaking them for fear of inviting famine to visit the land. The hungry may eat at the sound of the gong at sunrise on the eighth day, following a restrained ablution of tears. By such travail-that results in the death of many of the old and the young-may the enemies of the coming harvest be propitiated. On the ninth and tenth days, provided there has been rain 22

since the planting, comes the Feast of Blessing, orgies of wine and meat, and music. The young act as food and wine bearers and the old as musicians, leaving all intermediate ages free to interpret the pageant of desire. 

On this year there is bounteous rain following the planting.

To the palace, near the evening of the eighth day, on camels, mules and on foot come the old members of the council. Comes also this year in accordance with an ancient law the youth of highest birth who may hope before the next festival to be betrothed to Judith. He is disguised as one of the elders and his place at the royal table is to be chosen by lot. Should he draw a place next to either Judith or Ruford he must leave the palace immediately and never show his face there again, but should he draw a place next to Bilke he must withdraw to the Temple and stand waiting until the finish of the long feast when he will be joined instantly to 

Judith in marriage.

By custom, the elders were entrusted with choosing the eligible lad and they now arrange the lots determining every one's position at table-the positions of Ruford, Judith and Bilke being fixed in advance. So skilful is the disguise of the youth, so like an elder would he seem to any neutral person, that only Ruford -used to scanning the faces of the members of the council-is able to detect him.

With restive emphasis, the monarch awaits the drawing of the chances. To heighten the suspense, the youth takes his turns in passing the slab of gold where lie the fatal tablets of choice but he only feigns to draw-feigning so skilfully that the ones following him should not detect it. No one takes his place until all the lots are chosen-then each approaches the banqueting table 23

according to his number. On this occasion, the places next to Bilke and next to the emperor and his daughter remain almost to the last. All notice by counting that the youth is seated at the table. The king observes that he sits one seat removed from Bilke. 

Is that a good or an evil omen? For the two plantings preceding his own marriage, the king's prospective bride had chosen the position next to himself and on the preceding one next to his mother. Thus, the three most eligible brides in his kingdom had been denied to him forever and the fourth had been chosen inexorably -drawing a position next to the priest. She had been weakly, being carried away by an inflamed zealot in the first year following Judith's birth. 

So! Judith is to have what Ruford never enjoyedfree choice, at least so far as the first eligible is concerned. This is an exciting custom. It leaves to fate or to choice the marriage of the future queen of the Valley. It leaves little to the council, for the selection is laid down in the category of families. Only on years when there is no rain following planting may the council depart from tradition. In such a year was Judith's mother chosen and curiously the elders did not then elect either of their three preceding choices. 

The feast is a test for the young eligible. Throughout the dozens of courses he must eat and drink in unison with the elders-and then be able to stand and remove his disguise. There is gaiety and laughter by the time young Faill rises and quite steadily takes off the elder's coats and wigs and bows to the assemblage. Cantors come with warm water and a great mirror to wash the stains of age and wrinkles of wisdom from his face. In the normal clothes and colors of a young man, Faill has 24

to speak a few words from the dictionary of gratiation: "King, Holy Priest, Princess, Elders. The Allseeing has not chosen that I be sent forever from your presence nor-as I had prayed-did He send me to wait in the temple for the dawn of heaven. I shall seek to deserve from Princess Judith the favor which the Allseeing saw fit not to force her to bestow upon me. In such a happy event . . . I am voiceless to proceed." 

Judith recognizes Faill, has seen him at horse tumbling exercises on the Plateau of Mirages. She feels indifferent to him and how she wonders to find herself thinking now of Earnest-actually an alien, one so far beyond the edge of the Valley that he probably does not even observe the Feast of the Planting. What religion did he have? That wonder gives her courage to resolve to go again to Rheinalt, to climb the precarious slope, to risk the danger of discovery by jealous guardsmen, to question Earnest as to what he believes of priests and fasts and festivals. Looking at Faill, she thinks of Earnest, of the blind candle and of Myra the unforgettable. The thought of waiting until the feast days should be over merely adds to her impatience to fly across the plain and rise laboriously to the falling gables. 

Jarveth, the head elder, replies to Faill's speech: “Illustrious scion, we did not choose you; the fated lineages of our Valley chose you from the many attenuated stems. At the last moment before the final upward sweep they saw the group of the faithful, dissolving into pairs with what seemed to be monsters of wild land and buried cave. But Earnest, viewing the unnatural features of the Seven, does not trust them. He gives his message to a passing runner who carries a long thong, as he says, to whip away starving eagles and wild bears. The Seven try to speak in remonstrance but are stilled and join the troop of recent singers as they return by long ways to their starlit homes.

Earnest, the Mother and the Seven are accepted by the faithful—the former placed in beds of honor and the latter shown to the floors of new granaries, now partially covered with fresh-mown grasses and herbs. Autumn is chill and the Seven lie as closely together as may be, in accordance with the laws of warmth. All are troubled by thoughts of the faithful, swarming on an Island with those who may be harmful to them but at last they find a troubled sleep that is doomed to frightened awakening. 

Following the voices of dogs, whose thongs have been lengthened that they may seize intruders, there is the sound of horses and the cries of late night travelers. The

Seven rush into the turmoil and fling the foaming dogs aside as appointed Valley men leap astride the horses to pinion those who are thought to be enemies. But these are the horses of the king, carrying Judith, Avoine, Myra and Elgar! The Valley men are prevented from rending them only by Earnest and the Mother coming out of hiding to recognize and plead for them. It is found that the four persistents have pressed on through the bright challenge of day and the foreboding of darkness to call at every door and to oppose every resistance to their en- 260

quiries. Earnest and the Mother are moved to ask all

Valley people to join them in penance. 

The horses sprawl in the courtyard in quivering collapse following their latest advance, the four riders fall to the ground-limp and senseless-so that the village becomes a camp of resuscitation. Challenged by the grim duties of what was intended to be a catastrophe, all who are skilled in restoration run swiftly to their tasks. The horses are drained and raised to a life-like posture on the pigeon beams. After a fluttering of lifebeats, all are found to have failed to rally. Carried, still upright, to the equine pyre, they are arranged so as to represent the positions natural to them—running elongate on the lowlands; leaping walls, streams and all other hazards; making terrifying descents along the sides of mountains, carrying unexpected burdens, passing through impassable shrub-bush with its lacerations, fleeing to escape wolves and other dangers, following-exultant—in the chase for game, daring the avalanche and the thunderbolt and leaping in white fear at each sudden harmless apparition. Before the fire is applied, the brave remnants are arranged in memory of their notable cruises. The settlers are summoned from afar as witnesses that these faithful members failed only from having extended their exertions too far. The trumpets are sounded and, as all fall forward excepting those intrusted with the rite, the farewells are recorded upon the ledgers of the Valley. 

All rise to wonder if the passengers fare as badly as their carriers.

The watchers withdraw to a hill and cover it with darkness greater even than that which creeps and cools from the lakes of night. Waiting for a signal as to whether further grief is in store for them, they pass beyond the uneven brow of the small black mountain, 261

find charred debris overlooked by the flames in their haste and raise a conflagration to the sky that it may seem to hasten the lagging dawn, sluggish and logged in sleep beyond the mountains. As dawn rises upon the pinnacles, runners come from the bedsides to announce that all may live, although it is thought that Elgar, from her advanced age, may remain forever too weakened to return to Rheinalt, excepting perhaps to be carried there where she may walk gently among the soft-eyed denizens whose glances assure her of their understanding. 

The fever has already so far lifted from Myra, Judith and Avoine that they are able to sit up and to play with spools. Broth is being brought to them and it is noted that they soon will be impatient to be after some other purpose. Myra asks if, following the usual convalescence, she may be permitted to follow the feeble Elgar to the scenes of her sculpture and to the ever-renewable bright images of her childhood. Now that the palace has been restored, she feels that she and the invalid can live there in protection against whatever storms and hunger may arise. Judith and Avoine have decided that they cannot return to the unnatural roof of his father. They are to ask Amitar and Zoll to combine their desires in a home for painting, where students from every part of the Valley may continue to come, from which Rheinalt may be conveniently visited. The new house shall harbor cruising horses and there is to be an adapted wing to welcome the sandmen when they come. There are to be the chambers ideally laid out for painting in the colors of viatude and other chambers mounted on wheels in which the works may be carried to every community and shown to all who would care to see them. There is also to be a free room in which all passing souls who care to may rest. 262

Earnest and the Mother announce that they feel divorced from Rheinalt by the complex of events, particularly by the disappearance of the faithful. They feel that they must seek these throughout the years and, thence, seek to develop the art of petition. They feel entirely clear and changed from their former selves for the reason that self-likeness has been completely restored to them. Earnest's moodiness has disappeared, it seems, through the administration of Ergone. The Mother aims always at the expression of quick energy. 

After gilded waverings, the fulmination of sunrise crosses again the horizon of the world in mountain wall as four figures of men are seen to approach. They signal that they have been traveling all night and that they would ask to know news of such import that flares have been casting their warning wanes. As they hear what has happened, the king and after him Jarveth inquire with solicitude for the welfare of the four riders, remarking that it was they who sent forward the faithful steeds, now reduced to ashy splendour by the commemorating flames. As the presence of the visitors is known, all but 

Elgar are lowered on willing arms to greet them and to be visibly moved that they have followed so far for the reunion. Judith makes known all the plans to Ruford who agrees to them, excepting that roads and a bridge are to built to Rheinalt and that the house of the painters is to be built not too far from the palace. 

Jarveth explains that they have been followed by several souls who, when they heard that all within were expected to live, drifted, as it seemed, idly away. Judith and Avoine glance eagerly into the azure but must be content with hoping to see the souls in some future time.
