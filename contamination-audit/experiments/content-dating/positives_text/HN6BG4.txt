Introduction 

My experience in the immediate transition from high school to college was nothing short of terrific. The change from the Sunday School overthe-counter type of religion which the average college freshman experiences may be taken as significant to an extent in every case. And yet to be thrust rudely into a declamatory arena wherein the impressive exponents of 20th century liberal education were seen to go so far as to debate the existence of the Divine itself was for me a shocking and painful occurrence. Every individual at one time or another arrives at a point where he claims that his "eyes were opened. Such experiences are undoubtedly common to all, and occur relative to any sort of important or trivial circumstance. It was similarly this exposure to the introductory orbits of higher education that, in short, set me to "thinking about things." when a plastic adolescent begins to think about things, results are about as problematical as anything can possibly be. And so it has been from this beginning that I have been progressively led from perplexity to perplexity, with each bit of solid ground attained but an exposure point for Now, some new doubt-stirring and upsetting argument.

Initially, the situation of which I speak might be analagous to a traveling salesman whose car is broken down and who cannot perform his job until it is repaired. The longer he puts off meeting the necessity of repair, so long is he prevented from doing his job. Certainly, had I refused to meet my problem squarely, so long would I have been kept from doing my job. From the outset, therefore, I felt that to a certain degree I could look back upon myself and not be blamed; but from then on I could not look ahead and not afford to take the blame. If the task of personal responsibility weighs heavily upon me now, at that time it loomed as some alien and insurmountable barrier--a philosophical hodge-podge that seemed to descend about me all at once. 

My first recollection of reaction to this scene of two and one-half years ago was a general feeling of intellectual lack. When I say intellectual lack I do not mean inferiority based upon an inability to cope with my classmates in college work. 

Rather I immediately associated knowledge and a philosophy of life as synonymous in my experience and set out. I wish to make this last statement

clear: when I say a "philosophy of life," I refer to an indistinct wonderment which I felt at the time as to why I was put on earth, whether heaven and earth would ever get together after all, and all the other common puzzles that beset the college freshman leaving high school as a man, but soon finding himself but an "infant crying in the night."

On top of this, however, I was made aware that society was in a bad state. It seemed that the common search for the El Dorado formula should take precedence over all things, and so there arose another worry that I took over as a personal responsibility. It was with this concern, therefore, over my deficiency in an intellectual justification of life combined with a burning anxiety over general social decadence that I headed into the major part of my freshman year.

Since this departure a great deal has happened, and my work in college has been characterized primarily by a series of high points, spots upon which I have been able to take conceptual refuge-only to be swept off again and again by the titanic wave of some new confounding premise. My choice in this paper is to dwell chiefly upon the most

significant of these high spots--what might be termed the "Milton stage;" for just as each succeeding stage thus far has been important, so Milton has acted more penetratingly upon me than any authority up to the present.

10 Generally speaking, the first year found me struggling with three central problems: that of the Divine, of human nature, and one a little more removed from these two closely allied questions, that of the contemporary conflict between individual integrity and social morality. The three specific enigmas involved at once every phase of my original dilemna as described above and were essentially an outgrowth of that dilemna.

In reviewing the state of social living today I was consistently drawn to a consideration of the Divine infinitude or finitude, the problem of God's alleged omni-goodness as over against flourishing worldly corruption, and an investigation of that elusive entity, human nature, that forever seemed to defy definition or understanding. ...

The freedom of a free man's choice is a grant of the Creator, and this freedom moves within a range of activities where the will of God must necessarily be a cooperating factor What is bad is due only to God's love in giving man his freedom, therefore all evil comes from man alone. God only tries to right this evil.

Evil is the result of man's abuse of his freedom, for which abuse God is not responsible.

As I look back upon this statement taken from a paper written at the end of my first year I can well see that it is easily the most important

conclusion to which I had arrived, but at the time 

It was

I confess that I did not realize it so. merely another link in a series of arguments which I had gathered from many sources, interpreted and postulated for myself as assurance of the existence and infinitude of the Father. At the time it served but shallowly as a behavior influence, existing only as part of an immature understanding of life. Far more significant then was the following, which I had felt as my greatest personal accomplishment up to the time:

If, through education, this mutual understanding among men with regard to the definition of the means and end may come about, then we shall be ready to set out in operation of these means towards the state which will not signal the beginnings of preparation for its overthrow, but rather will signify the achievement of that condition of life which men have agreed is the best and which will be stationary ... Large scale reform and direction of mass forces toward what we term "Utopia" cannot come about until the definition of the ideal is the same in all men's minds. (I shall refer to this more particularly later.)

The conflict between the ethical criteria, personal integrity and social morality, was probably of more concern to me during this period than any other single question. For it was in the solution of this problem that personal

1 responsibility and social well-being came into a harmonious unity, or separated completely for then and all time. It was a case of which must come first in order to unite the two, and the problem as a whole was so inextricably bound up in the social discord of the day--Fascism, Marxism, capitalism, socialism--that my entire interest was riveted inherently on the subject. 

Social morality, as a creed, must not be regarded as the direct antithesis of personal integrity in that it involves principally the manner in which each man conducts himself with respect to the welfare of society. Rather it is merely that man, while retaining his integrity, directs his actions in a manner which will be conducive to the essential betterment of society. He tends to retain his foresight and long range judgments, and as he practices his integrity he keeps in mind the fact that such is maintained not for his own material gain, but for his spiritual satisfaction, and with the prevailing thought that by acting as he does society as a whole is the beneficiary. Social morality is not an end in itself, but it is nevertheless a goal of personal integrity. Personal integrity is but a means to this higher end for which we are striving... The problem of human nature, however, was more bothersome than any other issue as it operated within the concern over individual integrity and social morality. My sincere belief was that I had resolved the question raised by Max Eastman

in his October, 1939, Harper's article, "Russia and the Socialist Ideal." Here he asked, "Is human nature, as it has developed in the struggle for survival, sufficiently self-dependent and sufficiently cooperative, or sufficiently capable of self-dependence and malleable in a cooperative direction, so that a collectivization of property would actually lead to the society of the free and equal, the dying away of state power, the condition of felicity described in the formula: 'From each according to his abilities, to each according to his needs?'" Naturally this was the most important part for me--it dealt with society and the struggle towards a happy state. Fundamentally

I was sure that Eastman was wrong in assuming that human nature did not possess the inherent qualities, latent up to now, leading to the development of the social soul. My fallacy lay, of course, in the impatient postulating of happiness, ease, and comfort as the be-all and end-all of human striving. 

And yet at the time I saw no fallacy, and my near anxiety for fairly legislating a Utopia into existence sparked the very core of my intellect.

Human nature as such is an outgrowth upon the individual of the melee in which he exists, and is a product of the society which man has created. If we still tend to believe that human nature cannot be changed under any circumstances, we are practically repudiating the entire ethical

basis of Christianity. That is, the phenomenon of conversion is an instance of the human nature taking on the Divine nature, and were Christianity not based upon the assumption that human nature can be molded, it would have been long dead by now. In simpler terms, the answer remains that there are certain seemingly instinctive actions and reactions on the part of men that we have come to term human nature. However, if we expect to get anywhere at all, we must cease to regard them merely as outgrowths in the individual of the general trend and force of social thought and action, and then proceed to correct them if they be in discord with society's final purpose. 

Human nature remains, therefore, an outgrowth of society because it is in accord with human nature to accept the dominance of society. "It is entirely in accordance with human nature that ordinary man should save himself the labour of thought by accepting traditional beliefs. 

It is entirely in accordance with human nature that ordinary men should be deceived into acceptance of an evil by its slow but relentless growth from something less evil." (A. A. Milne--Peace With Honour)

In other words it is human nature that makes men accept these beliefs, but it is not necessarily the beliefs themselves which are due to human nature. 

While I have no right to assume my "evolution" up to date as an inevitably solid block-upon-block process, I cannot help but sense a train of direction throughout the first two years which in several respects precursed the "Milton stage." In the analysis of personal integrity and human nature unknowingly I hit upon one of the vital pillars of Miltonic thought in recognizing the necessity and importance of the individual choice in human

Admittedly conduct, the necessity of truth unto one's self as the basis of the only full life. the conception of human nature as essentially a social thing in all its aspects instead of fundamentally an individual endowment differed from my feeling now.

However in so conceiving human nature as mutated in certain instances and realizing that individuals must take firm footing and take on another nature in order to live in any sense, I know that I presaged my present position with 

Milton. As far as heralding Milton goes, however the passage written during the first year and already quoted, regarding freedom of the free man's choice and the exoneration of God as responsible for worldly evil I now recognize as my chiefest claim in this respect. Would that I had not been so ignorant of the compass of the statement as I wrote it! For as I blithely solved my problem by making evil the result of man's abuse of his freedom, I could not see anything except what I saw in other men, in society. After Milton, and as I look back, I can look at myself, and my horror is starkly dilated.

In the wake of this struggle I headed into the second year. I had a feeling at the start of

indefiniteness, and severely criticized myself for spending the first year dealing with what then seemed abstractions. 

I was sworn to the definite for once and for all. And yet, in so doing, I still but oscillated in a world of mute generalities-that is, until the encounter with William James. 

The principle of pragmatism is that in order to attain perfect clearness in our thoughts on an object, we need only consider what conceivable effects of a practical kind the object may involve-what sensations we are to expect from it, and what reactions we must prepare. Our conception of these effects, whether immediate or remote, is then for us the whole of our conception of the object, so far as that conception has positive significance at all. There can be no difference anywhere that doesn't make a difference elsewhere--no difference in abstract truth that doesn't express itself in a difference in concrete fact and in conduct consequent upon that fact, imposed upon somebody, somehow, somewhere, and somewhen. The whole function of philosophy ought to be to find out what definite instants of our life, if this world-formula or that world-formula be the true one. If there be any life that is really better for us to believe or any life that is really better we should lead, and if there be any idea which, if believed in, would help us to lead that life, then it would really be better for us to believe in that idea, unless, indeed, belief in it incidentally clashed with other greater vital benefits. Ought we never to believe what it is better for us to believe? And can we then keep the notion of what is better for us, and what is true for us permanently apart?

Pragmatism says no. In other words, the greatest enemy of any one of our truths may be the rest of our truths. (From William James, Pragmatism) 

Such a thing as Pragmatism, I suppose, must be taken as the sort of thing that every one comes up against at one time or another. But it cannot be dismissed in those terms. To begin with, in spite of my own criticism of my former metaphysical abstractness, I had felt that the first year was productive. Therefore, as I spluttered and fought to see amid a pragmatic deluge, I wailed my predicament considerably, and for the first time felt solidly defeated. Such a feeling is not actually bad, however, for it is infinitely better to know one has nothing than not to know what one has or has not. Knowing that I had so much to do, therefore, fortified and strengthened me far more than had I drifted mutely and haltingly along the nowhesitant, now-rushing wave of disturbing thoughts. 

I shall not take space or time to outline this particular experience, for it has actually no especial bearing upon pre-Milton development, and the fearsomeness of Pragmatism itself gradually dispersed itself in the reconvincing effect of my earlier decisions.

I desire not to defend myself against any principles, code, or criteria now. I want only to define myself and what I stand upon, so that I can more adequately live.

If an individual possessive of a keenly perceptive mind can formulate a set of behavior patterns which to all appearances are indicative of making for the best interests and progress of society, and then cannot adhere to his own laws, does this minor fact detract from the essential value of his outlined code?

For that matter we can say that the person who governs his everyday conduct according to right moral and gentlemanly standards, regardless of whether he postulated those standards or took them unto himself otherwise, is turning a far more salient good than the individual who merely perceives and lays down the rules for better living, and carries his actions no farther than the perceptive stage.

It takes no more illustration to show, therefore, that for Milton knowledge is not a thing separate from life. That is, the systems and theories of the discoursive logic certainly are, but true wisdom, That which before us lies in daily life is not nor can be a thing separate from that life.

At this point one must ask how this distinction applies in daily life for Milton, The answer is simple if we stick to his separation of the intuitive and discoursive methods. On a primary level one may discern two basic facts of experience. The first, an individual freedom springing from this inward intuition; the second, an individual bondage which proceeds from perception of outer circumstances, and from which, through the agency of the discoursive reason, theory and system result. Milton herein met the powers of human analysis just as squarely as Newman, but he resolved the problem quite differently, as the last quoted passage testifies. 

That is, man, wandering amid a maze of intellectual probing, logic,

and argument, finds himself progressively lost.

Such is not adequate directive force for his life, because the more he tries to resolve his difficulties through the agency of that which progressively brings them about, the more he becomes bound to that agency.

Thus the necessity of the intuitive impulse as the real authority arises. 

However one might question the soundness of the distinction by demonstrating that either means taken alone projects the same sort of yoke. That is, one may take his "intimate impulse" as the only dictate, and accepting such as proceeding from the ultimate Force or Real, come to the conclusion that in the final run the individual choice is meaningless. 

Or, by attaching all validity to the second choice, one is forever enslaved to a chain of outer circumstances that automatically denies validity to inner bidding and in reality unseats any possibility of individual or Divine authority.

The answer lies, of course, in the use of both all the way. Neither is final, for both are primary date of life. Hence the immediate recognition of the primacy of conduct as the determiner of my life sprang into view. This matter of conduct has been for me the greatest, the finest direction which

It squarely met up with my former arduous concern for the definition of man's or society's ends in order that I might know what I was doing, and thereby do the right thing, in moving toward those ends. 

The ends would have to justify the means and the means would have to justify the ends. To be aware of one and not the other was for me all along social treason, with destiny of confusion alone. 

Not so with Milton. Neither Jesus nor Samson argue ends and yet their self-control is equal to all emergencies.

Comte once said that to seek a system of salvation that shall be absolutely best, without reference to the civilization and general social conditions amid which it is erected, is analagous to seeking a method of treatment universally applicable without reference to the disease of the patient. This is true, but it is that sort of facile truth that if we are not careful will blind us to the other side of the matter. Man has long searched for a set of principles, a groundwork of understanding and reference to which he can submit all matters of experience for direction. That he suddenly became dismayed and abandoned the quest in the nine-teenth century does not necessarily deny the possibility of such discovery, much less the value of such attempt. Whether in the realm of personal behaviour, of social control, or of government, no intelligently thinking person has ever approached these problems of human existence without first seeking to understand the nature of the principle which gives meaning to the act. Subject and Object in other words. Coleridge's subject is the self or the Intelligence, the sentient knowing Mind; his Object is Nature, what is known by the mind in the act of knowing. The coalescence of the two is that knowing making, being activity we have already considered. (Richards)

For Coleridge therefore, the subject is not a hypothetical abstraction, a conscious ego about which nothing can be known; but it is an act of 

Knowing--"a realizing intuition." 

It is a "subject which becomes a subject by the act of construing itself objectively to itself; but which never is an object except for itself, and only so far as in the very same act it becomes a subject." (B. L., I., 183)

If man is to move toward regeneration, he must cease to construe himself as an objective factor within his experience, determined by past experience, and regard himself as the subject of 

The token of victory in human that experience. behaviour will come only through individual transcendance of the forces of experience, through surmounting the influences of environment and a taking on of a new nature. Nor will this nature be seen as a residue from habits or mores, but as arising from their transcendance.

One cannot deny that rational insight arises out of experience. That is, we see because of our thought development up to the time of insight. This is objective. But we understand because we act, and to act we require reasons to act. But the act itself is dynamic; it is the union of subject with object. It is in the act alone that the two coalesce.

To a divine superhuman spectator the whole human experience would appear objectively. But we cannot wait upon perfect objective knowledge in all fields before acting. To act we must have principles, and if we do not have them, then we must act without them, and perhaps through that act come to an understanding of the cause and purpose of our action.
