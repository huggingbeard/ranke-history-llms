STUDY OF UNIFORM COMMERCIAL CODE I. MEMORANDA AND CORRESPONDENCE PRESENTED TO THE LAW REVISION COMMISSION RELATING TO ARTICLE 5-DOCUMENTARY LETTERS OF CREDIT Item 1 BRIEF STATEMENT ON ARTICLE 5 By K. N. Llewellyn 

(August 16, 1954)

I have just received and read the proceedings of your Honorable Commission in regard to Article 5 on banker's credits. 

Those proceedings drive home the need for some kind of petitioner's introductory statement, as the focus moves from the wellinformed New York Law Revision Commission to the country at large. One does not gather, as one reads the Proceedings, any one of such things as the following: (1) That the Bankers for Foreign Trade-who are regarded as peculiar experts in the field of bankers' credits-have worked for a literal year, in meetings which were spaced for home-work, but which, in sitting and working time, amounted to more than three full working days-to get the Conference's eight-year text polished into a shape which is disapproved in essence by nobody but Roy M. Haberkern. (2) That Roy M. Haberkern has over more than five years been consulted and requested to offer any suggestion to better the drafting of this Article, and has produced not one. (3) That the Banker's Letter of Credit is not, for purposes of our law, a recent innovation-this is an error into which even that sound and careful banker, Mr. Chadsey, seems to have been misled. The Banker's Letter of Credit was thoroughly established in these United States in intra-national banking early in the nineteenth century. (4) The fourth, and most important thing which one does not gather from reading the proceedings is the relation of the law of banker's letter of credit to the lovely job done by the Uniform Commercial Code in Article 9 on Chattel Security. 

Article 5 opens up once more, by way of the law there laid down, domestic financing by way of the banker's letter of credit. Getting the basic law of the subject into available form is a major contribution of the Code. One cannot readily imagine any machinery which can do more to make our commercial credit structure solid than one which places credit judgment in the buyers' banker (interested in safety) rather than in a seller's sales agent (interested in sales). [5]

Nor can one find a better machinery to allow any seller to deal with someone else's "inventory lien" than is here provided by allowing him to arrange for a banker's letter of credit to cover the particular seller's own shipment.

Sub-Committee on Article 5 of the New York Clearing House Association Item 4 

Letter of Mr. Hugh Calkins

JONES, DAY, COCKLEY & REAVIS 1759 UNION COMMERCE BUILDING CLEVELAND 14, OHIO 

May 7, 1954 

GENTLEMEN : 

I have had occasion recently to examine the provisions of Article 5 of the proposed Uniform Commercial Code relating to Documentary Letters of Credit. While this Article has impressed me on the whole as a highly desirable codification of existing practice, in several minor respects it seems to me that the present draft could be improved. These suggestions have very likely already come to your attention; but on the chance that they have not, I should like to point them out to you. 

1. Section 5-116, relating to liabilities of the parties, limits the liability of a bank for improper dishonor or repudiation of a credit to the amount of the draft or credit. This limitation would prevent a customer from recovering against a bank foreseeable damages resulting from the improper honor or improper dishonor of documents presented to the bank. The limitation makes the bank's liability less for a default with respect to a letter of credit than for the dishonor of a simple check. In the latter case, Section 4-402 of the proposed Code permits the recovery of actual damages even though the bank acted in good faith. I do not see how this difference can be justified, particularly since the letter of credit transaction is more nearly tailor-made than the honoring of a check, and the consequences of an error should be more clearly apparent to the issuing or confirming bank. The usual rule that a bank, like any other person, is liable for the foreseeable consequences of its neglect should be applicable. 

2. Any letter of credit transaction necessarily involves more than two parties. It is desirable that a person injured by the neglect or

default of any party to the transaction be entitled to recover from the party in default. Rules of privity of contract which are in force in many states make it doubtful that all parties to a letter of credit transaction under the proposed Code will be able so to recover. I suggest that a specific provision be included that each bank participating in a letter of credit transaction is responsible for injury caused to any party to the transaction. 

3. The liability of a confirming as well as an issuing bank was not made explicit in Section 5-116 as drafted. I believe that this was an oversight which should be corrected. 

Very truly yours,

HUGH CALKINS 

Item 5

Report on Articles 3, 4 and 5 of the Uniform Commercial Code by Task Group 2 of the Special Committee on the Uniform Commercial Code of the Commerce and Industry Association of New York, Inc.

Your Task Group opposes adoption by New York State of the 

Uniform Commercial Code as a whole and of Articles 3, 4 and 5 in particular.

In the case of each of the three specifically mentioned articles, it is our unanimous opinion: (a) That there is neither demand nor need for it; (b) That existing law and custom adequately cover the field and can be amended where required; (c) That each of these articles is poorly drafted, confused, unduly complicated and of insufficient predictable improvement in substance of the law to justify it. 

Article 3 is definitely inferior to the Negotiable Instruments Law which it seeks to repeal. As to Article 4, relating to bank deposits and collections, flexibility is the keynote to the collection processes of the banking community and the rigidity set up by this Article would handicap severely the carrying out of banking functions. 

The need for and possible effectiveness of Article 5 is especially mysterious since the subject matter of this Article, Documentary Letters of Credit, is dealt with on an international basis and under an international code of practice. 

It is the view of our Group that the objections to these Articles and to the Code as a whole are so serious that there is no possibility of curing them by any patchwork revision. Where existing uniform laws require amendment, it can be accomplished by separate measures.

WILLIAM L. KLEITZ, Chairman

Item 6 Memorandum of Mr. Horace M. Chadsey 

May 19, 1954 

Observations by Horace M. Chadsey, Vice President, The First National Bank of Boston, on the Report presented to the New York Law Revision Commission at its hearing on April 20, 1954, by the Sub-Committee of the New York Clearing House Association on Article 5-Documentary Letters of Credit 

The general thesis urged by the Sub-Committee appears to be that Documentary Letters of Credit are an international device, that they operate under an internationally accepted set of rules 

(namely Uniform Customs and Practice for Commercial Docu mentary Credits fixed by the Thirteenth Congress of the International Chamber of Commerce) and that they should not be subjected to the rigidity of a statute applicable in a single area, namely, the State of New York. On this thesis it is not quite consistent that the Committee should cite as one of the deficiencies of Article 5 the fact that it "deals with a very small area of letter of credit practice, and even there it purports mainly to restate terms and principles well settled as a matter of case law and practice". This restricted treatment ought on the contrary to be welcomed as minimizing the area of rigidity and as dealing with principles so well established as not to be open to debate. 

In point of fact, in my opinion these factors constitute the merit of Article 5. That Article represents the hard core of basic letter of credit principles and philosophy to which letter of credit usage must conform. At the same time it leaves out of statutory enactment those numerous features of the "Uniform Customs" which partake of the nature of a working manual and which should therefore be capable of flexibility and change with the natural evolution of commercial practices. I feel too that the well established principles deserve to be made available in statutory form, ascertainable and understandable to the commercial community generally, as contrasted with the surprisingly limited segment of that community to which these matters are now familiar. Enlarged availability may well encourage the employment of the very useful letter of credit device in domestic as well as in international trade. 

Beyond the sweeping disinclination to accept any legislation at all, the Sub-Committee has voiced criticisms of some eighteen different features of the Article, on which I would like to comment individually in the same order of presentation. 

1. The Sub-Committee's contention is that the term "Letter of 

Credit" is used rarely in the Article and that the word “Credit" is used frequently, with the risk that it may "include many types of banking commitments which are not generally considered to be within the Letter of Credit field."

Since the Article is entitled "Documentary Letters of Credit", since it "deals with documentary credits" (5-102), and since "credit" is defined in 5-103 as a documentary credit, it seems highly unlikely that the interchangeable use of the terms can occasion any confusion. Some indication may be had of how naturally the shorter term lends itself to convenient use where constant repetition is involved by noting that the Sub-Committee's own paper employs it considerably more frequently than the longer "Letter of Credit" and does not seem to feel that any lack of clarity has thereby resulted. 

2. The Sub-Committee's contention is that there is ambiguity as to the effect of "uniform customs"; whether they would bear on the interpretation of the Code itself, or apply where the Code is silent. Doubt is also voiced as to the status of the "Uniform 

Customs and Practice" previously mentioned.

The doubts expressed in fact point up the exact purpose of ascertaining "uniform customs"—namely in order to help resolve questions, any questions, to which answers would not be satisfyingly clear without ascertaining what had been the habit of the market place and the general understanding with which the parties to the transaction were so familiar that such features neither seemed to be worthy of being specifically reaffirmed or conversely to merit being specifically excluded. As to the "Uniform Customs and

Practice", attention is called to them as a fruitful source of information, but caution is sounded against assuming that they are by implication incorporated in the law. They are not the only uniform customs" that might be pertinent, and by the time question arose the presently existing "Uniform Customs and Practice" might have been altered; and in fact some better compilation, under another name, might have taken their place. 66 3. The Sub-Committee stresses that the 5-103 definition of a 'credit" contemplates the honoring of documentary drafts and the Committee is concerned lest a credit which asks for receipts instead of drafts is not covered by the Code. 

Drafts are the common and the traditional instruments which serve well to identify the type of transaction. But an outstanding feature of letters of credit is adaptability to particular needs; hence the frequency of the "unless otherwise agreed" clauses in the Code. To delete in a letter of credit the standard requirement for drafts, or to say "no drafts need be drawn", or to use specially devised language which makes no mention of drafts but simply enumerates documents, would hardly prevent anyone from recognizing the undertaking as still being a letter of credit. It would be instructive to know whether the banks represented by the Sub- Committee do in practice consider that the occasional deletion of the draft requirement has so altered their letters of credit as to require a modification of that clause usually appearing in negotiable letter of credit forms and which customarily reads something as follows: 

"We hereby agree with the drawers, endorsers, and bona fide holders of bills drawn and negotiated in compliance with the

terms of this credit that said bills will be duly honored on presentation at the counter of the above mentioned drawee bank."

The unimportance of stereotyped language is further substantiated by 5-104, which says that "no particular form of phrasing is required for a credit". (6 4. The Sub-Committee protests that confusion may arise from the fact that the Code sometimes refers to a bank which is an "issuer' by the perhaps more euphonious term of "issuing bank”. 

This seems scarcely more likely than that a defendant' "should be taken to mean something different from a "defending party". 

The real difficulty for the Sub-Committee would appear to arise because a confirming bank is not always given the same mention and status as an issuing bank. 

That distinction has been deliberate and necessary. A confirming bank assumes an obligation in accordance with the terms of its own confirmation. It may or may not assume all of the commitments of the issuing bank. For example, in the "Uniform 

Customs and Practice" which the Sub-Committee agrees has been so useful, it is provided that "In case of credits available by negotiation of drafts, the confirmation implies only the undertaking of the confirming Bank to negotiate drafts without recourse to drawer." 

The confirming bank has not, under the quoted clause, had to undertake that the issuing bank would pay drafts in the currency of the credit, or that the proceeds of such drafts when paid would not be subject to exchange restrictions, but only that the beneficiary would be relieved of recourse once he had negotiated his drafts with the confirming bank. 

In 5-110 where both issuer and confirming bank are mentioned (an example cited by the Sub-Committee) so are advising, negotiating or collecting banks. In short, all of the banks are mentioned whe might have occasion to handle documents under a letter of credit. 

5. The Sub-Committee's position is that an issuing bank cannot determine when a credit has been "received" by a beneficiary and that ordinarily it considers irrevocability to have begun at the time the credit is mailed or the advice is cabled. 

In the ordinary case no question of cancellation or modification of a credit does arise until long after the credit has been both mailed or cabled and received, so that there is usually no alternative to obtaining the beneficiary's consent before any change can be made in the terms of an irrevocable credit as issued. It could happen, however, that within a few hours after mailing or cabling, the bank's customer, or the issuing bank itself, might desire to change some term. Then it would become a question of whether the advice to the beneficiary could be intercepted. Several possibilities are present.

(a) If the issuer had dispatched its cable to a correspondent bank (the ordinary practice)-a second cable might arrive in time to forestall notification of the first cable's terms to the beneficiary. (b) If the credit had been mailed by the issuing bank to a correspondent for delivery to the beneficiary-a cable could easily anticipate its arrival and prevent its delivery. (c) If the credit had been cabled or mailed by the issuing bank directly to the beneficiary-then no means would probably exist of preventing its receipt by the beneficiary, and in such a case dispatch of the cable or letter would appear to have become constructive receipt for purposes of irrevocability. In this situation, however, a neat question would arise should it later develop that the letter had never arrived or that the cable company had failed to deliver the message. 

It would appear, therefore, that the emphasis in the Code upon actual receipt by the beneficiary is of real significance. 

6. The Sub-Committee is likewise concerned with the purport of the provision that a credit is "established with relation to the customer as soon as the letter of credit is delivered to him." 

It maintains that in the ordinary course a credit is not delivered to the customer and that it is difficult to see the purpose of establishing the credit as to the customer.

Actually, delivery to the customer would seem to occur in one or both of the following senses. (a) It is quite possible, and in fact by no means uncommon, for the issuing bank to turn the original letter of credit over to the customer in order that he may himself forward it to the beneficiary. (b) Even when the dispatching of the credit is undertaken by the issuing bank, some copy of the instrument or advice of its issuance or some other evidence of the issuer's having complied with the customer's request to issue a credit and of the consequent effectiveness of the customer's corresponding obligation to the issuing bank must, it would seem, certainly be delivered to the customer.

Once either of these actions had been taken, the customer's consent would be required before any withdrawal or cancellation of an irrevocable credit could be effected. The issuing bank might in fact repent of having issued the credit within a few hours of such issuance and it might be capable of intercepting the advice to the beneficiary by the mechanism earlier discussed. 

But at that point a question would arise as to the right of the customer in the situation and it would become essential to determine at what stage the issuing bank ceased to be a free agent so far as its customer was concerned. This problem would be quite apart from that which would exist were a request for a change in the credit terms to come from the beneficiary-a request which

of course the issuing bank could not grant without obtaining the customer's consent. 

7. The Sub-Committee reasons that if it is permitted under the Code that an issuer may require that specified documents be satisfactory to it, then there is little point in the further stipulation of the Code that an issuer is not excused from honor by reason of a general requirement that all documents must be satisfactory to it.

There is of course merit in this observation, and the Code appears simply to be striving for a workable compromise between a legitimate need to examine and approve perhaps unusual documents and an effective negation of irrevocability by reason of making fulfillment subject to the issuer's discretion, caprice or responsiveness to customer pressure. At least such a requirement with respect to a specified document should come more conspicuously to a beneficiary's attention than might a general condition. The beneficiary is in the best position to determine whether the requirement that a specified document be satisfactory to the issuer is in all the circumstances reasonable. If he does not consider it reasonable then he ought not to accept the letter of credit. 

8. The Sub-Committee points out that a negotiating bank (unless it is a confirming bank) is not entitled to payment of an acceptance one day before its maturity. 

The conclusion with respect to a negotiating bank is correct; the statement that a confirming bank would ipso facto be entitled to such earlier payment is, however, incorrect. The recital of the Code (5-107) is "an issuing, paying, negotiating or accepting bank" and all of these are entitled to prompt reimbursement for eight drafts—although perhaps from different parties. But only the issuing or accepting banks (which functions may be combined in one bank or divided between two) are entitled to funds a day before maturity of an acceptance. A confirming bank, as such, has no such need of being thus equipped to pay a maturing draft unless it happens to be also an accepting bank. 

It is regrettable that the sentence as it appears in the Code was not divided into two statements, although its present form is not likely to cause much practical difficulty. At the worst it could mean only the shortening of apparent maturity by one day, and then only if the acceptance had continued to be held by a "negotiating" bank which it presented with a demand for earlier payment. Nonetheless, a redrafting of the section (5-107(2)) would appear to be desirable. 

9. The Sub-Committee's position is that the provisions of 

5-107(3) with respect to the issuer's obligation to its customer for the observance of customary banking practices but its nonresponsibility for the behavior of others, ought to extend to a confirming bank as well.

An automatic assumption that a confirming bank always stands in the place and stead of an issuing bank is hardly warranted, as discussed earlier in paragraph 4, and the "customer" of a con-

firming bank is the issuing bank itself rather than the issuing bank's customer. Furthermore, the claim of a confirming bank on an issuing bank may arise from negotiation or payment-functions which it might exercise without any confirmation at all. To the extent, however, that the confirming bank does have a "customer" it should enjoy the benefits and bear the burdens of that relationship and this section might well be redrafted to reflect that situation. 

10. The Sub-Committee contends that the provision in 5-108 (2) reading "Even though an advising bank incorrectly states the terms of the credit, its original terms control with respect to the issuer' is objectionable.

If the terms of the credit as issued and those of the credit as advised are at variance, then clearly one or the other must control. 

By their objection to having the original terms prevail, the Committee seems to imply that it would be preferable to have the advised terms prevail. But the primary obligor is the issuer. It is he who has set the conditions and limitations of his commitment in the credit. And to subject that commitment to such alteration of terms, amount or time, as may result from the careless or deliberate action of advising banks-banks with varying degrees of financial responsibility, competence and impartiality-would be an intolerable surrender of sovereignty. 

If the issuer himself expresses the credit in conflicting termsone way by cable and another way by letter, as the Committee suggests that is a different situation and the advising bank can hardly be held to have stated the terms incorrectly so long as it advises with accuracy the terms first reaching it-which would of course normally be the terms as cabled. 

11. The Sub-Committee's report indicates that it is in doubt as to what no "warranty or representation in connection with documents" means, and fears that it cannot mean freedom from "liability or responsibility for genuineness, falsification or effect' inasmuch as that provision appears in the next section. 

This seems to reflect an extraordinarily tortured reading. The first section would appear to say with requisite clarity that no bank handling the documents in any of the several possible capacities makes any representation with respect to them except that 'there the documents are". The second section says that those banks which have an obligation to examine the documents for their compliance with the credit terms-and that means the paying or accepting banks, because banks acting in other capacities are not required to examine documents unless they choose to do so for their own satisfaction or for the protection of advances which they propose to make-must exercise sufficient care to determine that on their face the documents do comply. But beyond that, those examining banks are not responsible for the form or effect of the documents.

12. The Sub-Committee contends that it is not clear from 5-111 of the Code whether a bank can or cannot-short of being enjoined -refuse to honor when there is purported forgery or fraud in a required document.

It would appear that the Committee is trying to read as a contradiction what is merely a re-statement for the sake of emphasis. Section 1 says that a committed bank is not excused from honor in the stated circumstances; Section 2 says that, proceeding then to honor, such a bank is entitled to reimbursement. This emphasis is appropriate because the party obligated to reimburse may very well be the same party which has alleged the fraud. 

The Committee further contends that an issuing or confirming bank which might be financing a beneficiary in anticipation of a draft subsequently to be drawn, would have no protection under this provision. a bank hardly entitled to any protection. In the first place, it has by its issuance or confirmation voluntarily given. being to this instrument which so departs from normality as to lose the special status conferred on normal credits. And secondly, it has long been the practice of banks to finance sellers against evidence of a definite sale and of reasonably assured payment. 

Sometimes this evidence has been in the form of a sales/purchase contract, sometimes in that of a letter of credit. But in either instance there is always the possibility that the transaction will not be liquidated as expected; the buyer may decline to accept delivery or to pay; the documents may fail to conform to th credit. The bank will then have an advance against some merchandise or some claimed indebtedness, a not abnormal situation or risk, and one against which the bank will presumably have protected itself by exacting a margin commensurate with its estimate of the hazards involved. In any event, a substantial cure would appear to lie at hand in requiring an assignable credit and taking an assignment, even though the Sub-Committee is inclined to dismiss that possibility rather lightly. 

13. The Sub-Committee asserts that the language of 5-112 might imply automatic honor at the end of three days, and that three days is not enough.

The first assertion can come only from a strained construction of the language and a refusal to recognize the clear intent to allow a longer time for examination of the documentary draft under a letter of credit than would be allowed for an ordinary draft.

The second assertion, stressing the necessity of consulting with parties at interest who may be at a distance, perhaps overseas, betrays confused thinking. Such consultation is in no way necessary if the documents do conform to the credit. In that case the draft must be honored anyway and no consultation with anyone is needed. What is needed is time to examine and to determine conformity, and because at times great congestion can arise in Letter of Credit Departments, three days are allowed for such examination. But even that period is capable of further extension

to whatever degree the presenter of the draft may consent-either expressly or impliedly by his failure to demand either payment or return of his draft and documents. 

It is only when examination determines that the documents do not conform to the credit-and when consequently the technically proper action is to dishonor-that the question of consultation arises. Then to be sure, given time, the beneficiary may be able to correct his documents, or the customer, or the issuing bank abroad and its customer, may be persuaded to waive the non-conformity and to authorize honor. But who will have keener interest in that possibility than the presenter and will he not therefore cheerfully consent to all the additional time required for such consultation?

So far as the paying bank is concerned, there is nothing dishonorable about dishonoring a non-complying draft. And if the presenter is not prepared to grant additional time, that is what should be done. The present practice claimed by the Committee of holding documents for an indefinite period and without either honor or dishonor, seems exaggerated in description, or else is actually done with the expressed or implied consent of the presenter. Whether under existing New York law documentary drafts under letters of credit are in fact-as the Committee suggestsexempt from the roughly 24 hour rule applicable to ordinary drafts, is a legal question on which I am not qualified to assert an opinion, but I do submit that for a paying bank to have complete liberty of indefinite inaction without peril would seem highly unfair to the beneficiary. 

14. The Sub-Committee takes the position, not that there is impropriety in the giving of indemnities but that the assertion of the right is either unnecessary or that it belongs in some other statute; also that the provision cannot be effective as to national banks.

The question of what indemnities are an integral and appropriate part of a banking function and as such intra vires, and what indemnities move into the field of suretyship and are ultra vires, lies in a twilight zone which frequently requires determination on the basis of the particular facts. Here in 5-113 are recurrent situations in which indemnities may be considered intra vires and so to state in the Code is to free this special area from obscurity. As to national banks, the approval herein given should at least be helpful and persuasive, even though not directly enabling. 

15. The Sub-Committee advances several objections with respect to the assignment provisions of 5-115. The first of these is that the 

Code's stipulation is in conflict with Article 49 of the Uniform 

Customs and Practice. 

Any superficial conflict here appears to be more in words than in meaning. Article 49 is aimed not only at retaining performance by the original beneficiary, but it also contains detailed provisions for preserving his right to substitute his own (and larger) invoices for those presented by the transferee. In short, the contemplation of Article 49 is that under a transferable

credit the beneficiary may serve as a supervising intermediary, may transfer the credit to another party if that is necessary in order to persuade the latter actually to supply the merchandise, and may ultimately obtain his own profit or surcharge without revealing its amount to the transferee. All of these possibilities are still open to the beneficiary under Section 1 of 5-115. 

The second objection of the Sub-Committee holds that 5-115 is inconsistent with other provisions of the Code which give a special status to negotiating banks, but only when the credit extends by its terms to such negotiating banks. 

Here again there seems to be no real inconsistency because two distinct, even though similar, operations are in question. The acquiring of rights to the proceeds of a credit by reason of an assignment is quite a different process from acquiring the draft itself by purchase under credit which by its own terms invites such purchase and undertakes to protect the purchaser. 

The third criticism of the Sub-Committee is that there is no limitation in the Code on the number of assignments or re-assignments as contrasted with Article 49 which contemplates that "the credit can be transferred once only.' 

Since the possibility of chain assignments relates only to proceeds and since Article 49 is not really concerned with proceeds but with performance, no international complications may be expected here.

As a urth criticism the Sub-Committee urges that an issuing or confirming bank has no defense, as against a buyer who has not authorized an assignment of proceeds, for failing to pay a complying draft presented by the original beneficiary, and thus obtaining for the buyer the documents he desires and this notwithstanding that the issuing or confirming bank may have received a notice of assignment. 

Inasmuch as the Code permits payment in such circumstances, this criticism appears to be simply an expression of regret that the assignee (whose existence has heretofore been resented by the Sub-Committee) cannot obtain a better protected claim. 

A fifth deficiency is urged by the Sub-Committee on the that a "collecting" bank, having been omitted from the list of handling banks recited in Section 2 of 5-115, is bound therefore for its own protection to search the filing records in order to ascertain whether there is a perfected security interest in the proceeds collected under documentary credits. This hardly appears to be a realistic concern in view of the provisions of Section 

9-306(2) and (3). Presumably funds collected by a collecting bank would constitute "cash proceeds" with the result that an assignee of rights under a letter of credit would have no right to such cash proceeds even in the hands of the original beneficiary unless insolvency proceedings were commenced within ten days.

Certainly the position of the collecting bank would be no worse than that of the original beneficiary. 

16. The Sub-Committee protests that under 5-116 the right of recovery for improper dishonor or repudiation may not be limited

to the amount of damages suffered; also that mention of an issuing bank ought to be accompanied by mention of a confirming bank. 

A bank which has improperly dishonored or repudiated has of course invited claims which the injured party is not likely to make any herculean effort to minimize. But the provision of Section 3 that no recovery shall exceed the amount of the credit or draft would seem to indicate clearly that the intent is to limit and not to magnify the sums recoverable.

With respect to a confirming bank-as mentioned before, the obligation of a confirming bank will be determined by the terms of its confirmation. It may itself have honored (even though the issuer has dishonored), in which case it will be the one attempting to recover from the issuing bank. 

17. The Sub-Committee holds that the granting of a preferred status, in the event of insolvency, to a letter of credit to which funds or collateral have been allocated, is questionable both on the score of legal effectiveness and of public policy. 

This is a matter which appears to me to lie solely in the legal field and on which therefore I am not competent to comment. As an operational problem it is of little consequence. 

18. The Sub-Committee asserts that the definitions of trade terms appearing in an earlier Article of the Code (Article 2) vary from those adopted in 1941 by several prominent organizations. 

This does not appear to be a very significant objection with reference to the Letter of Credit article (Article 5), nor to be of overwhelming consequence in any event. Considerable differences exist between the meanings commonly ascribed to such trade terms here and abroad; the definitions cited are thirteen years old and a revision is not inappropriate; and it is more important to have a definite rule, from which contracting parties can stipulate variations if they so desire, than to have no authoritative statement at all.

The Sub-Committee further contends that it is questionable whether a bank negotiating a draft with documents which prove to be non-complying can have become a holder in due courseapparently on the theory that a comparison by such a bank of the documents with the terms of the credit itself would have revealed the non-complying features, and hence would have put it on notice as to the defense against the instrument on the part of the paying bank.

This appears to be a question of legal niceties. Operationally a negotiating bank is not required to examine the documents against the credit unless it chosses to do so for its better assurance of collectibility and its lesser dependence upon the responsibility of the drawer. And such an examination might reveal no obvious discrepancies, while the examination of the paying bank might be more rigorous and might develop other discrepancies. Furthermore, as the committee has emphasized in its discussion of the time necessary for examination (its criticism No. 13), the practical result in many instances is that initial non-compliance is ultimately either corrected or waived.

The Sub-Committee's summary of its objections to Article 5 

recites the existence of ambiguities, conflicts, and undesirable changes in letter of credit principles; alleges that the Code's Letter of Credit article is unsatisfactory, that it covers only part of the field, and that in any event it is preferable to avoid statutory rigidity; and it concludes that no Letter of Credit article is needed, and that no demand for such legislation exists. 

I have tried in my observations to deal with such ambiguities and conflicts as the Sub-Committee has alleged and to demonstate that they have little existence in reality. I feel that the existence of an authoritative rule confined to the essentials of Letter of Credit philosophy is preferable to the absence of a statutory standard, and I feel that Article 5 of the Code constitutes an acceptable workable rule. I agree that there has been no insistent clamor for such legislation, but I am impressed by the narrowness of the field in which familiarity with Letter of Credit practices now exists. Out of some 14,000 commercial banks in the United States, only about 100 have any Letter of Credit business of consequence, and of this 100 probably 25 account for three-fourths of the volume. Any step toward expanding the availability of authoritative standards in this field would not seem to run counter to the public interest.

DEAR SIRS:

This letter is written to comply with Dean Smith's request made at the public hearing on April 20, 1954, that I communicate to the Commission my views on Article 5 of the proposed Uniform Commercial Code, and in particular my views as to the report of the Subcommittee of the New York Clearing House Association on that Article. I am flattered by the request, the more so since my comments at the hearing were entirely personal and, as does this letter, represent my own opinions and not the views or position of any client or other interest.

I am struck by the fact that there are two issues raised by the consideration of Article 5. The first relates to the desirability of legislation in New York State on the subject of commercial letters of credit. The second relates to the sufficiency of the proposed Article and its effect on existing New York law. The

Commission, as I understand it, is concerned primarily with the duty of study and report on the effect of the proposed Article on New York law. I shall, therefore, address myself to that reportorial function, and in that connection, to the specific comments by the Clearing House Subcommittee and to the memorandum preparatory to the public hearing, which was circulated by the Commission staff. 

Nevertheless, I deem it important to state for the record my views on the first question, which is as to the need or desirability of legislation in New York in the field of documentary commercial credits. In this connection, I have no quarrel with the report of the Clearing House Subcommittee. Such objections as I do have to that report relate to portions of its criticisms rather than to its conclusion. The conclusion is that Article 5 should not be enacted in New York. With that conclusion I am perfectly content and I believe, indeed, that the banking community as a whole is similarly united that a State statute in the field of letters of credit is a work of supererogation. Mr. Chadsey of the First National 

Bank of Boston pointed out at the hearing that the commercial credit industry, if it can be so described, is comparatively small, including only about 100 out of the more than 14,000 banks in the United States. Most, if not all, of these 100 banks are members of the Bankers' Association for Foreign Trade which, through a special committee, gave detailed study to Article 5. The conclusion reached by that committee was: 66 "... Actually we have found among bankers no particular enthusiasm for letter of credit legislation. Banks have been little troubled by litigation in this field and hence are not conscious of any existing legal deficiencies. It is an area, however, which is presently nearly devoid of statutory law and in which the enactment of basic legislation need not be injurious so long as it is soundly conceived and avoids unnecessary rigidity. 'As to Feasibility, the Letter of Credit Article which is contained in the proposed Uniform Commercial Code consists of a statutory Text and an explanatory Comment. The Text has been painstakingly reviewed by your Committee and, in its present form, we feel that it is consistent with sound banking practice and legal principles. The Comment contains nothing which appears objectionable, but in any event, should a conflict develop, it is the Text which will prevail. If this Article 5 were enacted into law, we would not anticipate that any operating difficulty would flow from it." 

This statement seems to me to be accurate. I have been quoted by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws as "urging" legislation on the subject of letters of credit. (See "The Uniform Commercial Code," p. 5). This is not wholly accurate. For what it is worth, I should like to direct the Commission's attention to that portion of my article in the March 1952 issue of the Wisconsin Law Review (reprint of which is enclosed herewith) which was not quoted by the Commissioners and which reads as follows:

"To sum up the issue of whether there is a need for a statute on letters of credit, it may be said that there is no need, in the strict sense of the word. The business is now done cheaply and efficiently; there are no abuses which require remedial legislation; the rules of practice are written down and, in large measure, accepted and followed. Even if there is utility, there is no exigency. If this analysis is correct, the proponents of 

Article 5 must bear a heavier burden of proof than would be the case if there were demonstrated necessity for a statute on letters of credit. The necessitous accept what they can get; those who merely seek additional efficiency are bound to be more particular."

It seems to me perfectly clear that the banking community does not need a statute on letters of credit and, indeed, would probably prefer not to have one. I know of no demand anywhere for such a statute; there has been no suggestion known to me that any substantial or responsible mercantile group has reported itself as desiring such legislation. The question before me, however, and 

I take it the question before the Commission, is not whether or not a statute should be enacted, but rather the extent to which the proposed statute alters existing law and practice, and on this score I believe that it would result in little, if any, alteration and may have some limited utility. 

On this primary issue, the effect of proposed Article 5, I believe that the Clearing House Subcommittee was wrong more often than it was right. I shall discuss the items of its report in the same order and numeration that they are set forth in the report. 

1. The Subcommittee expresses concern about the scope of the 

Article. This concern seems to me unwarranted. In the context of Article 5, it is quite clear that the Article is confined to documentary letters of credit. "Credit," as defined in 5-103(1)(a) is a commitment (or notice in the case of a revocable credit) made "at the request of a customer." A "customer" is defined in 5-103 (1) (g) as a "buyer or other person who causes a bank to issue a credit." This emphasis on the tri-party character of the transaction seems to me to exclude confusion with such banking arrangements as lines of credit, loan agreements and the like, which are essentially bi-party in character. Remittances and payment orders which in international usage reflect the source and destination of the payment seldom, if ever, involve documentary drafts, and are not likely to be confused, even by an ignorant court, with documentary letters of credit. 

2. I am in sympathy with the Subcommittee's uncertainty about 

Section 5-102(3), particularly in relation to 1-205, but am not very seriously concerned. It seems to me that 5-102 (3) must, as the Subcommittee says it may, apply usage in the interpretation of provisions of the Code and in areas where the Code is silent. 

This is a laudable object since, as was brought out at the hearing, Article 5 is intended to state general principles and not to dictate detailed instructions for the handling of specific situations.

The important feature of 5-102(3), however, is one which the Subcommittee appears to have overlooked. Section 5-102(3) limits 

Section 1-205. It must be remembered that most commercial credits arise out of a mercantile transaction, but are wholly distinct from such transaction. Thus, in the case of a sale of Formosan goose feathers, financed by a bank credit, the sales contract is, under 1-205, to be construed in the light of customs among feather merchants, but 5-102(3) makes it clear that the letter of credit is to be construed in the light of customs prevailing among bankers. This is good sense and good law. See Rayner & Company Ltd. v. Hambros 

Bank, Ltd. (1943) 1 K.B. 37.

To the extent that 5-102(3) and its Comment establish the basic separation of the banking contract from the sales contract, they are to be praised, not condemned; to the extent that the draftsmanship fails, it should be fixed. 

At the hearing there was considerable discussion as to the effect of the Uniform Customs and Practice under the Code. Again 

I am unable to perceive the difficulty. With few exceptions Article 5 is subject to variation by agreement. If a particular letter of credit (or the customer's application and agreement for a particular letter of credit) invokes the Uniform Customs and Practice, then I would take that to be an importation of the Uniform Customs and Practice into the credit contract, and I would assume that the Uniform 

Customs and Practice would control. 

3. The Subcommittee's concern that the definition in Section 5-103 (1)(b) of a documentary draft may exclude documents called for by a letter of credit which does not require a draft, seems exaggerated. Granting that this suggested interpretation of the section is possible, nevertheless it seems to me that an instrument which calls for payment against documents would inevitably be considered as a documentary credit, any request for payment being treated as a draft for this purpose, even if not for purposes of the negotiable instruments law. Bouvier's Law Dictionary defines 'draft" as, "An order for the payment of money, drawn by one person on another. . . . It is said to be a nomen generalissimum and to include all such orders." ، ، 

4. I think the comments with respect to definitions of "issuer' and of "confirming bank" may be well taken as verbal criticisms but are minimal in their effect. It seems clear to me that, to the extent of its confirmation, a confirming bank is an issuer, and that the issuing bank is a "customer" of the confirming bank within the meaning of Section 5-103(1)(g). I don't have much doubt about the existing law and practice nor about the fact that the Code attempts to reflect it; in other words, any problem seems to me one of draftsmanship only. 

5. I am in accord with the objection made by the Subcommittee to the rule establishing the time when a credit is established (Section 5-106 (2) (a). For the reasons stated by the Subcommittee and by Mr. O'Halloran at the hearing, it seems to me that it would be manifestly a better rule to provide that a credit is established

with respect to all parties when the instrument or authorized written advice of it is dispatched. On the other hand, there are distinct virtues in having the obligations bite down only when the credit or authorized advice is received by the beneficiary. Suppose, for example, the customer becomes insolvent after a night letter cable has been dispatched to an advising bank; the issuer can (and frequently does) send a full rate cable to intercept the message before the beneficiary is advised. Perhaps the bank's customer remains solvent, but either he or the bank discovers that the intended beneficiary is a crook; again it would be desirable to be able to intercept a message. As I said at the hearing, however, my discomfort about this section is greatly alleviated by the fact that it permits variations by agreement. It would be nicer not to have to contract out of a statutory provision, but it is not cruel or unusual punishment to have to do so. Once again, while this seems to me not to be a structural defect, it is a defect. 

6. As in the preceding paragraph, I am in agreement with the 

Subcommittee on this point, although not exercised about it. I can suggest a very fine reason in response to its inquiry as to the "purposes of establishing the credit as to the customer". It prevents him from canceling. It might be desirable to amplify 

Section 5-106(2)(a) by providing that the credit is established with respect to the customer, "as soon as the letter of credit or a copy or other advice of establishment thereof is delivered to him". 

7. I think that what the Subcommittee says with respect to 

Section 5-107 (1) is probably true, but I do not see what difference it makes. 

8. The Subcommittee's objection to Section 5-107 (2) is meritorious to the extent that it raises reasonable questions as to interpretation. I personally find no serious difficulty with 5-107(2) because it talks about "reimbursement" of any "payment" duly made "under" the credit. Thus a confirming bank upon which drafts are drawn would be entitled to immediate reimbursement because it pays the draft. On the other hand, a bank which negotiates a draft under another bank's credit does not pay under the credit; it buys the instrument. I suppose it is possible, although it is unusual, to have an arrangement whereby a bank would at once negotiate and pay. Nevertheless, the labors of interpreting 

5-107 (2) seem to make it clear that the Subcommittee's objection is well taken. A statute for the multitude should not require such strain. Perhaps deletion of the word "negotiating" is all that is required. 

9. I do not concur in the Subcommittee's objection to Section 

5-107(3). The confirming bank is in the position of an issuer vis-a-vis its correspondent, the actual issuer, and it is required to observe customary banking practices, but it is not responsible in any other respect. It might be desirable to spell this out by an additional sentence, but it scarcely seems necessary. 

10. I just flatly disagree with the Subcommittee's comments with respect to Section 5-108 (2). This section provides a rule

which I believe to be the one presently in effect. If Bank A issues a credit to finance oranges and in the advice Bank B inaccurately tells the beneficiary that the credit is to finance apples, the inaccurate statement by Bank B does not and ought not alter the rights or increase the responsibilities of Bank A. The beneficiary is protected by a claim against Bank B for his damage, if any, due to the misinformation. The Subcommittee points out quite accurately that the section does not cover a variance between cable instructions and subsequent mail confirmation; it is not so intended. 

This is not a mistransmission by the advising bank. It is a fault internal to the issuer, and if such a question were to arise today, I don't know what the answer would be. 

11. The Subcommittee's comment with respect to Section 5-110 

seems to me unjustified. Section 5-110 deals with two separate things. The first is the matter of representation or warranties. 

Subdivision (1) codifies the existing law in its rule that the handling of documents in a commercial credit transaction does not involve any warranty or representation by the various banks. 

Each one passes the document on to the next one and it is up to the party receiving the documents to examine them and be satisfied in its own mind. Parenthetically, I might say that while this absence of warranty or representation is, in my view, the law as it stands today, there have been criticisms of it. See Thayer, Irrevocable Credits in International Commerce; Their Legal 

Effects, 37 Columbia L.R. 1342, for the minority view, and Ward and Harfield, Bank Credits and Acceptances (Third Ed.) pp. 

95-98, for the correct view.

The other matter with which Section 5-110 deals is the obligation of the paying bank (whether it be an issuer or otherwise) to examine documents. Here Article 5 follows the Uniform Customs and Practice almost verbatim. Article 9 of the Uniform Customs and Practice provides, "Banks must examine all documents and papers with care so as to ascertain that on their face they appear to be in order." This is the duty which an issuer owes to its customer or which a paying bank owes to its correspondent. The comment of the Subcommittee that a negotiating bank is excluded from the protection of subsection (2) leaves me baffled. The negotiating bank does not need the protection of subsection (2). The negotiating bank owes no duty to anyone (except itself) to examine documents at all. If, however, the documents are not in good order on their face, the negotiating bank may not get paid. There may, if the Code is enacted, be an additional problem under Section 3-302, which is discussed, infra, under item 18. If the negotiating bank presents to the issuer documents which are not in good order and the issuer makes a mistake and pays, the negotiating bank is home free because under subdivision (1), it has made no representation or warranty as to the sufficiency of the documents. 

I am completely unable to see the interrelation of the holding in the Kingdom of Sweden case and Section 5-110, nor can I find what "negative inference" the Subcommittee deems objectionable. 

The Kingdom of Sweden case negated any necessity of going behind

the documents, as to which no warranties are made. (197 Misc. 431 at 449-450). 

12. I disagree with the Subcommittee's objection to Section 5-111. 

Subdivision (2) says that whether or not the bank is notified of forgery or fraud, it may, unless enjoined, pay. Neither this subdivision nor the previous subdivision, however, says that the bank must pay. The only case in which the bank must pay, even though it knows of fraud, is the case in which there is an intervening innocent third party, which is clearly covered by Subdivision (1). 

Section 5-111 seems to me to do a very good job of codifying the opinion in the Sztejn case, which the Subcommittee cites. 

There is, of course, the fact that 5-111(1) commences with the statement that a bank is not excused from honor or reimbursement by the fact that the goods or documents do not conform to the underlying contract for sale or to the warranties implied from dealing with documents. This could conceivably mean, taken by itself, that a bank would be required to honor documents which it knew to be forged or fraudulent, even though they were presented by the beneficiary directly and even though the bank's customer neglected to enjoin it. I am not sure what the result would be in such a situation under present law; I should imagine that a court under 5-111(1) might excuse the bank from honor in such case, taking the section as a whole. Under present law, were I faced with such a situation, I should advise the bank not to pay (unless expressly instructed to do so by its customer with full knowledge of the facts) and to rely upon the inability of the rascally beneficiary to show any damage.

I am not impressed with the Subcommittee's objection that Section 5-111 brings no succor in the new kind of credit there referred to. I have been and am suffering with credits of that character and wish that there were some clear provision which would put the financer in the position of a bona fide holder under the Sztejn case. That situation presents a headache under present law and would present the same kind of a headache under Article 

5. I would like to have the headache cured, but I cannot in good conscience rebuke draftsmen because they do not solve all my problems for me but only some of them. 

13. As I said, perhaps too vehemently, at the hearing, I disagree with the Subcommittee's conclusion that Section 5-112 means that a bank has accepted or paid a draft if it holds it for more than three days. I am not enthusiastic about the three-day rule in 

Section 5-112. I am in accord with the Subcommittee's view that anything which tends to rigidify the time for inspection of documents is disadvantageous, and not just to the banks but to the merchants they serve. Nevertheless, 5-112 seems to me about as flexible as a rule could be and still be a rule. The bank can withhold honor indefinitely if the presentor "expressedly or impliedly" consents thereto. There is thus plenty of scope for practical necessities. 

My major objection to the report, and one that I feel very strongly about, is the statement by the Subcommittee of its belief that if a bank, without the consent of the presentor, withholds honor for

more than three days, it is deemed to have honored. Where in the section do they find any support for this? The penalty for unauthorized withholding is dishonor and the immediate accrual of a right of action for dishonor. That is all the penalty there is. 

I do not know what the situation is under present law. Mr. Chadsey said he thought, as a Boston layman, that the New York rule, probably more honored in the breach than in the observance, was 24 hours. I think his speculation is wrong in respect of New York. 

I know of no rule which would govern the time within which a bank is required to pay a sight item drawn on it under a commercial credit. Where the draft is presented for acceptance, the present law is found in Section 224 of the Negotiable Instruments Law, which reads, "The drawee is allowed 24 hours after presentment in which to decide whether or not he will accept the bill; 

This, however, does not mean, any more than does Section 5-112, that delay for more than 24 hours results in constructive accept ance. Section 225 of the Negotiable Instruments Law (which, unlike Section 5-112, is a penalty provision) reads:

"2 "Where a drawee to whom a bill is delivered for acceptance destroys the same, or refuses within 24 hours after such delivery, or within such other period as the holder may allow, to return the bill accepted or non-accepted to the holder, he will be deemed to have accepted same." (Italics mine) 

Now this goes beyond Section 5-112. This has an express provision which is conspicuously absent in 5-112, that unauthorized retention is equivalent to acceptance. And yet it has always been my view that a drawee has not accepted a bill merely because he retains it for more than 24 hours without permission of the presentor. Note that the statute says that the drawee is liable as for an acceptance, where he "refuses" to return the bill. I take it that this means that if the presentor hands in the draft over the counter and calls back later and says, "Either accept or return the instrument", then 24 hours after such demand the drawee is liable as for an acceptance unless he returns the instrument. But if the presentor does not make some such specific demand, then there is no acceptance because, there being no demand, the drawee has not refused. I have some support for this proposition. See Matteson v. 

Moulton, 79 N. Y. 627 (1880).

So on this troublesome point, I claim that Mr. Chadsey was misinformed if he was told that under present New York law and practice a bank must act within 24 hours, and I claim that the Subcommittee is wrong in its assertion that under 5-112 failure to act within three days would result in acceptance or payment. 

I adopt Mr. Chadsey's position in respect of the Subcommittee's allegation that there is a conflict between Section 5-112 and 

Article 10 of the Uniform Customs and Practice. Article 10 

applies to an entirely different situation. Let me try to straighten out the conflict. Section 5-112 deals with a situation in which the presentor (whether the beneficiary or a negotiating or collecting bank) tenders documents to the issuer and says, "This is what you

ordered, isn't it? Please pay me. "Section 5-112 says that unless the presentor consents to a longer period, the issuer must make up its mind within three days whether the documents are in order, and if it fails to do so, it has dishonored its credit (not honored it). 

Article 10, however, deals with this kind of a situation: The negotiating, confirming or paying bank sends to the issuing bank documents which it (the negotiating bank) has bought. Under the first paragraph of Article 10 (and under Section 5-107 of the Code) the negotiating, confirming or paying bank is entitled to reimbursement if the documents are as called for by the credit. 

Now let us assume that one of the documents is defective in some particular, which may or may not be indicative of a serious defect in the merchandise. There had been an alarming tendency on the part of many foreign banks to wait for the arrival of the merchandise, allow the buyer to examine it and then (usually when the market was falling) throw back the draft and documents to the presenting bank with a statement, which was quite accurate, that the documents did not conform to the terms of the credit. The reasonable time allowed for inspection in Article 10 is a time to examine the documents to see that they are on their face as called for by credit and to advise the bank which is out of pocket that claim is going to be made either for recoupment of a debit or as grounds for a refusal to reimburse. Perhaps I can put the distinction this way: Section 5-112 sets a prior time limit for honor or dishonor; Article 10 of the Uniform Customs is a short statute of limitations. 

14. The Subcommittee's objection to Section 5-113 seems to me unreasonable. There is no suggestion that the provision is wrong in policy or (for a wonder!) that there is any ambiguity in its language. The objection seems to be entirely that this provision ought to be put in some other body of law. 

15. There is some basis for the Subcommittee's objection to 

Section 5-115(2). I would say that the objections could be on two grounds: first, a policy objection to permitting assignment of proceeds without the consent of the issuer or its customer, and, second and conversely, a policy objection to the absence of protection given an assignee. In dealing with this particular section, let me digress for a minute to talk about the general problem of assignments in respect of commercial credits. The rule is clear, reflected clearly in Section 5-115(1), and I think clearly desirable that the right to perform under a credit cannot be delegated in the absence of express consent. Now the question comes as to what 

3 the policy rule ought to be with respect to an assignment of the monies payable under the credit if and when they become due and payable. On the policy level, there are excellent reasons why a bank should not be required to accept an assignment of the proceeds of a credit. Under the present law, a bank can so provide, and in my view under the Caristo case and the Ericksson case, such a provision would be effective. However, I understand that the 

Code, in Article 9, reverses the Caristo case. Moreover, under

present law, where a bank does not expressly provide that the proceeds of the credit may not be assigned, I lean to the view that an assignment of proceeds may be made effective under Section 41 

of the Personal Property Law. 

As I said, there are very good reasons why this situation is unsatisfactory. In the case of an ordinary contract, there is comparatively little hardship in requiring one party to make payment according to the terms of the contract to the assignee of the other party. The situation is quite different in respect of commercial credits because the issuing bank does not select the beneficiary. 

Thus it may be that a bank will issue a credit binding itself to pay a million dollars to the XYZ Corporation, which is completely unknown to it but which is known to the bank's customer. If the 

XYZ Corporation then has an absolute right to assign its right to be paid under the credit, the bank is faced with the problem of verifying the signatures and corporate authority of a party which is a complete stranger to it.

Perhaps this needs clarification. In the case of a beneficiary unknown to the bank, the bank protects itself by paying the beneficiary by check and when the check clears the bank has the protection of the guarantee of prior endorsements involved in the clearing process. Where, however, the unknown beneficiary assigns rights under the credit, the bank will have to act at its peril in determining whether the assignment lay within the corporate powers of the beneficiary, whether appropriate corporate action had been taken to authorize the assignment and whether the signing officers were, in fact, empowered by the corporation. This is the kind of burden and the kind of risk which is inconsistent with the greatest commercial value of letters of credit, and it is this kind of thing, among others, which has led to the stiff rule of Article 49 of the Uniform 

Customs and Practice. 

Section 5-115 is quite frankly a compromise. I personally would have said that the better view would be to carry into the assignment of proceeds the same unyielding rule which applies to transfer of credits.

If, however, the draftsmen of the Code refuse to accept this view, then the language of 5-115 (2) seems to me necessary to protect the banks by giving them an option either to pay the beneficiary or the assignee, as may be desired. This being so, I am unable to agree with the Subcommittee in its allegations on page 19 [51] of its memorandum that there is an inconsistency between Sections 

5-115(2) and 5-116(1). Similarly, I am not concerned about the alleged multiple assignments first by the beneficiary, then by his assignees. If the bank chooses not to recognize the subsequent assignments, it simply does not have to do so. 

Overall, however, I must agree with the general position of the Subcommittee on Section 5-115 because I still believe that the 

Uniform Customs and Practice, Article 49, rule should be applied 'across the board". 

16. If the Subcommittee's reading of Section 5-116(1) had any substance, I would join with them wholeheartedly in their objection

to the section. I am, however, entirely unable to see what they are complaining about. They interpret 5-116 (1) in a fashion which 

I would regard as subversive to the first principles of our democracy and then they say, if this dreadful thing is true, is it not inconsistent with subsection (3)? Perhaps I am living in a fool's paradise, but it does seem to me that when you have subsection (1), which says that the holder may do thus and so, and subsection (2), which says that the holder may do thus and so at specified times and in a particular manner, and then you come along with subsection (3), which says, "In no event shall recovery exceed the amount of the credit or the draft, as the case may be",-then section (3) controls, and if there is any conflict, it is promptly resolved by applying the limitations of subsection (3). 

17. I am unable to make any categorical objection to the Subcommittee's opposition to Section 5-117. This is without doubt a highly controversial section and it may ery well be that the Subcommittee is correct in its suggestions as to ineffectivity of the provision with respect to national banks or under the Bankruptcy Law. So far as the existing law of New York is concerned, I do believe that Section 5-117 represents present law. See 

Shawmut Corporation of Boston v. Bobrick, 260 N. Y. 499 (1933); 

Greenough v. Munroe, 46 F. 2d 537, aff'd 53 F. 2d 362, cert. den. 

52 Sup. Ct. 127; and Bank of United States v. Seltzer, 233 App. 

Div. 225 (1931); contra: cf. Taussig v. Carnegie Trust, 156 App. 

Div. 519 (1913), aff'd 213 N. Y. 627 (1914). The Bobrick and

Taussig cases are difficult indeed to reconcile, but the Bobrick case would seem to me to control. On the policy question, I feel that the rule enunciated in Section 5-117 harms no one and may be beneficial to many. I have unburdened my soul in this connection at pages 208-225 of “Bank Credits and Acceptances" (3rd Ed.); and there is no point in repeating the discussion here. I think that 

I am right on the policy and on the law, but there is no doubt in my mind that it is a very close question on both matters, and I am not only in a numerical minority on the point, but am regarded by many of my betters at the bar as being a low fellow for asserting it. 

18. The Subcommittee makes a very valuable contribution in this portion of its report by its indications of the relation of Article 5 to other Articles of the Code. 

I do not regard the possible undesirability of Sections 2-319 to 2-324 as appropriate criticisms of Article 5. If the Code as a whole were enacted, then the impact of these sections on commercial credit business would presumably be the same whether or not Article 5 were included. 

On the other hand, while these sections may not affect the desirability or adequacy of Article 5 as such, there is no doubt that they will affect commercial credit business, and the Subcommittee properly directs attention to them. Similarly, I think that the criticism of Section 1-201 (6) is valid. The Comment to Sec tion 1-201(6) makes it quite clear that the definition of bill of lading has been "enlarged to include freight forwarders' bills",

and Article 20 of the Uniform Customs expressly provides, "Bills of lading issued by forwarding agents will be refused... Again, however, the fact that Section 1-201 (6) conflicts with the present international understanding in respect of bills of lading tendered under commercial credits is not, to my mind, so much a citicism of Article 5 as of the approach in the Code generally. Moreover, I would be disposed to believe that a bank which issued a credit subject to the Uniform Customs and Practice would be entitled to refuse a forwarder's bill of lading notwithstanding the effect tivity of Article 5 and of Section 1-201 (6). Section 1-201 (6) is a general definition; appropriate invocation of Article 20 of the 

Uniform Customs would, in my judgment, simply mean that for purposes of the particular credit, a forwarder's bill, although a "bill of lading", was not the kind of bill of lading which would comply with the terms of the credit. I join the Subcommittee in hoping it will be unnecessary to engage in such metal gymnastics. 

The other point made by the Subcommittee in respect of the definition of "holder in due course' seems to me not only valid but deserving of far greater emphasis than it was given. It seems quite clear to me that Section 3-302 materially cuts down the class of bonafide holders in due course. Under the present law, a person becomes a holder in due course if he takes an instrument before it is overdue, without notice that it has been previously dishonored, and if he takes it in good faith and for value and without notice of "any infirmity in the instrument or defect in the title of the person negotiating it". Section 3-302 adds additional requirements, that the party take the instrument with 'observance of the reasonable commercial standards of any business in which the holder may be engaged", and "without notice ... of any defense against or claim to it on the part of any person". 

The variation in the language of the NIL and the Code could have very serious repercussions in the handling of commercial credits. Let me present a hypothetical case. Bank A issues an irrevocable credit whereby it undertakes to honor drafts if accompanied by commercial invoice evidencing the sale of apples and an on board bill of lading. The credit also contains the usual negotiation clause whereby the issuing bank engages with all bona fide holders of drafts drawn under and in conformity with the terms of the credit that such draft will meet with due honor upon presentation. Let us assume that the beneficiary sells his draft with accompanying documents to a factor, X, who in turn negotiates the draft with Bank B. Bank B presents the draft and accompanying documents and Bank A refuses to pay on the ground that the documents on their face are not as required by the credit. Under present law, it seems to me quite clear that Bank B has no cause of action against Bank A, assuming there is actual non-conformity of the documents. 

It seems equally clear that Bank B does have recourse on the draft against prior parties and is a holder in due course. There is nothing wrong with the draft and no indication that there is any defect in the title of the person negotiating it. Consequently. 79

it seems clear that Bank B could recover on the draft as a holder in due course.

Now let us consider what might happen under the Code. Let us assume that X, the factor, is insolvent. Bank B, therefore, elects to proceed against the beneficiary, who is the drawer of the draft. 

The beneficiary asserts as a defense failure of consideration in the negotiation of the draft to X, or some other similar defense. 

Bank B maintains that it is a holder in due course, and that this defense cannot avail against it. The defendant, however, points out that even though Bank B had no notice of an "infirmity in the instrument" and no notice of any defect in the title of the person negotiating it, nevertheless under Section 3-302(1)(c) Bank B should have known that there was a defense against the instrument on the part of the issuing bank. The defendant's argument would be that the draft specified that it was drawn under a letter of credit and that in the observance of the reasonable commercial standards of the banking business, Bank B would or should have examined the credit instrument and compared the documents accompanying the draft to it and therefore would have been on notice, not of an infirmity but of a defense against it on the part of the issuerdrawee. In other words, a defense by the drawee might be sufficient to open the door to unrelated defenses by prior parties. 

It has been suggested, with some force, that the drawee has no "defense" to payment of the draft because, as a drawee, it has no liability to the holder on the draft itself. This is an ingenious argument. I should like to believe that a court would accept it, or would otherwise refuse to countenance the hypothesis I have suggested, but I should be unable to give a banking client unqualified advice that it would be safe in the supposititious situation. If this interpretation of Section 3-302 is correct, it would represent a body blow to the commercial credit business. At the present time, when the credit of a prime bank extends by its terms to drawers and bona fide holders and negotiators of drafts drawn under and in compliance with its terms, and where a draft drawn in purported compliance with such a credit is tendered for negotiation by a responsible party, it is not the practice of banks to make an exhaustive inspection of documents; they are completely satisfied that they have recourse against prior parties and the draft is bought with that factor in mind. If Section 3-302 means what it seems to mean and is applicable to negotiations under commercial credits, the utility of the negotiation credit would be seriously impaired. 

If the Code should be enacted with Section 3-302 in its present form, and with Article 5 a part of the Code, then it would seem to me imperative that there be in Article 5 an express provision eliminating from the commercial credit business the impact which I have suggested 3-302 might have upon it. 

Having gone on at such length in respect of the Clearing House Subcommittee report, I should like to trespass further upon the time of the Commission and to make some comments on the memo-

randum circulated by the Commission staff, and these comments will also follow the order and numeration of that memorandum.* 

A. Need for Codification 

(1) Codification of the law of letters of credit is desirable to the extent and only to the extent that it does not stultify the development of the device. As the Clearing House Subcommittee very aptly said, the letter of credit is a delicate instrumentality and its usefulness depends in large measure upon its flexibility and adaptability to many kinds of transactions and developing trade practices in this country and abroad. I hope, and in large measure 

I believe, that Article 5 is so broad in its statements of law and so confined in its scope to fundamental principles that, if enacted, it would not operate to restrict the development of the letter of credit business, but if there is any serious doubt about this, then certainly it should not be enacted. (2) The existing New York law on letters of credit is satisfactory. It is not by any means all encompassing and perfect; there are day-to-day problems which have no answer in the decided cases. There are, however, surprisingly few, if any, really bad decisions. New York has been fortunate in its commercial courts. (3) The existing letter of credit practice, as embodied in the Uniform Customs and Practice and similar documents, is satisfactory and is reasonably responsive. In the nature of things, the Uniform Customs cannot be altered from day to day, but as a consensual regulation they are closely keyed to current problems and the understanding of them, not only by bankers but by the mercantile community, is adequate. (4) Article 5 is certainly, in my mind, not an essential part of the Code. The very reasons which I find to its advantage, namely, that it is so generally subject to variation by agreement, that it states broad principles of commercial credit law, and that it is strictly confined to documentary credits, lead me to the conclusion that its omission would in no way weaken the rest of the Code. 

Letters of credit are specialties and the absence of legislation in respect of them should not adversely affect legislation in respect of other commercial transactions.

B. Coverage 

(1) I suspect that the principal reason for confining Article 5 to documentary letters of credit is that there is very little room for codification in respect of clean credits. A clean credit classically is an unconditional promise, whereas a documentary credit is a conditional one. Of course, this statement is not wholly accurate; a clean credit is conditioned in the sense that a particular person must draw and must draw within a stated length of time, but there is really very little to talk about in connection with clean credits. And I would assume that if the law as to documentary * See p. 36.

credits were clearly stated, it would flow over into the field of clean credits. I see no reason to worry about the exclusion of clean credits even in respect of such ambivalent devices as the red clause credit. (2) As stated above, I am convinced that a letter of credit is a specialty and I would, therefore, feel strongly that any financing arrangements which are not documentary credits should be kept out of the documentary credit tent. 

C. Uniformity (1) Existing practice relating to letters of credit is substantially uniform among the 48 States and, indeed, internationally. 

This is a very broad statement, but I think it is warranted. Of course, there are areas in which there is some discrepancy of practice. There are places and times where there is deliberate divergence. And there are always areas in which people interested in the field can debate, sometimes acrimoniously, for hours. Fundamentally, however, the people engaged in the business are talking the same language and living by the same book, and this curiously enough is true even in the case of our British cousins who have not formally adhered to the Uniform Customs. An interesting commentary on this can be found in an article by A. J. Rice and W. J. Thorne ("The Uniform Customs and Trade Practice for Commercial Documentary Credits") appearing in "The Canadian Banker", Vol. 56, No. 3, Autumn 1949, which compares the

British practice with the Uniform Customs. It is interesting to note that the area of disagreement is very small indeed. 

Whether Article 5 would increase the danger of nonconformity by creating new ambiguities is a $64 question. I think in honesty the answer would have to be yes. The very fact that Mr. Chadsey, Professor Schlesinger, the Clearing House Subcommittee and I have been able to cover so many pages of memoranda and stenographic transcript with debate, indicates that any statute in this field will raise problems of interpretation. I believe that Article 

5, if enacted, would not be susceptible of much non-uniform interpretation because, in large measure, of its limited area. It is certain, however, that I should hate to make any warranties in this regard and I have no doubt that there would be some litigation under it which would not be occasioned in its absence. (2) I do not think the adoption of Article 5 would have any appreciable effect on international uniformity. If, as I believe, Article 5 is enunciatory of the Uniform Customs, then it should not come as a bombshell. In some instances it might be a useful lever to avoid disputes, but in the main I think it would be substantially without effect internationally. I would certainly assume. that the Uniform Customs are uniform within the meaning of Section 5-102(3), that they are customs as distinguished from "'usages of trade" and that they are quite definitely customs among banks. If by uniformity is meant an unswerving adherence and complete uniformity, then there is no uniformity under the Uniform

Customs and Practice, but I hasten to point out that on that definition there is no uniformity anywhere, in the law or elsewhere in the western world. (3) I do not think that Article 5 would operate one way or the other to reduce divergence, if any there be, between practices followed in international transactions and practices followed in domestic transactions.

D. Particular Provisions of Article 5 

(1) I would vote for subdivisions (c) and (d) of this inquiry. (2) I am not quite prepared to accept the statement that under existing New York law a credit which is ambiguous as to whether it is intended to be revocable or irrevocable will be considered as being irrevocable. Section 5-105 follows almost literally Article 3 

of the Uniform Customs, which reads: "All credits, unless clearly stipulated as irrevocable, are considered revocable even though an expiry date is specified.'

I do not regard Section 5-105 as constituting a change in existing law. If we were dealing in a vacuum I would say that the rule should be the reverse of 5-105 and that a credit should be irrevocable unless clearly stipulated to be revocable. We are not living in a vacuum, however, and if such a rule were adopted, it would represent a diametric opposite to the generally accepted international practice. In fact, the issue is academic because revocable credits, in the United States at least, are a dying breed. (3) I had always understood that the "no consideration" rule stated in Section 5-106 (1) was basic law. 66 (4) I would believe that the distinction, if it is real, be left to the 'customs" and to the agreement between issuer and customer. (5) The rule should not be changed. It is fundamental to the ease and availability of commercial credits, and an alteration in it would make them more expensive and less useful. Besides, I am deeply opposed to curtailment of the right of contract. This is particularly true in the commercial field where the contract is made between businessmen. (6) The absence of details as to what constitutes documentary compliance is, in my opinion, one of the greatest single virtues of Article 5. I disagree vehemently with the criticism of the omission in 62 Yale Law Journal 226. To my mind it would be disastrous to attempt to legislate on factual minutiae which could never be expected to remain constant. (7) I have nothing to add in this connection to what was said above in reference to the Clearing House Subcommittee report. 

Conclusion 

I should like to sum up this inordinantly long letter as follows: (1) there are deficiencies in Article 5 which should certainly be remedied before serious consideration is given to enactment; (2)

there are deficiencies in the Code which, while not part of Article 5, might seriously affect the letter of credit business and it would be well to give consideration to correction of these imperfections; (3) in the main Article 5 accurately restates the broad base lines of existing commercial credit law and practice, and footling criticisms are not only unwarranted but create unnecessary confusion; (4) in view of the highly specialized nature of the business, the satisfactory operation of the business by those engaged in it and the eminently satisfactory public service which is performed by the letter of credit device, there certainly seems no need for legislation on the subject of letters of credit and if, as appears to be the case, the parties most directly affected are either indifferent to legislation or oppose it, then legislation should not be enacted. 

Very truly yours, (Signed)

HENRY HARFIELD

QUESTIONS ON WHICH ARGUMENT IS INVITED 

(Prefatory Note: The questions listed in this paper are illustrative rather than exhaustive. Argument at the hearing will not be limited to the questions listed herein. 

Inclusion of a question in this paper merely means that in the opinion of the consultants such question seems especially susceptible of clarification by debate. No inference should be drawn that questions not appearing in this paper are foreclosed or considered unimportant.) 

A. Need for Codification 

(1) Is it desirable to codify the law of letters of credit? (2) Is existing New York law on the subject (i.e., pertinent case law and such statutes as may have a bearing on letters of credit) a satisfactory body of law? If not, what are its specific defects? (3) Is existing letter of credit practice, as embodied in the

Uniform Customs, in printed forms and in other documents typically used in connection with letter of credit transactions, satisfactorily responsive to the needs of business? If not, what are the specific defects of present practice? (4) Is Article 5 a necessary part of the Code in the sense that omission of this particular article would adversely affect the effectiveness of the code as a whole, or of other articles? 

B. Coverage 

(1) What policy considerations militate for, or against, codifying the law of documentary letters of credit while leaving the subject of "clean credits" uncodified? Is it probable that the exclusion of "clean credits" from the coverage of Article 5 will lead to difficulties with respect to credits which may be partly documentary and partly clean, such as red clause credits? (2) Apart from the "clean credit", are there other types of financing arrangements which are frequently used and which are, 36

in function or concept, so closely related to letters of credit that they should not be excluded from the coverage of a code article regulating the latter? 

C. Uniformity (1) Is existing practice relating to letters of credit substantially uniform among the 48 states, and especially among the important commercial states? Would such non-uniformity as may presently exist be substantially reduced by the adoption of Article 

5? Does Article 5 increase the danger of non-uniformity by creating new ambiguities which may invite divergent interpretations in different states? (2) Would adoption of Article 5 increase or reduce international uniformity in this field? Would the effect of the "Uniform 

Customs and Practice for Commercial Documentary Credits" 

(herein referred to as Uniform Customs), in transactions in which they are not expressly incorporated by reference, be clarified by the language of Article 5-102(3) and Comment 4? Are the Uniform Customs (a) "uniform" within the meaning of Section 5-102(3); (b) "customs", as distinguished from "usages of trade" (compare section 5-102 (3) with section 1-205 and Comment 5); (c) customs "among banks"? (3) Would Article 5 help to reduce divergence between practices followed in international transactions and practices followed in domestic transactions?

D. Particular Provisions of Article 5 

(1) An "irrevocable credit" within the meaning of section 5-103(1)(a) can exist only if there is a signed writing; pursuant to section 5-106 (2) (a) such credit can be established only by receipt or delivery of a writing. Under these provisions, would a promise which is not embodied in a proper writing but which otherwise comes within the broad definition of a credit", be 66 (a) void or (b) unenforceable, or (c) valid and enforceable but not governed by Article 5, or (d) subject to such existing statutes of frauds as may be applicable? (2) Under existing New York law, a credit which is ambiguous as to whether it is intended to be revocable or irrevocable, will be construed as being irrevocable. Section 5-105, reversing this rule, provides that a credit is revocable unless it clearly stipulates that it is irrevocable. Pursuant to section 5-106 (2) (c), revocable credits may be modified or cancelled at any moment without notice to the customer or the beneficiary. This latter provision would seem to apply not only to credits which are clearly labeled as

revocable, but also to those which are treated as revocable by virtue of the new rule of construction contained in section 5-105. 

Is the change in existing law which would be brought about by section 5-105 a desirable one? (3) Section 5-106 (1) provides that no consideration is needed either to establish a credit or for an agreement modifying its terms. 

This provision does not appear to embody a drastic change of existing law, in terms of practical results as distinguished from academic theories. In so far as hypothetical situations might be projected in which the results reached under section 5-106(1) differ from those reached under existing law, are the changes desirable? (4) Under existing New York law it seems that documentary compliance is judged by a strict standard in actions by the beneficiary against the issuer but by a reasonable standard (even in the absence of an exculpatory clause of the kind mentioned in the next question) when the issuer, having honored the drafts, seeks reimbursement from its customer. Should this distinction, which is not (or at least not expressly) adopted in section 5-107, be expressly preserved? Or should it be expressly abolished? Or should the matter be left to be determined in accordance with the "customs" mentioned in section 5-102(3) ? (5) In applications for letters of credit, made on printed forms supplied by the issuer, the applying customer often assents to exculpatory clauses such as the following: "The applicant authorizes you and/or your correspondents to accept and pay drafts drawn under said credit provided the documents presented are at the time deemed sufficient in your or their absolute discretion, and agrees to reimburse you in the manner aforesaid, even if such documents should, in fact, prove to be insufficient, defective, or forged."

It was held in Overseas Trading Corporation v. Irving Trust 

Co., 82 N.Y.S. 2d 72 (Sp. T., N.Y. County, 1948) that this particular clause cannot be said to be invalid as matter of law. This rule does not seem to be changed by section 5-107. Should it be changed? (6) Article 5 in its present form, as distinguished from earlier drafts, does not contain any detailed rules as to what constitutes documentary compliance. The omission has been criticized. See 

Comment, 62 Yale Law Journal 226, at 243-8 (1953). Is this criticism justified (a) as to transactions governed by the Uniform Customs, and especially by Articles 10, 15-35, 39, 42, 46, 47 thereof; and (b) as to transactions not, or not clearly so governed? (7) Section 5-115 is a provision which was subject to considerable debate and change in the drafting process. In its present form, is it satisfactory? In particular (but not by way of limitation) the following questions appear to be of interest:

(a) Section 5-115 deals with assignments of the right to draw, and with assignments of the proceeds of the credit. 

Article 5 does not deal with the element of assignment which is inherent in the negotiation of drafts drawn by the named beneficiary under a negotiation credit (See Harfield, Secondary 

Uses of Commercial Credits, 44 Columbia Law Review 899, at 906-7, reprinted in Ward and Harfield, Bank Credits and 

Acceptances, 1948 ed., pp. 136-9). Is this omission justified? (b) Section 5-115 (1) permits an assignment of the right to draw under the credit if the issuer consents and expressly designates the credit as assignable. Would a credit which is so designated and expressly authorizes assignment of the right to draw thereunder, be an engagement to honor "drafts drawn by a named person" within the meaning of section 

5-103(1)(a)? If there is an inconsistency between the two sections, which of them should be changed? (c) Does section 5-115(1) contain a sound solution of the problem created by a letter of credit which is silent or ambiguous as to assignability of the right to draw? (d) Section 5-115 (2) changes existing law, at least to some extent. Cf. Ericksson v. Refiners Export Co., Inc. 264 App. 

Div. 525 (1st Dept. 1942). It should be noted, moreover, that the first half of the first sentence of the subsection is not subject to the clause "unless otherwise agreed". Is this subsection an improvement compared to existing law? Is it satisfactory as applied to the "proceeds" of every transaction which under sections 5-103(1) (a) and 5-104 may be called a "credit", keeping in mind the great variety of such transactions? (e) Is it desirable for banks to have the right of election conferred upon them by the "but" clause in section 5-115(2)? 

Is the elective provision desirable from the standpoint of nonbank parties to letter of credit transactions? (f) Does section 5-115 (2) indirectly control the question of attachment of the "proceeds"? If so, is the subsection sound as applied to attachments? (g) Is section 5-115 in all respects consistent with Article 

49 of the Uniform Customs?

Letter of credit law and practice has been developed through the centuries as a simple and practical device to provide pay-

ment for merchandise shipped primarily in international trade. 

Letters of credit issued, confirmed or advised by the New York City banks during the year 1953 amounted to several billions of dollars, of which much the greater part involved international shipments. The letter of credit is, therefore, obviously a device of major importance in international trade. 

The ability of the New York City banks to issue, confirm or advise such credits in future years with the same facility and elasticity and at the very low rates which now prevail is essential to the foreign trade of our country and to the continued importance of New York as an international financial center.

Although letters of credit are handled in New York City in the tremendous sums indicated, the device has been used extensively in this country in substantial amounts only since around 1913. Great Britain was particularly active in letter of credit practice prior to that time and continues so today. 

In Great Britain banks and traders have not found it necessary to enact legislation on this subject, but have relied on case law and customs developed throughout the years. 

Although the letter of credit device is relatively new in this country and growth in its use has been enormous, there has been remarkably little litigation. Such litigation has related for the most part to questions as to sufficiency of documents presented. The proposed Article would not settle such questions and, indeed, it would seem impracticable, if not impossible, to provide for the disposition of such varied and complex problems by means of statute. 

The proposed Article 5 deals with a very small area of letter of credit practice and even there it purports mainly to restate terms and principles well settled as a matter of case law and practice. Our Subcommittee has found a number of troublesome ambiguities in these purported restatements; also new concepts which we believe to be wholly impracticable. 

Before dealing specifically with the text of the Article, we wish to mention generally at this point a matter of vital concern. As indicated above, the letter of credit practice of this State is largely in connection with international trade. 

In any letter of credit transaction there may be involved shipper and buyer, the bank opening the letter of credit, the bank confirming or advising the letter of credit and one or more negotiating and collecting banks. Among these parties there will be a number of contractual relationships. The parties may be scattered throughout several states and countries and not infrequently the only contact with the State of New York is that one of the banks or one of the parties is located here. Under these circumstances it seems unnecessary to state that in order for letter of credit business to be conducted efficiently and cheaply there must be common practice, common understanding of terms and provisions and common usage. The letter of credit is a delicate instrumentality. Such large sums are involved and transactions are conducted in

such great degree by cable or business letter that any factor which would serve to disrupt or make difficult the common understanding and reliance which is so essential to this field would seriously impair the usefulness of the device and the world-wide acceptability of New York letters of credit. We feel strongly that legislation here would be a disruptive factor in that it would bind New York banks and parties to rules not necessarily accepted or understood abroad or, if understood and accepted abroad today, might not be accepted abroad as trade practices and customs develop. We see no need for legislation in this field which although it involves billions of dollars annually touches only a limited area of the population, primarily banks, exporters and importers, factors and brokers, who are thoroughly familiar with the device and with customs and usages here and abroad. 

It is to be noted that from time to time the International Chamber of Commerce has adopted Uniform Customs and 

Practice for Commercial Documentary Credits which have served a very useful purpose in leading to a common understanding here and abroad of basic principles. It is noteworthy that only in a few instances has the International Chamber of Commerce attempted to cover the same areas which Article 5 purports to deal with and that among those instances the Code provisions and the Uniform Customs and Practice do not appear to conform with each other in important respects. 

In summary, we believe that whether or not other parts of the Code are adopted in New York there should be no Article 5. While we do not recede from this position in any respect, in the remainder of this presentation we will deal specifically with various provisions of Article 5 which we find to be ambiguous or contradictory and therefore leading to confusion and uncertainty, or to be objectionable as a matter of principle and practice. 

1. Scope of Article. The Article is entitled "Documentary 

Letters of Credit," but we find that the term "letter of credit" is used rarely in the Article, the word "credit" or "credits" being frequently used. The word "credit" by its definition in 5-103 and treatment throughout the Article would appear to include many types of banking commitments which are not generally considered to be within the letter of credit field. Apparently any credit is deemed to be covered which involves a written commitment to make a payment if based on a "documentary draft," which by definition is a draft accompanied by a paper or papers. 

2. 5-102 (3) provides that in construing the Article reference may be had to uniform customs among banks. 

The effect of this general provision is indefinite. The Comment states that it is "a particular application" of the rules stated in Article 1, 1-205. The latter Section, however, apparently refers to the interpretation and construction of agreements between the parties, and not to the interpretation and

construction of the Code itself, which seems to be the purport of 5-102 (3). Thus, there is a possible conflict between the plain words of the subsection and the Comment. Possibly the subsection is intended to mean that custom may be used in the interpretation of provisions of the Code but not to vary their terms and, perhaps, that custom is to be applied where the Code is silent. It is also possible that the draftsmen intended to accomplish a statutory recognition of such written statements of practice as the Uniform Customs and Practice for 

Commercial Documentary Credits fixed from time to time by the International Chamber of Commerce. If that was the intention it seems to have been frustrated by the Comment which states that the Code refers to actually existing practice at the time the question arises and not to "any particular written formulation." Thus the status of such an important document as the Uniform Customs and Practice is left in doubt, and it is even doubtful whether the Code provision accomplishes more than a statement that actual proven custom may be used to interpret terms of the Code, leaving in question the extent to which even actual proven custom applies where the Code is silent. 

3. As indicated above, 5-103 (1) (a) in defining the word "credit" refers to "documentary drafts" which under the definition in 5-103 (1) (b) are drafts accompanied by a paper or papers.

The letter of credit device may, however, include many transactions in which drafts are not required. For example, where under the law of a country a tax is imposed on drafts, simple receipts may be called for rather than drafts. The definitions, therefore, raise questions as to whether or not the Code is intended to apply only to documentary letters of credit where drafts are required, and as to whether the term documentary draft" as used throughout Article 5 does not result in the exclusion from coverage by the Code of documents called for by a letter of credit which does not require a draft. 66 66 

4. 5–103 (1) (c) defines an issuer' in such terms as to include a bank which confirms a credit as well as a bank which opens the credit. 

"Confirming bank" is, however, also defined and in other Sections such as 5-110 reference is made both to "an issuer' and to "a confirming bank. On the other hand, in some 

Sections such as 5-107 (1) and (3), 5–108 (3) and 5-116 (1) only the "issuer" or the "issuing bank," and not the "confirming bank, is mentioned, although it seems probable that the term "issuer" or "issuing bank" when used in such Sections is intended to include both the opening bank and the confirming bank. Further confusion is added by the use of the term "issuing bank" in several parts of the Article, which term is not defined. 

5. 5-106 (2) (a) provides that a credit is "established" when the beneficiary "receives" the letter of credit or authorized written advice.

This provision is seriously defective since a bank in many instances cannot ascertain when a credit is "received." Unless the credit expressly negatives application of the Code, a bank which is instructed by its correspondent or customer to cancel or modify a credit will not know whether or not the credit has been "established" and thus will not know whether or not the credit at that time can be cancelled or modified without the beneficiary's consent. In ordinary letter of credit practice, a bank considers that its credit or confirmation becomes irrevocable at the time the document is mailed or the advice is cabled. 

6. The succeeding clause of 5-106 (2) (a) provides that a credit is "established" with relation to the customer as soon as the letter of credit is "delivered" to him.

In the ordinary course, a credit is not delivered to the customer and it is difficult to see the purpose of establishing the credit as to the customer. It is also to be noted that the word "delivered" is used in this clause rather than the word "received," which emphasizes the difficulty connected with the word "received" as used in the preceding clause. 

7. 5-107 (1) provides that if a documentary draft complies with the terms of an irrevocable credit, the issuer is not excused from honor by reason of an additional general term that all documents must be satisfactory to the issuer. However, under the further provisions of the subsection, an issuer may require that specified documents must be satisfactory to it.

The latter provision seems to make meaningless the prohibition against a requirement that all documents must be satisfactory to the issuer. If the issuer can determine in its sole discretion whether or not one document is unsatisfactory, it would hardly make any difference that it had no such power with respect to other documents. 

8. 5-107 (2) indicates that a "negotiating bank” is entitled to immediate reimbursement of any payment duly made under the credit and to be put in funds not later than the day before maturity of any acceptance duly made under the credit. 

To the extent the term "negotiating bank" refers to a confirming bank the provision is correct. The provision seems to include, however, a negotiating bank which is not a confirming bank. If a negotiating bank, not a confirming bank, buys a sight draft, it is entitled to payment of that draft by the issuing or confirming bank only upon presentation of the draft. 

If such negotiating bank buys a time draft it is entitled to acceptance and return of the draft and to payment of the draft upon the maturity of the acceptance, and not on the day before maturity as the subsection would seem to indicate. 

9. 5-107 (3) states that the "issuer's" obligations to its customer include observance of customary banking practices but do not include responsibility for acts or omissions of others. 

As indicated above, it would seem that this provision is

also intended to apply to a confirming bank, and consequently the provision is defective. 

10. 5-108 (2) provides that the original terms of a credit control with respect to the issuer even though the advising bank incorrectly states the terms. 

This provision is objectionable for several reasons. Under certain circumstances the issuer may be bound by the terms of the credit as advised, for example, in the case of ratification or laches, in which event the original terms of the credit would not control. This provision also appears to ignore the possibility of conflict between a credit advised on the basis of a cable which is subsequently confirmed by letter stating different terms from the cable or containing additional terms. 

In the latter circumstance, the words "original terms" in the provision would present a problem since there would be a question as to whether the cable or the letter confirmation constituted the original terms. 

11. 5-110 deals with the banks' responsibility as to documents. 

Subsection (1) states that neither an issuer nor an advising, confirming, negotiating or collecting bank makes any warranty or representation in connection with documents. It is difficult to tell what this provision means, but it is doubtful that it has the same effect as a statement that a bank has no liability or responsibility for the genuineness, falsification or effect of any document apparently regular on its face, because these matters are covered specifically in subsection (2) of the Section. The distinction between subsection (1) and subsection (2) becomes important because while the first subsection extends to an issuing, advising, confirming, negotiating and collecting bank, subsection (2) extends only to a bank "called upon to pay or accept under a credit." Thus, a bank (not a confirming bank) negotiating drafts under a credit is excluded from the protection of subsection (2). It is also to be noted that subsection (1) negatives representations and warranties as to documents only and does not extend to the legality of the underlying transaction. The latter point can arise as in Kingdom of 

Sweden v. New York Trust Co., 197 Misc. 431. The negative inference of the Section is objectionable. 

12. 5-111 (1) prohibits a bank from refusing honor even though it knows that the goods or documents do not conform to the underlying contract for sale or to the "warranties implied from dealing with documents." The subsection then provides that in the event of forgery or fraud in a required document, a court of appropriate jurisdiction may enjoin the issuing or confirming bank from honoring or reimbursing unless honor or reimbursement is demanded by a paying, accepting or confirming bank which has acted in good faith in reliance on the document or by a negotiating bank which is a holder in due course. Subsection (2) provides that whether or not the issuing or confirming bank is notified of a forgery

or fraud or alleged forgery or fraud in a document apparently regular on its face, such bank, unless enjoined, may nevertheless honor or reimburse.

There are several difficulties with this Section. In the first place the Section may mean either that a bank cannot refuse to honor even though it knows of forgery or fraud and even though there are no intervening rights of an innocent third party or that a bank can refuse to honor if it knows of forgery or fraud or an alleged forgery or fraud even though there are intervening rights of an innocent third party. Either possibility is objectionable. See discussion in Sztejn v. J. 

Henry Schroder Banking Corp., 31 N. Y. S. 2d 631.

Also, under subsection (1) a court is prohibited from enjoining honor or reimbursement in the event of forgery or fraud in a required document only where such honor or reimbursement is demanded by a paying, confirming or accepting bank. 

Recently, there have been attempts to develop a form of sight credit under which the draft would not be drawn and presented until a stated period after the date of the bill of lading. In such a case the issuing or confirming bank might be requested to finance the beneficiary for the interim period and to accept as security an assignment of drawing rights under the credit made pursuant to an express provision in the credit. Such financing bank would receive no protection under this provision. This is an example of possible developments in practices in letter of credit transactions and of the danger of any attempt to specify rights and obligations by statute in this field. It is no answer to state that under the Section there may be agreement to the contrary since a provision permitting an assignment of drawing rights under certain circumstances with an additional provision that no court may enjoin payment in the event of forgery or fraud would probably receive little understanding or acceptance here or abroad. 

13. 5-112 establishes a specific time of three banking days within which a bank may withhold honor of a draft without dishonor.

This language is quite artificial. Literally, it is only the "honoring" which may be withheld for three days. We think this sentence means that if the bank holds a draft for three banking days it is deemed to have honored. We understand it has been stated that under this sentence the bank may, without penalty, dishonor at any time before or after the expiration of three banking days provided the draft and documents do not comply with the letter of credit. Under either construction, a bank which does not determine within the three-day period that it is entitled to dishonor withholds honor at its peril. This is a particularly objectionable and dangerous provision since there are many circumstances which may render it impracticable to make this decision within the arbitrary time limit. Since letters of credit frequently involve international dealings, it is often necessary for a bank to communicate with its customer or with a beneficiary or negotiat-

ing bank abroad as to its action and the eventual honor or dishonor of the draft may depend upon communications between the customer, the beneficiary or negotiating bank and the issuing or confirming bank extending over a greater period than three banking days. The provision also seems to be in conflict with Article 10 of the Uniform Customs and Practice which provides, in part: "The issuing Bank shall have a reasonable time to examine the documents." 

The provision destroys the flexibility which is essential to international letter of credit transactions and may work hardship on all parties concerned. Where documents are questionable, banks may be forced by the Code to dishonor since the time allowed may not permit communication with its customer or the issuing bank. The Comment implies that the three-day period is longer than that allowed in the case of ordinary drafts, but we know of no applicable decision holding that unless a bank returns a documentary draft presented under a letter of credit within the time allowed for an ordinary draft it is deemed to have honored the item. In any event as a matter of established practice banks do not handle documentary drafts under letters of credit within the time permitted for clean drafts.

We believe that under the rule proposed banks in this State would suffer by reason of the irritation and resentment which would be felt by parties abroad if the banks were required to adhere to a specified deadline and were not permitted a reasonable time within which to act. Moreover, and very importantly, the provision makes no allowance for extraordinary conditions which may exist during time of war, strikes, freezing regulations, etc., or even holiday periods here at home or in the country of the correspondent or beneficiary. Very recently a bank strike in one foreign country delayed communications beyond the three banking day period permitted by this provision. 

14. 5-113 provides that a bank seeking to obtain payment, acceptance, negotiation or reimbursement under a credit may give indemnities.

The Comment states that the purpose of this provision is to make clear that it is proper for a bank to give such indemnities and that doing so is a proper part of the business of banking and therefore not ultra vires. If the power to give indemnities already exists, there seems to be no need for this provision. If it is intended to give banks a power which they do not now possess, clearly it could not extend to banks or other corporations not incorporated in New York, including national banks. If this Section is intended to give New York 

State banks power to give indemnities in circumstances where it is believed they do not now have such power, it would seem that such legislation belongs in the Banking Law and not in a statute dealing with documentary letters of credit. Since the introduction of the Code in the New York legislature, the

American Law Institute has amended the Comment to Section 2-323 to remove a clear implication formerly appearing in that Comment that under certain circumstances a bank could be required to accept an indemnity covering missing documents required by a credit. Obviously, the amendment proposed is necessary since apart from the questionable rule previously stated in the Comment, the provisions of Article 5 should not be subject to modification or supplementation by a Comment to another Section. 

15. 5-115 (2) provides that the proceeds of a credit may be assigned unless there is an agreement to the contrary. 

This provision is questionable from a number of viewpoints. 

First, the Section appears to be in conflict with Article 49 

of the Uniform Customs and Practice for Commercial Docu mentary Credits fixed by the Thirteenth Congress of the International Chamber of Commerce which provides, in part: "A credit can be transferred only once on the express authority of the opening bank and provided that it is expressly designated 'transferable' or 'assignable'." Eriksson v. Refiners Export 

Co., Inc., 264 App. Div. 525 (1st Dep't 1942), involved a claim by Cities Service Oil Company that it was the owner of the proceeds of a credit as assignee of the letter of credit and of the draft drawn against the credit. Although the 

Court stated that Cities Service Oil Company was an assignee of the letter of credit, it appears from the facts that it was an assignee of proceeds since the opinion expressly mentions that the draft was drawn by the original beneficiary and was endorsed to Cities Service Oil Company. The letter of credit issued in the transaction was a special confirmed irrevocable credit which in that case made express reference to the Uniform Customs and Practice for Commercial Documen tary Credits fixed by the Seventh Congress of the International

Chamber of Commerce. Article 49 of the Uniform Customs and Practice as in effect at that time also provided that a credit could only be transferred on the express authority of the principal. The Appellate Division held that the letter of credit itself not being subject to assignment, the credit could not be transferred by any assignment of a draft against it. The Court stated at page 528: "It seems clear, however, that the purpose of Article 49 is to prevent a special letter of credit from becoming general merely by some form of transfer. We are constrained to hold, therefore, that Cities Service Oil Company acted at its peril in attempting to become assignee of the letter of credit.' 99 The Appellate Division held that not only was the performance under the credit not assignable, but that the proceeds were not assignable. 5-115 would reverse this rule because it purports to make a distinction between the right to draw under a credit, which under subsection (1) can be assigned only on

express authority of the issuer, thus following the pattern of Article 49 of the Uniform Customs and Practice, on the one hand, and the right to assign the proceeds of a credit, which under subsection (2) are expressly made assignable, on the other hand. In view of the international character of letters of credit, this change would inevitably result in confusion and uncertainty since parties abroad or in non- Code states cannot be expected to take cognizance of this important change.

We also believe it important to point out the inconsistency between the assignment of proceeds provisions of 5-115 (2) and 

Comment 2 to 5-107, and the text of 5-111 (1) and 5-116 (1).

Although, as indicated above, 5-115 (2) makes proceeds of credits assignable, the Comment and Sections referred to do not afford to a bank negotiating a draft, under a credit which does not extend by its term to a negotiating bank or endorsee, rights which would prevent an injunction against payment under the credit in the event of forgery or fraud in a required document or right under 5-116 (1) to recover the face amount of a complying draft in the event of dishonor. Second, the 

Code provision does not state by whom the proceeds may be assigned, which permits various possibilities leading to confusion such as multiple assignments by the beneficiary and his assigns. Third, the intention of the buyer, it must be assumed, is to obtain the documents which he has specified for inclusion in the credit, and if a buyer has not authorized an assignment of proceeds there would seem to be no defense against payment of a complying draft and documents if presented by the original beneficiary whether or not the issuing or confirming bank has received notice of an assignment. To illustrate, suppose the beneficiary gives to the issuing or confirming bank a notice of assignment and thereafter presents a complying draft and documents for payment. Would the issuing or confirming bank be protected against the buyer if it refused to pay and to take up the documents? We think the answer is clearly "No," and in order to meet this point subsection (2) substantially dissipates the right of the assignee to require payment. Our fourth point is with regard to this provision. The Section provides that in the absence of other agreement, the issuer or any negotiating, paying, confirming or accepting bank may, notwithstanding the filing of a notice of assignment, honor or negotiate a draft drawn by the beneficiary. The provision making proceeds assignable would seem to be of small comfort to an assignee of proceeds who extends credit in reliance on the assignment if notice of the assignment may be ignored. On the other hand, it would seem that the provision would be of small comfort to a "negotiating or paying bank" if the issuing of confirming bank is given the right to refuse to pay the beneficiary's draft if it has received a prior notice of assignment. In view of the limited rights which are afforded an assignee of proceeds under this Section we must inquire: Why should the attempt be made to reverse

customs and practice, with the resulting confusion and uncertainty, when the possible benefits to even the beneficiary or his assignee are so restricted? Fifth, the last provision of subsection (2) states that, subject to the provisions of the subsection, an assignment of proceeds creates a "security interest" to the extent provided in and subject to compliance with Article 9. Under the subsection the issuer, or any negotiating, paying, confirming or accepting bank may, notwithstanding perfection of a security interest in proceeds, honor or negotiate a draft drawn by the beneficiary, but a collecting bank is excluded from the list of banks afforded protection by this provision. It therefore appears that a collecting bank as a matter of ordinary precaution may have to examine the records of financing statements filed under Article 9 or otherwise to ascertain whether there is a perfected security interest in the proceeds before it can safely dispose of proceeds collected under documentary credits. 

The Comment to this confused Section compares an assignment of proceeds to an assignment of money claims. We submit that there is, in fact, no such similarity since the obligation of the issuing or confirming bank is not merely to pay money to a designated beneficiary, but is an undertaking to honor the beneficiary's draft drawn and presented in compliance with the terms of the letter of credit and accompanied by the required documentation. 

16. 5-116 (1) provides that if an "issuing bank" dishonors a "complying draft" under an irrevocable credit, any holder to whom the credit extends by its terms may recover the face amount of the draft and has with respect to any documents the rights of a person in the position of a seller. 

We are not certain of the effect of this provision. Is it intended that the holder of the so-called "complying draft" shall have both a right to obtain payment of the face amount of the draft plus a right to withhold or stop delivery and recover incidental damages under 2-707? If that is the effect, isn't the subsection in conflict with subsection (3) which states that recovery shall in no event exceed the amount of the credit or the draft as the case may be? We also inquire whether the bank is to be liable for the full amount of the draft under all conceivable circumstances regardless of the amount of damage suffered by the holder. See O'Meara Co. v. National Park Bank, 239 N. Y. 386 at p. 400. We point out again that the Section refers to an "issuing bank" whereas we assume that in this instance the intention is to include also a "confirming bank." 

17. 5-117 contains an extraordinary provision, under which, in the event of the insolvency of the issuing, advising or confirming bank, or of the bank which has for a customer procured issuance of a credit by another, the beneficiary of the credit obtains priority under certain circumstances to funds set aside for the credit.

It seems probable that this provision would not be effective so far as national banks are concerned. Moreover, it would probably not be effective to alter the Bankruptcy Law with respect to a person other than a bank which issues or confirms a documentary credit or which is authorized to honor drafts under a credit. Such a person under 5-102 (2) is a bank for the purpose of the Article. The provision, likewise, raises serious questions of policy. A party holding a cashier's check is not entitled to priority in the event of the bank's insolvency even though the check was purchased with cash.

Is there any justification for giving the beneficiary of a credit greater rights? 

18. Article 5 is affected by other provisions of the Code. 

For example: (i) Sections 2-319 through 2-324 define such trade terms as f.o.b., f.a.s., c.i.f., etc. In at least one instance, Comment 7 to 2-320, the Comment recognizes that the definition changes the common law. In other instances the definitions vary from the definitions contained in the Revised American Foreign Trade Definitions, 1941, adopted by the Chamber of Commerce of the United States of America, the National Council of American Importers, Inc., and the National Foreign 

Trade Council, Inc., as well as the definitions adopted by the 

Department of Agriculture under authority of Congressional act. (ii) Under the definition of "holder in due course" in 

3-302, a holder in order to be a holder in due course must not have notice of any defense against the instrument on the part of any person. In view of this definition it is questionable whether a bank which negotiates a draft with “noncomplying documents" can be a holder in due course. Under 

5-103 the definition of holder in due course is made expressly applicable to Article 5. (iii) The definition of bill of lading in 1-201 (6) conflicts with Article 20 of the Uniform Customs and Practice and again presents a problem to a bank governed by the Code in dealing with foreign banks and out-of-state banks which follow custom and practice. 

In summary, the Subcommittee of the New York Clearing House Association has found that Article 5 contains a number of ambiguities, a number of conflicts with existing custom and practice and several very undesirable changes in the principles of letters of credit, and that as written, and apart from other reasons, the proposed Article is not satisfactory. Apart from the unsatisfactory features of Article 5, we have concluded that it is highly undesirable for any one or more states to attempt legislation in this particular area of trade and finance. 

The Banks which are members of this Subcommittee have very active and very large letter of credit departments and close contact with trade and business here and abroad which use the letter of credit device. We know of no demand or need for legislation and it is our experience that the institutions and persons who use documentary letters of credit are for the most part thoroughly familiar with this financing medium and with its terms, customs and practices.

The proposed Article deals with only a very small area in the letter of credit field and even were it to be so limited and to correctly state recognized customs and practices here and abroad, we feel that legislation would inevitably lead to rigidity. The usefulness of the letter of credit device depends upon its flexibility and adaptability to many kinds of transactions and developing trade practices in this country and abroad. We feel that banks and businesses which engage in letter of credit transactions must deplore any attempt to crystallize by statute the device and the related customs and practices. Finally, since letters of credit involve, in the main, international and interstate transactions, involving a number of parties and institutions, we believe that the opportunities for conflicts of laws and for misunderstandings and controversies between parties would be greatly augmented by attempted legislation in this field by one or more states in this country and that the ability of banking institutions in such states to furnish this important service to world-wide trade and business would be seriously impaired.

MR. HORACE M. CHADSEY [Vice President, First National Bank of Boston] As you know, I appear here by invitation, and it is only on that account that I have overcome the diffidence I might otherwise feel about trying to impress on a purely New York situation certain opinions that originate in Massachusetts. 

I presume that Mr. Peal has invited me to be present here and testify before you because of a certain familiarity that I have. acquired with letter of credit operations over a considerable span of years, and that familiarity comes first from having been connected with the Foreign Division of the First National Bank of Boston for something in excess of thirty-five years. 

It comes, additionally, from having served over a period of years on the Committee of the Bankers Association for Foreign Trade, which deals with uniformity in documents and practice, and from having served on a similar committee of the International Chamber of Commerce, which is styled the Committee on Banking Techniques of the United States Council. And then, finally, from having been a member of the Special Committee appointed by the Bankers Association for Foreign Trade in 1951 to consult with the Editorial Board with respect to the Article on Letters of Credit then in preparation for the Uniform Code. 

The background of this entire matter, I think, is one which is interesting and refreshing. It is a field in which there is relative absence of statutory law. Little seems to exist. There is not a great deal of case law as contrasted with other areas in the commercial field, and happily enough there has been very little litigation.

The absence of that litigation, I think-to the commercial community, comprising merchants and bankers, in any event-is a situation devoutly to be desired but I do not think that it springs

from the sweet reasonableness of bankers or of their customers, but I think it arises, rather, from the existence of a self-imposed set of rules which fortunately have worked very well. 

The operation of letters of credit in the United States has presumably been known to our banks really from their beginning, but it was not until the passage of the Federal Reserve Act in 1913 that a broadened base was provided by reason of the provisions in that Act for the creation of bankers' acceptances, and bankers acceptances frequently, although not necessarily, grow out of commercial letters of credit.

Following almost immediately upon the heels of that enabling legislation, came the impetus given to American international banking by the first world war, and so in the years which succeeded, there are a great many more letter of credit operations undertaken by American banks on their own responsibility, as distinguished from employing the intervention of European banks than had earlier been the case.

That activity produced almost immediately an indication that the field was rife with chances for misunderstanding, dispute, possible litigation, and that some sort of controlling rules needed. to be established. So that as early as 1920, the New York Bankers 

Commercial Credit Conference adopted a set of rules for application in the letter of credit business, and proceeded to follow them with considerable faithfulness for a number of years. But, inasmuch as letter of credit operations are traditionally and historically inclined to be of an international nature, rather than purely domestic, although there is no inherent limitation that should keep them out of the domestic field, it became evident that a set of rules which were not solely those of the New York banks but which had international acceptance, would have a utility, and the International Chamber of Commerce proceeded to undertake formulation of such a set. 

That eventually came into being under the style of Uniform Customs which was issued first in 1933, and some five years later, in 1938, adopted by the United States banks in substitution for their own earlier set, adopted however—and I think this point is of some interest-with certain reservations as to the practice of American banks as distinguished from those European banks whose habits. were particularly reflected in these rules. That is of interest because the existence in the United States, and particularly in New York, of practices which were at variance with those which were common in Europe was, on the one hand, not considered to be destructive to letter of credit operations, and on the other hand, was sufficiently important to American banks to be worth preserving, even though difference was created. 

The evolutionary process continued, and over the course of the next few years various deficiencies, changes in practice, and different approaches to these letter of credit operations made desirable the revision and refinement of the international set of rules, and such a revision came about in 1951 under the aegis of a 13th Congress of the International Chamber of Commerce, and that

revision was quite promptly adopted by American banks effective as of January 1, 1952.

I cite that background because I think it is important that throughout this period great reliance was placed by the banks, and by the commercial community (although the commercial community less voluntarily, and under the leadership of the banks) on the existence and the reasonably uniform application of these rules.

The rules were adhered to with great faithfulness on the part of American banks, and although there were differences that arose, and there were disputes to be resolved, for the most part litigation was successfully avoided. However, these rules are rather curious instruments in themselves. They represent an attempt to find a common ground between somewhat conflicting national practices, and between confusing phrases which were peculiar to different languages, and in translation didn't lend themselves to a clear interpretation. 

They are a combination of statements of principle, assertions of intent, definition of terms, a rather curious conglomeration which, in their entirety, make up a working manual, and a very useful one.

They have the advantage of international acceptance-although that is not complete, because the nations in the British Commonwealth for the most part elected not to adopt them, even though in a great many respects their practice parallels that set up by rules and they have the advantage of flexibility and relatively easy change if conditions and practices suggest such a change, as evidenced by the revision that I cited a few moments ago. 

They have the deficiency, however, of lacking any authoritative application. How you make those rules really applicable in the event of dispute, by what contract, by what notice, by what acceptance by the parties to the dispute, I think lies in the field of considerable obscurity, and they can boast actually neither legal precision nor a rhetorical perfection.

I think it was in part for those reasons that when the Uniform Code came to be drafted with respect to the letter of credit Article, no attempt was made to incorporate in it these Uniform Customs which had been working so successfully. They wouldn't have been acceptable to any board of editorial competence and legal scrutiny because of their conglomerate nature, as I mentioned. Furthermore, they had in them a great many features which were of consequence in the daily routine, but didn't represent matters of fundamental principle. Now, that daily routine might be changed, and it might be desirable to permit it to change. 

It was certainly not desired that rigidity should be attained by statutory process which would freeze forever the form of letters of credit and of behavior thereunder. The letter of credit has been an extraordinarily flexible instrument, capable of being tailored to a particular transaction. That is where its value has become so enhanced for the commercial community, and to freeze it would be unfortunate, but to state those fundamental principles on which it rests perhaps would be helpful.

In any event, in the end, the Article which was inserted in the Uniform Code is not, to my mind, in the least revolutionary. I do not think that it is in conflict with the Uniform Customs. On the contrary, it is my own belief that out of these Uniform Customs, the essence of the legal philosophy, the hard core of the legal principle that is applicable to letters of credit, has been distilled and restated in the Uniform Code.

We have left-I say "we" in perhaps a sense that I have no business to use it, but only because these matters were the subject of prolonged discussion during the drafting sessions of the Editorial Board, some of which I was privileged to attend. But there has been left to the Uniform Customs those aspects of practice which seem to be in the shape of a working manual, rather than of basic philosophy. And only those basic principles have been codified which it seemed worth while making available to the commercial community in a readily ascertainable form, that community being the merchant, the banker and the lawyer. It is surprisingly restricted at the moment, I think, despite the existence of these Uniform Customs over the years.

There are in the United States some 14,000 commercial banks, and that is after the curtailment which occurred in the early 1930's. But out of those 14,000 banks, there are scarcely more than 

100 who do any international business of consequence, and out of that 100 banks, there are probably twenty-five who handle 75 per cent of that business. It is really an amazing concentration of knowledge, skill, know-how, ability to give technical performance. 

In the Code, there are a number of provisions, most of which I have gathered this morning are objectionable, unacceptable, and have little merit. In fact only, perhaps, that one which stipulated that documentary credits deal with documents and not with goods escaped criticism.

There are, however, some provisions that seemed to me consequential and valuable. The one which I just mentioned is fundamentally important, the emphasis on the documentary aspect, and the freedom from entanglement with the underlying contract between the buyer and the seller, or the goods that are delivered thereunder.

I think it is important, too, that the distinction be made between credits which are irrevocable and those that are revocable, and that proper emphasis be given to the nature of an irrevocable credit and to the hazards that are attendant upon a revocable credit, because experience in the banking business has indicated that the beneficiary of the credit frequently is not sufficiently conscious of the marked difference in the sort of promise he obtains between an irrevocable and a revocable letter of credit. 

Flexibility has been preserved even in that restricted amount of philosophy which it is suggested be enacted into law by reason of inserting in a great many instances indeed those words "unless otherwise agreed" still making it possible for the parties to a letter of credit to contrive a situation, a device, an instrument, which will accomplish what they want it to accomplish, even though it varies somewhat from the interpretation which the law would

give to purely general phrases, should they resort only to them. 

It is important, too, I think, that the principle be stated that there can be no reservation of a lien by a seller on the documents which he tenders under a letter of credit, if he receives payment under that letter of credit. To have a situation which was contrary to that would be an attempt on the part of a seller to eat his cake and still to have it. To make a delivery to a buyer for an amount presumably in excess of what the buyer had contemplated paying, since his credit had not been opened for the complete amount to obtain payment to the extent possible under the letter of credit, and still to retain control over those documents, so as to prevent the buyer from obtaining what he had paid for until he made still a further payment-that would be in conflict with the entire objective of a letter of credit, and to have its impossibility stated in the law, I think, clearly is of value. 

I think there is merit, too, in the Section which deals with the issuance of indemnities. Indemnities lie certainly in a twilight zone of banking capacity. It is difficult to determine at what point they cease to be a natural, normal, necessary, perhaps a self-preservative function of the banking business, and at what point they become an intrusion into the surety field. Here is a situation where a great many cases arise with discrepancies from the required terms of the letter of credit, discrepancies that are technically important but commercially insignificant. 

To enable the commercial transaction which the credit was intended to facilitate, to go ahead, rather than to obstruct it by rejection of documents for technical inadequacies, and to enable that to be accomplished by reason of such an indemnity, is to foster the objective at which credit was aimed without violating the fundamentally protective provisions for which the credit was created.

To recognize that that sort of an indemnity isn't a departure from what is normal or appropriate with banks, I think is to endow it with a certain dignity and blessing. To be sure, to enact it in this statute would be directly helpful only to those banks established under state charter. But by association of ideas and the company that is kept, it may be persuasive for national banks as well. Since, as I mentioned, this is definitely a twilight zone in a debatable area, some light may result as to the use of indemnities by national banks in that situation. Actually, the practice does exist, right or wrong. That is the way it is done, and this is a recognition of an actuality. 

There are some new concepts in the letter of credit Article, too. One of these is the provision of three days for the examination of documents. Whether the penalty for having taken no decision with respect to documents in a single day is as severe as some of us have been inclined to feel, and that some of the opponents of the legislation have indicated is not the case, I really don't know. But I do know that what has happened in actuality is that during periods of stress, national emergency or war, when the exchange of goods has ballooned up in tremendously rapid

fashion, there have been presented to banks, and primarily banks in New York—and if I might put in a parenthetical observation, this provision is of much less consequence to banks in Massachusetts, and I think to the banks in probably any other state in the Union, than it is to those in New York, because here it is that the concentration of this business lies. Here it is that the emergency becomes most common. 

We in Massachusetts can still live in contemplation of having to give an answer, affirmative or negative, as to the acceptability of certain documents within twenty-four hours. In New York, they cannot; they don't. They run for luck. Whether that has been very hazardous or not, I am not in a position to determine. 

But this provision in the Code at least was a recognition that that reality actually did exist. 

It was inserted on the urging of New York bankers, and it contemplates allowing them a period during which documents may be examined in a sufficiently leisurely and careful manner to come to a reasoned conclusion. It prevents their having to take the protective device of rejecting the documents for some deficiency, real or fancied, in order to give themselves an opportunity to have them re-presented, or left with them for further examination, because it isn't ordinarily the case that the person who presents the documents stands by impatiently, first on one foot and then the other, waiting for an immediate decision. He is usually reasonable about it and prepared to wait for a time. In fact, that preparedness to wait for a time is reflected in another clause of this same Section, which indicates that not only may three banking days be utilized as a matter of inherent right, but that further time may be taken, too, with the consent, express or implied, of the presenter. That has a bearing, I think, on one of the criticisms mentioned this morning, that there is a certain amount of time required to consult with people abroad, and that to compress within three days the examination of the documents, the consultation abroad, the obtaining of instructions from abroad, is asking that it be done too promptly. 

It is three days, plus, more than they have at present, I think, and in point of fact, so long as the presenter of the documents is willing that additional time should be taken for such consultation, no difficulty arises. If he isn't willing, all he has to do is ask for his documents back. He certainly is entitled to them. 

And he may have them back perhaps without a definite statement of honor or dishonor, and that after three days during which the New York bank has had an opportunity to make an examination more careful, more fruitful, more productive than at the moment. 

Another new concept, and I think there are only two which are really new in the letter of credit business, because I should like to reiterate that in my opinion, there are no substantive differences, no real conflicts between the Uniform Customs on which so much reliance is placed, and that part of the letter of credit operations which it is proposed to enact in the Code. 

The other new concept, then, is the drawing of a distinction which is sharp and clear between the right to transfer perform-

ance, that is, the right to transfer the ability to draw under a letter of credit, and the assignment of the proceeds which will result from such a drawing.

I think there is a very real difference. The opener of a letter of credit, the buyer of the merchandise abroad, let us say-because to make it international is not only in keeping with what happens, but better illustrates the point-the buyer of the merchandise, I think, is quite privileged to say that he is willing to have performance under the letter of credit which he establishes only by that person in whom he has already had sufficient confidence, about whom he has already known enough, to justify his making a contract in the first instance, and that it isn't fair to him that that person should transfer the fulfillment of the contract to someone else, unknown to the buyer, and possibly project him into a situation which would be unhappy, by reason of inadequate deliveries, insufficient deliveries, and various other failures to perform. I think that we have to recognize that and the Code has recognized it by saying that such a transfer of the right to draw, the right to perform, is possible only if it says so in the letter of credit, and that means only if it is satisfactory to the bank which has issued the credit, and that in turn means only if it is satisfactory to the buyer who has come to the bank to ask that the credit be issued.

At the same time, the beneficiaries of the letters of credit have protested very stoutly, and I think with fairness, that once they have such an instrument in their hands, once they have such assurance of ability to collect the proceeds of the sale which they have made, they ought to be able to take advantage of its bankability. It ought to have some value to them as a source of raising funds with which to acquire or fabricate the merchandise which they have sold, and which they must now deliver. That seems not unreasonable either, and so it is contemplated in the Code that if the beneficiary wants to assign his rights to what he will get out of the performance, he may.

Then you have the question of who is going to police the transaction, and the commissions which are exacted by banks in this letter of credit business are very small. They are very fractional, in the order of 8, 10, 4 of one per cent. They are possible only on the basis of handling a volume of business and handling it in as expeditious and as little troublesome a way as possible. To become involved in notices of assignment, to have to put red flags on letters of credit, set them aside, give them a treatment which varies from that which is ordinary and traditional, is to incur additional expense. It is probable to have to resort to counsel as each situation comes up, and it is the sort of thing which the banks don't want to get involved with. It appeared in the course of the drafting sessions that banks would be quite likely to avoid altogether letter of credit transactions which were likely to project them into that sort of a situation, rather than to take them on for the small remuneration which they got. 

And so it is contemplated in the Code that banks may, if they choose, disregard this assignment of the proceeds. As between

2 the man who assigned it and the one who received it, the rights exist which are recited in Article 9. But with reference to the banks who are implementing a letter of credit, the choice lies with them as to whether they try to police the transaction or don't. 

They wouldn't try to police it in any way, as a practical matter, unless someone interested in this phase of the operation comes to them and makes a special arrangement with them, in short, offers them sufficient compensation to justify their assigning to the letter of credit the personnel, competence and skill needed in order to control it.

I don't know that I can deal with satisfactory effectiveness with some of the critical matters which were recited this morning. 

Of course, in my own observations, I have commented on some of them. I have cited my belief that there is no essential conflict between the Code and Customs. Certainly I know it was the attempt of the Editorial Board to avoid that conflict. Not from the beginning, not initially. There were other ideas, other concepts, other approaches, taken into account initially, but in the end, I think the editors were persuaded that nothing should be attempted that was revolutionary, that in this field operations had gone on in a very satisfactory way, and that all that was indicated was a codification of those principles on which the commercial community already relied. 

One reference was made to the possibly confusing aspect and the possible inadequacy of a remark in the Comments which indicated that in the event of need, a court might attempt to discover what were the uniform customs prevailing between banks. The reference to uniform customs appears in small letters, not related to these Uniform Customs which is the style of a set of rules, which 

I previously mentioned.

I think that sort of conclusion was inevitable on the part of the draftsmen. I believe, although I have no legal skill, that it is a well established principle of law that when the statute is not sufficiently clear or sufficiently comprehensive, it is helpful to the court to discover what is the practice in the trade, and to endeavor to apply that, having in mind that that presumably was the intent of the participants in the transaction. That, it seems to me, is precisely what this. Comment contemplates, and the fact that there are Uniform Customs under such a style and name may be extremely helpful in determining what is the uniform custom in the trade. But since the Uniform Customs, as a set of rules, are the sort of a document that I have earlier indicated, it would seem to me manifestly impossible to incorporate them in their entirety into the proposed law, even by reference, and to do so, I think, would be most unfortunate, because there you would achieve the rigidity, you would lose the flexibility which is desired to preserve for the letter of credit business.

I might comment, too, on the observation that it made little sense to provide that the undertaking of a bank in a letter of credit might not be negated by a general stipulation that all documents be satisfactory to the bank, and yet at the same time to concede. that certain documents might be required to be presented in a form

satisfactory to the bank. There again I think there is a difference. 

If the intent of the letter of credit is to substitute for direct relationships between the buyer and the seller a third party, with somewhat detached interests and abundant financial responsibility, who presumably has certain impartiality in his approach to documentary examination and certainly has a prestige of his own. to preserve-if that is the intent, then to have a letter of credit say in one breath that if there is compliance with provisions A, B and C, then payment will be made, and to have it say, at the same time, "If I like the form of the documents this morning, and that will depend on their color or my breakfast," I think is to negate the whole concept and the approach and objective. That is why such an admonition appears.

That is not quite the same thing as permitting an issuing bank to stipulate that with respect to some particular document, it may be essential that approval be reserved until the document can be examined. There are plenty of cases of that sort that may arise, and given the flexible aspect of the letter of credit, it is desirable that they should be recognized.

It is possible, for example, that a certification of inspection is desired. It is possible that the point at which inspection will take place is too remote, too small, to have established an internationally known inspector. It may be perfectly reasonable, therefore, that the bank issuing the credit stipulate that that certificate of inspection must be in a form or by an inspector satisfactory to it, because only after it has seen the instrument can it exercise reasonable judgment with respect to it. 

And you should also bear this in mind, that no beneficiary has ever had to accept a letter of credit that had provisions in it that he didn't like. Acceptance of the letter of credit is purely voluntary on his part. If he gets one that has stipulations in it that seem unfair, not capable of compliance, not in accord for that matter with his underlying contract, all he has to do is to say, "I don't like it;

I won't take it, and I won't function under it." 

I think, then, there is nothing left for me to do but relieve you of my observations, and to sum the thing up. It seems to me that the letter of credit Article is not revolutionary, that it is in substantial agreement with the best American practice which has developed over the last third of a century and is now pretty much reflected by the Uniform Customs and Practices of the International Chamber of Commerce; that it takes from that rather rambling instrument what I call legal principle and philosophy and incorporates that into the law; that the working manual aspects, the day-by-day variations are left to the Customs. where they belong, where they are capable of modification as the necessity arises. Flexibility is thereby preserved and it makes these basic principles available to the whole community in a form which is casily ascertainable and easily understandable. 

Thank you.
