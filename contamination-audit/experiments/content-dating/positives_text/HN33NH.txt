A statement of the form "If is called a conditional, the first factor being referred to as the antecedent, the second as the consequent. Arguments which follow the pattern of example II consist of a conditional premise, a further premise asserting its antecedent, and a conclusion which asserts its consequent. These are always valid. The argument of example IV depends on denying the antecedent of a conditional premise in order to deny its consequent as a conclusion. This is called the fallacy of denying the antecedent.

A fallacy is a type of argument which presents the appearance of being formally correct, but is not. The factor of apparent correctness is important in this connection for there are an endless number of ways of drawing incorrect conclusions and it is impossible to find ways of classifying most of them, in fact very few errors in reasoning have been given special designations. The only way of explaining a fallacy is to show that it suggests a type of argument which would actually give the required conclusion.
