1 Thomas Haley's first recorded appearance in Maine was in 1640. He was a juryman at "Saco in the Province of Mayne" in the [second] General Court, held on the eighth day of September of that year. Among his fellow jurymen was Mr. John 

West, who was a planter under Mr. Richard Vines and whose daughter Mary Thomas Haley married. Haley was probably a West-of-England man, similar names Hayley, Hele, and 

Hearle - being firmly established in Devon and Cornwall.*

Andrew Haley of the neighboring town of Kittery may have been a near relative, although proof is lacking. 

Thomas Haley was among the inhabitants of Saco who acknowledged on July 5, 1653, that they were subject to the government of Massachusetts. At a town-meeting following the submission of Saco to Puritan control, held on July 12, 1653, "Thomas Haile is granted 2 akors of marsh next Puding point in that marsh at the end of the railes in the Cove," and shares an acre of upland immediately adjoining with his father-in-law. 

His farm was on the west bank of the Saco near its mouth, where the house erected by his grandson, Deacon Benjamin Haley, still stands. Thomas Haley acted as ferryman between Winter Harbor and the settlement of Captain Bonython and Mr. Lewis on the east bank of the river, and in 1654 the town book records that "Thomas Haile is allowed to take of every one yt he setts over ye river 2 d." 

Mary (West) Haley was buried "10 month 24, 1658," leaving four children. In 1660, “a cricke Comanly Called by the name of Thomas Hawly's Cricke" is mentioned as a boundary,* the same landmark as is mentioned in 1681† as “a certain small water runne called Halys Gutt, being the Antient bound Marked between the aforesaid Bonighton, or his father, & the sayd Gibbins." Saco. Two years after the latter date, Thomas Haile was among those "assessed for ye prison," with a share of four shillings, one of the smallest sums recorded. An entry in the town book for 1672 in which Thomas Haile is paid £2, and another in which "Thomas Heyley hath Received of Jon Helson Constable ye sum of ten shillen for ye yere 1671" do not make clear whether the sums were given as alms or taken as collections by Haley in the office of constable, which position he held later, in 1683.¶ The will of George Knight of Scarbrough mentioned a debt due to Thomas Hayley, which he received in 1672. In 1673, Thomas Haley was ordered by the court "for the more secure transportation of travellers, for men and horses, to provide a good sufficient boat fit for carrying persons and their horses, large enough to carry over three horses at one time." On December 8, 1681, he was granted sufficient land to make his house lot fifty acres.

The last authentic date of any kind about Thomas Haley is on July 13, 1685, when he was chosen with John Davis and 

George Page "to lay out or run ye Town bounds between Cap.

Porpus & Saco." However, it may be properly inferred, from a reference to his son as "Thomas Haley the younger," that he was living in 1687. If it was Thomas, Sr., who was fined for beating the constable (See p. 7), he must have been enjoying a vigorous old age a year later; if the second Thomas had then become Thomas Haley, the death date of Thomas Haley the first seems fixed.

Folsom states§ that Thomas Haley was killed at Winter Harbor during one of the Indian raids in 1724. He does not specify that this was the Thomas Haley of the early settlement, but every other Thomas Haley, for three succeeding generations, is fully accounted for. Mr. Folsom's source is unknown. 

If the Winter Harbor victim was the first Thomas Haley, he must have met his death at a very advanced age. He must have reached at least his majority when he served as juror in 1640, a calculation which would place his birth year not later than 1619. Historically, the best that can be said for the theory that Thomas Haley, the emigrant, survived until 1724 is that it is barely within the bounds of possibility. 

By local tradition, Thomas Haley, going out for the family cow at nightfall, was lured into the woods by the cow's bell in the hands of the Indians, there surprised, and hacked to pieces.

June, 1688, he was fined £5 for beating William Dicer, constable, and recorded as Thomas Haley. This misdemeanor may have been committed by Thomas Haley, Sr., but as the culprit was also "bound in the Penall Sum of Ten Pounds to be of good Behaviour... untill the Next Quarter Sessions," it does not seem as if the older man could have been so combative. 

During the Indian war which broke out in 1689, Haley, who was a sergeant in the military force of Saco, remained at the garrison near the falls in the Saco River. Here, after witnessing six years of frontier alarms and depredations, he fell a victim to an Indian attack in the summer of 1695. The diary of Rev. 

John Pike states, "1695 Aug. Serj: Tho: Haly killd p Indns a little out of Saco-Fort," and Rev. Cotton Mather, in his account of the local warfare, entitled "Decennium Luctuosum," records that "Sergeant Haley, Venturing out of his Fort at Saco, Stept into the Snares of Death." In 1745 the New England colonists under the leadership of Sir William Pepperell undertook what is known as the Louisburg expedition against the French. Folsom, writing when several of Deacon Haley's grandchildren were still living, states that in spite of his age, he entered the service, and died of fever at Louisburg. In 1790, Samuel Haley, an ambitious and energetic man, moved from Winter Harbor to the Plantation of Little Falls (now Hollis), where he engaged in the lumber business for many years. He lived at first in a small log house near "Great Brook," on which his saw-mill was located. A story illustrative of the hardships of that day is told of him. The family being short of food at one time, he crossed the river on a log and walked to Saco, to purchase a supply of provisions, which he carried back to Hollis, a distance of some ten miles, on his back. This journey he made without food for himself, except for a little porridge in the morning before he started.

His early holdings have been occupied by the family since 1790, and are now represented by the Sumner O. Haley homestead in Hollis, Maine.

This Samuel Haley figures in the story of a famous wreck, which took place January 14, 1813, when a Spanish ship stranded on Smuttynose in the night, in the midst of a fearful snowstorm. No one knew of the wreck until morning, and all hands were lost. The Gosport records call the ship the Sagunto, but

Samuel Haley, testifying before the General Court of Massachusetts January 12, 1818, names it the Conception from Cadiz.

The ship is described as made of mahogany and cedar, but old and rotten. Both stories agree that fourteen bodies were found on shore, or "grappled up" in the straits between the islands, and tradition says they were buried by Samuel Haley on Smuttynose, and their places marked by little stones, still visible.

UNIDENTIFIED HALEYS, POSSIBLY MEMBERS OF THE BIDDEFORD FAMILY 

Joseph Haley, born June 10, 1752,* married Esther Towne, int. January 27, 1776, in Wells. She was born March 13, 1751.

He was probably the Joseph Haley, adult, who was baptized March 15, 1772. He removed to Topsham, where he passed the remainder of his life, and was known as "Fuller" Haley. 

Bradbury, in his History of Kennebunkport, says of Benjaman³ Haley: "His son, John, married a daughter of Capt. John

Fairfield and was residing in this town as late as 1764. A son of 

John, who was a clothier, married Ruth Towne and moved east.”

This statement of Mr. Bradbury's is most inconclusive since no search to the present time has produced anything to support it, while on the other hand, it contains several errors. The name of Joseph Haley's wife is known to be Esther, not Ruth. 

Thomas Haley (Benjamin³), not John, married a step-daughter of John Fairfield, who was appointed administrator, at her request, when her husband died in 1747.‡ In 1762, Samuel Haley sold to his brother Joseph Haley his right in his brother John's share in their father's estate.§ In 1771, Molly Lord and Olive 

Haley, daughters of Thomas Haley, sold to Robert Haley (William*, Benjamin³) all their interest in the estates of their grandfather Benjamin and their uncle John Haley.¶ As his brother and nieces had an interest in John Haley's estate it would seem to be indisputable that he died without legitimate issue.

That "Fuller" Haley was closely related to the Thomas Haley family, however, would seem to be indicated by the fact that Olive³ (Haley) Jardine, daughter of Thomas¹ Haley of Wells, left property by will to "her friend" Apphia (Snow) Haley, wife of "Fuller" Haley's son Jesse.** 

Joseph Haley was a clothier. Family tradition says he came to Saco when the first textile mills were established in 1826, to teach his dyeing methods. He died September 29, 1832, in Topsham.
