Foreword

Recent public interest in a state children's psychiatric treatment center dates from 1942 when Dr. Bert Nash, psychologist in the 

School of Education, University of Kansas, initiated a psychological testing service in the state institutions. He and his students discovered numbers of children inappropriately placed in institutions with programs not designed for their specific needs. Concerned about these misplaced children, Doctor Nash proposed a state diagnostic center. In 1943 the Legislature established the Kansas Receiving Home for Children as a diagnostic study center. As this facility was used by courts and agencies, it soon became apparent that diagnostic study was not enough. Many of the recommendations made could not be followed for lack of available treatment facilities. 

A Children's Treatment Center was proposed by the Kansas Council for Children and Youth as early as 1945. But the Legislature was not yet receptive to this idea. Attempts were made to establish treatment facilities on a private basis in some communities. 

Existing institutional programs were extended to provide limited treatment services. A Treatment Center for mentally and emotionally disturbed children was proposed each year in the Legislature. 

In 1952, the Kansas Council for Children and Youth and its member agencies succeeded in winning support from the public and the legislators. Finally, in 1953 a law was passed establishing the Treatment Center for Children. However, funds appropriated were insufficient, and construction was deferred until the 1955 legislature passed an additional appropriation. 

The law required the Center to be built on land already owned by the state. A committee appointed to select the site decided to erect the buildings on the grounds of the Topeka State Hospital, far removed from the adult area. Construction began June, 1955 on two cottages for 15 children each and an administration-adjunctive therapy building. As the autonomous Treatment Center for 

Children went into construction, plans had to be drawn for program and services; decisions had to be made on treatment philosophy and functions. The law provided for diagnosis and treatment of emotionally, mentally, and socially disturbed children on a short- (6)

term basis.

How would this new Treatment Center fit in with existing diagnostic and treatment services in the other state institutions for children? It seemed a good time to take a look at the present residential child care and treatment institutions in the state to determine just what institutional services for children we already had, and to decide how these might best be integrated with the functions of the new Center.

To do this the State Board of Social Welfare and the Director of Institutions appointed a committee of three consultants from the 

Menninger Foundation Department of Child Psychiatry, Dr. J. 

Cotter Hirschberg, psychiatrist; Dr. Walter Kass, clinical psychologist and Dr. Mary Ella Wheeler, psychiatric social worker. Their assignment was to survey all the institutions for children in the state and then present a plan of organization for the new Treatment Center most suited to the needs of the total institutional program. 

The consulant team visited each of the seven residential institutions for children, conferred with the superintendent and staff, and surveyed the institutional functions, programs, and problems. 

These visits and discussions provided an intimate view of each institution and how its legislatively defined function was interpreted in policy, staff, and program. Before visiting, the consultants had access to the biennial reports and other publications for each institution. In addition, detailed statistical data were requested in advance, which, when analyzed, frequently pointed to significant areas of inquiry. Various reports of previous surveys and visits by other officials, organizations, and departments were likewise studied, including reports made by the State Department of Health, the Kansas Council for Children and Youth, the Kansas State Teacher's Association, and several legislative committees. Many conferences were also held with individuals who, by reason of their official positions, had intimate knowledge of the institutions, their programs, and community problems relating to the institutions.

The objective was to recommend an organizational structure for the new  most consistent with the total institutional child care program and based on an analysis of exising institutions. PART I Summary of Observations and Conclusions A. GENERAL COMMENTS 

Our observations and discussions with children, staff, and superintendents indicate that all institutions in Kansas providing residential care to children have moved or are moving away from the static concept of custodial care. All have incorporated to varying degrees the rehabilitative concept that institutional care is a method of treatment. At all institutions a large number of children were described as having emotional problems associated with separation from their parents or with the particular condition which necessitated institutional placement. On the problems of emotional disturbance we found a common philosophy of treatment goals and ways of helping children.

Some of the elements contributing to the development of a common child-care and treatment philosophy are: 

1. A growing similarity in the problems of children referred to all institutions. 

2. The gradual expansion of available foster home placements for children who need no longer remain in the institution. 

3. Earlier recognition and understanding of serious personality disorders. 

4. Increased knowledge about how to help disturbed children. 

In some institutions, children are still kept in care even though they present needs or problems requiring special services which the institution itself cannot provide, and there is little evidence that their retention is part of a planned treatment. Till recently there were no facilities designated for the psychiatric treatment of children. With the opening of the Children's Section at the Topeka 

State Hospital in 1953, a treatment facility became available to which disturbed adolescents could be transferred. This unit is now filled and plans are underway to increase its capacity. Some time in 1956, the new  will become available for the younger disturbed child. Efforts by an institution's staff to give psychiatric treatment services are necessarily limited to those severely disturbed children who become too difficult to handle and who cannot safely be contained in the institution population. 

The degree to which institutions successfully manage seriously disturbed children varies from isolation and restraints to meager professional treatment already available within the institution, to (11)

transfer to a State Hospital, or a maximum security penal institution in the case of Boys' Industrial School and Girls' Industrial School youngsters.

It was gratifying to observe that no institution regarded itself as an end point (except for cases of the most extremely retarded or physically handicapped), and notable efforts are made to reduce the length of stay. In those institutions serving the severely retarded and most seriously physically handicapped child, recognition was expressed that the institution would become the way of life for these children. Some institutions still suffer from having to do too much of the rehabilitation job alone. In some cases, there is an effort to provide those after-care services which the county welfare departments seem unable to make available at this time. 

Everywhere continuous efforts are made to overcome the barrier which has traditionally separated an institution from its surrounding community. All attempt to utilize available community resources as an adjunctive part of the institution program so that seldom is a child completely cut off or isolated from normal community experiences. A few localities are unable to provide as much of a variety of community experiences as might be wished. Within their own limits, however, interested community groups and individuals are volunteering their time and homes to provide these children opportunities to experience some degree of normal family living which often serves as motivation for the child to seek and later sustain release from institutional life.

Considerable concern was expressed by institution staffs about the lack of before-and-after-institutional-care, social, diagnostic, and treatment services. Our studies reveal that many children in institutions could be placed in foster homes, that rehabilitative work with parents would facilitate discharge of more children to their own homes, and that still others might not have needed placement at all if preventive casework had been done.

The cost of building new institutions could be saved, and present institutional overcrowding reduced, if ways could be found to implement the very important before-and-after-care services to disturbed children and disrupted families to the end of helping children remain with their own parents whenever possible. Responsibility for these services is at present assigned to the county welfare departments. Effectiveness of our child care program should not be measured by the number of children placed in institutions but rather by the number helped to remain at home and the number of families

reunited after placement. In general, institutions are greatly, and in our opinion unnecessarily, limited in their freedom to return the child to his own home, or to some kind of family care within the community for those who cannot be reunited, because the institution is entirely dependent for these social services on the county welfare departments.

Almost all institutions expressed a desire to be part of a more co-ordinated administrative unit in the total child welfare program. 

All institutions now face a major problem in securing active participation of the county welfare departments in cases of children designated as ready for foster placement. A few institutions have succeeded in establishing a limited working relationship with welfare departments in some counties. 

Prior to a child's admission, county welfare departments have responsibility for securing a social history of the child, his family, and his activities and relationships in the community. When such histories are delayed or inadequately compiled and essential information is lacking, the institution is handicapped in its diagnostic function and in devising a program to meet the child's needs. 

Whenever possible, institution staffs actively seek participation of the parents in the child's treatment program. This is done through encouraging parent visits to the child and correspondence with the parents to keep them informed of the child's progress. 

While the child is in the institution, county welfare workers have the remaining responsibility to work with the parents of children who are removed from their care because of the child's disorder or parental inability to meet the child's needs. Unless continued social services are provided for the purpose of maintaining and reconstructing family life, many children cannot be returned to their own homes but will require a foster family home or residential group care under the auspices of the state. 

All institutions have established some diagnostic procedures for children admitted to care. All give psychological tests in an effort to appraise the child's potential. Social histories are requested from the county welfare departments, and necessary pertinent information is secured by social work staffs from the child or from his parents when they visit. At some point during the child's orientation to institutional life, he is also seen by a psychiatrist or other medical staff.

The effectiveness and extent of the in-service training programs vary, but in all institutions there is need for more intensive continu-

ous training for those employees in direct care of the children. 

Some institutions use their own professional staff to give courses and lectures to cottage personnel in order to apply psychiatric concepts of child growth and development throughout the program. 

Staff conferences are a regularly scheduled part of their programs, and various ways are devised for sharing with the cottage child care personnel the results of the diagnostic examinations. 

Most institutions try to form homogeneous groups by assigning children to cottages according to common needs. But this effort is handicapped when inadequate buildings and overcrowding result in lack of alternative choices. 

Concern was expressed in the larger institutions that their program may lack flexibility to individually meet the wide variety of needs of all children committed to care. Within the limitation of program structure, however, effort is made to plan for a child in relation to his individual needs, although often a child may be required to fit into the established program. 

The concept that the skills of the several professional disciplines is necessary to a sound child-care and treatment program is accepted throughout the institutions. In each, the value of this multi-discipline approach to the understanding of a child is evidenced in program development. Problems of collaboration of the professional staffs, however, seem to stem primarily from varying levels of staff professional competence. Some staffs are engaged in resolving problems regarding their shared functions, but there seems to be no clear concept of the most effective way in which the different skills of the individual professional staff could be used in providing services to both child and parents. In a few institutions, staffs were attempting to define and delimit their job functions so that their efforts could be better focused in the helping process with a child. 

Many of the institutional staffs expressed a desire that more professional consultant services be provided by the State Department of Social Welfare or by the Division of Institutional Management. At present, psychiatric consultation is obtained primarily from private agencies or clinics by individual institutions. Most of the institutional staffs stated their preference that such consultation be provided from within the administrative structure of the Division of Institutions. However, fear was also expressed that if given, such central consultation might tend to weaken the institution's relative autonomy within the state program. 

Some of the important functions performed by all superintendents

and their staffs are to keep the public informed of what is going on in the institution, to interpet the institution's philosophy, to share major problems, and to actively solicit community participation in their programs. Interest in the individual institution is being stimulated and we were aware of positive response from community groups. Some institutions have moved ahead farther than others in developing an active volunteer program, utilizing interested persons in the community. Few institutions, however, had established a working group of interested individuals in the community to serve in an advisory or consultative capacity. These institutions utilizing this method viewed it as an effective way of keeping the community informed of the various problems facing the institution. 

Within the limits of their staffings, accommodations and administrative arrangements, all institutions were reasonably well run from the standpoint of physical care. No child was found to be cruelly used in the physical sense. However, in some institutions, children were being reared by unimaginative methods with little opportunity for developing their full capabilities, and with very little brightness in or interest from their surroundings. In these places the standard of child-care seemed no better, except in respect to disciplinary methods, than that of, say, 30 years ago. In some institutions because of deplorable widespread shortage of child-care staff and facilities, the child was merely one of a large crowd without any place or possession of his own, without even a quiet room to which he could retreat. More important, most children are without the feeling that there is anyone vitally interested in his welfare or who cares for him as a person. The effect of this on the smaller children was reflected in their behavior towards visitors, an almost pathetic clamoring for attention and contact.

B. PROBLEMS COMMON TO ALL INSTITUTIONS 

1. Many of the institutions are not fully using their total budgetary appropriations. In some cases, the reason for this is that they are unable to secure the needed numbers of different kinds of qualified personnel. This is due in part to the relatively low salaries established for particular positions. Therefore, although most of the institutions do fill the job positions established in their budgets, often these positions are filled by less-qualified persons. 

Each institution must recruit its own staff. In so doing it may refer to the roster in the Personnel Division of the State Department of Administration. To lessen competitiveness among the

several institutions for staff and to relieve them of the burden of individual recruiting, recruitment might be centralized on the state level. 

2. Many children have been separated from their parents for perhaps unnecessarily long periods from their early years. In such separations there is also a judgmental nonacceptance by staff of many of the parents, an attitude which causes further alienation of children from parents without substituting other family relationships for them. Each institution was aware that a child exists first within a family. Yet frequently the view expressed was that, to some degree, the institution was "better than" the parents, "better than" the home, "better than" the family. Overly-possessive attitudes were expressed by some staffs toward the children they were providing care, indicating a wish to continue their relationship with the child after he returned to community living. 

3. The institution itself cannot provide family life. Its value comes from group living in a controlled environment directing forces in a child's life so that he can be helped, as quickly as possible, to return to family living, either in his own home or in a substitute home. Not an end in itself, a good group-care program depends on other home-placement resources easily accessible so that the institution does not become simply a holding place for the child. Ideally, an institution should be integrally connected with other placement services to avoid the usual “refer, transfer," pulling and hauling between agencies to which placed children are too often exposed.

Most of the institutions have not been able to achieve an effective ongoing working relationship with the county welfare workers during the time the child is in the institution. In part, this accounts for the lack of keeping an even pace between the progress the child is making in the institution and the progress of the family in the community. For children with ties to their own families, the casework direction must always be toward return to their own home. This is best accomplished when the parents and the child are receiving help simultaneously. It requires the co-ordinated efforts of institution and county welfare department. 

One of the problems in achieving necessary co-ordination between institution and county welfare department is the geographic location of the institution. All the institutions are located in the eastern part of the state. Since the western section is almost entirely rural and has a relatively small child population, children

placed in institutions may be a considerable distance from home. 

To this problem is added the lack of funds available and the lack of freedom for county welfare workers to participate in the work of the institution. County welfare workers rarely attend conferences on the child even though they carry responsibility for all the case work before and after the child is in the institution. Since county welfare workers can rarely attend institution staff conferences, it may be desirable to have the Division of Child Welfare Services consultants assigned to those distant regions carry a more active responsibility as liaison between institution and county. 

Consultants from the Division of Child Welfare Services do carry some responsibility for consultation and limited supervision to those county welfare workers engaged in certain child welfare services. 

A further problem is the multiple-aid caseloads of county welfare workers. With excessively large caseloads and heavy responsibility for social services in the five public assistance programs, and services to the blind, as well as the specialized child welfare services, county welfare workers usually find themselves unable to provide needed intensive social service to parents of children in institutions. 

No final solution to this problem can be proposed for any one institution or the total group of institutions which now find themselves part of a scattered program of child care. There is no single position in the State Department of Social Welfare with responsibility for co-ordinating the various systems of child care. The result is disconnected services. The present arrangement of disconnected child-care services should be reviewed on the basis of broader social welfare planning. With recognition that institutions cannot do the job alone and that the public agency cannot provide all the necessary extra-mural services, a possible answer may be in greater co-ordination. Were the Division of Child Welfare 

Services field staff to carry supervisory responsibilities increased coordination might be achieved especially if the same individual were to supervise both the county welfare worker and also provide consultation to the institution caring for the child. 

4. Obtaining and holding qualified personnel is one of the most pressing problems of all the institutions. At most of the institutions the number of qualified cottage parents, quarters supervisors, teachers, recreation staff, and other personnel is inadequate. Personnel turnover rates are too high. Staff who have closest contact with the children tend to be overage and often lack minimum educational requirements. Personnel shortages and deficiencies are attributable 2-4373

to a number of factors. Outstanding are (a) limitations and tensions of institutional life, (b) very low pay, (c) insufficient career system incentives, (d) insufficient in-service training and supervision. 

5. Not all the institutions have full professional clinical teams for planning treatment, for general program planning and for inservice training. To create a therapeutic environment requires qualified personnel in the three major disciplines, psychiatry, psychology, and social work. Since qualified personnel are especially difficult to secure in the children's field, it is suggested that sufficient training funds be made available to the Division of Institutional Management for use in training present staff to qualify in the children's field, with commitment to return to the program of the Division of Institutional Management on completion of special training. 

6. There is a serious deficiency of more meaningful state-wide statistical data for use by the institutions and the Division of Institutional Management in program research and planning. Present systems of reporting and compiling statistical data are neither sufficiently comprehensive nor refined to permit the kind of evaluative interpretation most helpful to the institutions collectively and individually. Better-planned statistics would be more useful in evaluating the effectiveness of the individual institution's program. 

There is need for further research on the kinds of children who come into institutions, what happens to them during their institutional stay, and what treatment is most effective. Research is also necessary to determine specific needs of children which might better be treated in noninstitutional programs. Statistics on population growth should be correlated with present knowledge of needs so that there may be paced planning for the future. 

7. The academic educational programs at some of the institutions do not adequately meet the special training needs of many children in several respects: a. The programs are not all state accredited, and many teachers are not specially trained in the teaching of atypical and emotionally disturbed children; b. Institutions do not have sufficient professional guidance from or collaboration with public school authorities; c. Academic assignments are sometimes subordinated to pressures for assignment of children to maintenance work, particularly in the industrial schools. Most children there attend school only on a half-day basis and those over age sixteen usually receive no academic education.

d. Vocational and academic programs at many of the institutions are not fully integrated. These programs tend to operate independently, and individual assignments to vocational and academic training are neither closely related nor complementary. 

The State Division of Institutional Management should consider requesting consultant services from the State Department of Education to each of the institutions on a regular basis so that the teaching programs may be brought more in line with state requirements for education. The work completed within the institution's own academic program should be transferable to the public schools upon the child's discharge. There is also widespread need for more special classes in the public school system. Some children come into institutional programs who could be accommodated better in their own communities if special educational programs were made available in the public schools. 

8. Very diversified groups of children congregated in some institutions through present commitment practices and admission policies confront the institutions with a difficult problem of accommodating to widely different program requirements. Major revisions are needed in the laws affecting children and their commitment to institutions. It is recommended that the Advisory Commission on 

Institutional Management join with representatives of other children's organizations and agencies now actively working toward a revised juvenile code and its enactment by the Legislature. 

9. In reviewing the various program needs confronting the Director of Institutions, it is recommended that certain additions to his state staff be considered: a. An assistant to the Director of Institutions who is a qualified psychiatrist, to assume responsibility for consultation to the children's institutions, and to aid in the planning and establishment of community clinic programs. At present many of the institutions are being called upon to provide diagnostic outpatient services to surrounding communities. If the plan to place the state mental health authority in the office of the Director of Institutions is carried out, responsibility for establishing community clinics throughout the state will be greatly increased. b. A consultant in psychology with clinical and research orientation, to provide consultation to the institutional psychologists, to aid in refining psychometric and diagnostic tests for assessing the intellectual and personality potential of retarded and other children, to aid in selecting and improving psychotherapeutic treatment to children, and to work with other professional staff members conducting research in various aspects of the institutional programs. c. It is recommended that the position of psychiatric social work consultant be filled as soon as possible to supply central psychiatric social work consultation to the social service staffs in the several institutions.

C. SPECIFIC PROBLEMS OF INDIVIDUAL INSTITUTIONS 

The Kansas Receiving Home for Children and the Kansas 

Children's Home 

As we surveyed the functions of the Receiving Home, major violations of sound diagnostic and treatment concepts appeared.

The diagnostic service offered by the Receiving Home is largely the reporting of psychometric data together with conclusions drawn. from Rorschach material. A total picture of the child, viewing him in his family setting and as an individual both reacting to and being reacted upon by his social environment is not given. The too brief psychiatric interview has only adjunctive weight in the total report formulated.

The length of inpatient residential stay for diagnostic purposes in the Receiving Home is excessive. On the average, a child remains there between three and four months, many much longer during which time there is no educational program, no vocational program, and no recreational program. The object of this lengthy inpatient residential period was purported to establish a base line on which to evaluate the child psychologically and for observation by the child-care staff. Yet these two considerations are not reflected in the diagnostic reports from the institution. 

There are two possible ways to handle the unsound diagnostic situation in the Kansas Receiving Home for Children. One would be to improve the quality, if it is decided to retain the function; the other is to delegate this diagnostic function elsewhere. 

At the time the Receiving Home was established, there was no psychologist on the staff of any of the children's institutions. The need for this limited type of psychological testing service has passed. Such reports are of relatively little value to any of the other institutions, since most have more diagnostic services available to them than the Receiving Home at present can provide. 

In the statistics of the Kansas Receiving Home report for the biennium ending June, 1954, the impression was given that many more children entered and remained for study than is actually the case. Figures of children seen for tests only on a single day without being admitted into residence are computed with those who came in to residence, thus making it appear that the average length of stay is far less than it really is. Our review reveals that sixty-eight percent of all children seen were on a one-day basis and only thirtytwo percent were studied in residence. This means that more than two thirds were tested on an outpatient basis. The major service

rendered, therefore, in number of children seen is not for residential study but for outpatient psychometric testing, which, incidentally, was limited almost entirely to children from the surrounding community.

The law calls for a small fee from parents who request diagnostic service from the Receiving Home. The Institution avoids fee charging by having parents arrange for service through the court or the county welfare department to which services are free. This also avoids the clinical responsibility of dealing directly with parents and interpreting to them the findings on their child. There are no social workers on the staff to do such work with parents. 

Specific recommendations for the Kansas Children's Receiving 

Home are: 

1. Children should not be held longer than a month for diagnostic evaluation purposes. 

2. Boys' Industrial School 

Besides the problems in the Boys' Industrial School due to changing superintendents, shift of the chief psychiatric social worker to the position of the director of home life, and shift of the chief psychologist to acting clinical director and then to acting superintendent, the staff was struggling without a consistent concept or philosophy of treatment and ways in which such concept might be applied in the vocational, educational and basic child-care program. A part of the difficulty in arriving at such a philosophy derives from a conflict of the individual and group psychotherapy programs with the concept of a milieu program. It is hoped the appointment of a chief consultant to the Boys' Industrial School will help eliminate confusion among the several consultants and staff as to what the prevailing treatment approach shall be. 

An attempt is made by the cottage committee to continually evaluate progress; the vocational staff fills out weekly rating sheets;

2.

 the academic teachers make observational reports every six weeks. 

But there is no formulated set of criteria for evaluating progress in personality integration and degree of outward improvement which the boy should achieve before he is ready for discharge. Nor are there known gradations in status for the boys to advance progressively through from admission to discharge. 

It may be desirable to provide closed, semiopen, and open units for more differentiated child care and treatment. Assignment to these would be based on a differential diagnosis and according to the severity of the child's disorder. In an industrial school the closed unit is not to be interpreted merely as a detention unit but rather one in which this degree of security is one necessary phase of a continuous treatment program aimed at developing independent self control.

It is recommended that this institution be granted authority to refuse admission of any child under twelve years. Lacking this, the institution will require facilities to create age-homogeneous groupings to more effectively separate the younger from the older children.

It is recommended that the primary emphasis in the Boys' Industrial School be placed on milieu therapy rather than on intensive individual or group-session psychotherapy. It is suggested that the 

Advisory Commission on Institutional Management seriously consider whether large-scale closed-unit psychiatric treatment is a proper function of the Industrial School. If so, additional personnel will be required since present staff is insufficient to conduct such closed-unit treatment without depriving the majority of children of other treatment they vitally need. 

Girls' Industrial School 

In contrast with the program aims at the Boys' Industrial School, which attempts to deal with the problem of delinquency as a symptom of emotional disorder and considers itself a psychiatrically oriented treatment center, the Girls' Industrial School program is primarily based on the concept that delinquency is a social condition best dealt with by education and vocational rehabilitation methods. However, there is an attempt to differentiate those girls wih emotional problems, who are referred to the psychiatric consultant, from the socially maladjusted or culturally deprived who need mainly social, educational or vocational training. Every girl admitted to the school is seen by the psychiatric consultant as part of the admission procedure. The superintendent recognizes “that

there is a limited number of staff and that it is difficult to carry out the complete objective as the members of the clinic [case conference staff] visualized."

Psychiatric consultation is available in once-a-month visits by a psychiatrist from the Menninger Foundation. Individual psychotherapy is provided by the not yet fully qualified staff psychologist whose treatment is supervised by the psychiatric consultant. Diagnostic appraisals of girls admitted is relatively superficial and does not have a central part in the treatment planning. As at the Boys' 

Industrial School, we were unable to derive any concept of what constitutes progress in personality integration, and by what criteria, other than the report card system and the practical test of the girl's outward adjustment while she is home on visits, the staff decide when a girl is ready to be released.

The present Girls' Industrial School is a well-run institution in its good level of domicilary and physical care and its nonpunitive management of the girls. Efforts are made to provide each girl with some kind of vocational preparation for return to community life. This attitude seems to have been well communicated to the child care personnel.

Individual and group expressions of sexuality or aggression, hostile or negative feelings are dealt with to a major extent by social measures rather than through therapeutic, emotionally reconstructive methods. However, there is growing awareness that these are significant treatment problems and addition of a resident psychiatrist to the staff is planned for July, 1956.

Much more staff development and inservice training will be required for the staff to absorb the concepts of psychiatric treatment and integrate these concepts into its program. This should take place gradually to avoid confusion in staff thinking and institutional practices. 

Future construction should preferably plan for cottage-type dwellings rather than remodeling of older congregate-style structures. Plans are now being completed for a new education building. 

The program at the Girls' Industrial School is severely handicapped by its isolated geographic location which hampers recruitment of personnel, creates a lack of community resources for use in the program, and limits the frequency with which consultants in the psychiatric disciplines can visit. Moreover, the geographic isolation forces the staff to live within itself and, like the girls, become institution-bound. To counteract this staff members have

been active in speakers' bureaus, attending conferences, serving on panel discussions and visiting other institutions. 

Winfield State Training School 

Although hampered by many problems, Winfield State Training School seemed to be working constructively in developing a program of rehabilitative work with severely mentally deficient and multihandicapped children and adults. Considering the severe limitations of its population which requires mostly only physical care, the devotion of its staff is commendable. 

The school has initiated an active continuous inventory of human potentialities as the basis of more effective program placement. It utilizes all community resources possible, has an active volunteer group, and has developed supervised work placements in the community for individuals trained to perform vocational tasks. 

It is difficult to convey a full picture of the Winfield school with its stark constrasts, crowded population and lack of facilities, its barren wards and day rooms with no toys, games or equipment, need for more personnel just to provide adequate basic physical care, and how these factors contribute to an atmosphere of depression, impoverishment, futility, and low morale. In the regressed wards where children and adults are intermingled there is a "snake pit" quality. Children and adults naked and dirty mingle in feces and urine, with no treatment attempted for lack of staff. 

Prior to the survey an appropriation had made possible the hiring of approximately 100 additional aides necessary to raise the level of physical care on the wards. However, these additional personnel will not be enough to overcome the problems of unsuitable housing and inadequate grounds for simple outdoor activities. 

In shining contrast, the program in the hospital building was of high quality. Once an inmate becomes a bed patient, quality of physical and medical care is higher. About half of the institution's employees are assigned to duty in the hospital program, the major emphasis of which is on medical and surgical aspects rather than social rehabilitation. Despite use of patient assignments and this heavy distribution of staff, the number of hospital personnel is still inadequate.

More than in any other institution, there is a need here to more realistically set the optimal number of patients who can be properly cared for, in terms of available beds, staff, and program facilities such as classrooms, play space, recreation areas, and other factors. 

The need for additional new buildings and expansion of physical

facilities in almost all areas is so acute that one wonders whether the institution should be further expanded or whether it would be wiser to construct a third state training school to care for the mentally retarded and multiple-handicapped. 

Various ways to deal with the numerous problems in the care of the mentally retarded and multihandicapped have been considered. One solution may be assignment of admissions on the basis of the following distribution: 

1. One institution for the care and training of all mentally retarded children and youth under twenty-one, regardless of degree of retardation. 

2. Another institution for mentally retarded adults (over twenty-one) offering complete training and treatment facilities, without distinction of mental level as the basis of admission. 

3. A third institution for all multiple-handicapped, retarded individuals who require an active hospital or medical rehabilitative program, regardless of age.

The Winfield school has recently established a psychiatric-aide training program in an attempt to relieve personnel shortages for aides. However, the adequacy of inservice training, teaching, and supervision is limited by the difficulty in securing qualified staff to provide the most effective kind of program. 

There seems to be at this time an overemphasis on the educational and vocational aspects in the school's training program which, while it meets the needs of a relative few, doesn't reach the needs of the many. Staff has concentrated on these more advanced program elements to the exclusion of such basic functions as toilet training among the nonhospitalized and self-care. 

Winfield State Training School seems to be in a transitional phase of its development, changing over and revising its training program which had been originally developed for a more able group than its present population. Under the new program, the more able of the retarded were sent to Parsons. This necessitated active reorientation toward meeting the needs of the much less potentially able group constituting its present population. 

Parsons State Training School 

As this is an institution in transition, it is difficult to formulate the definition under which it is operated. The school's present program has been in existence only since this institution was changed in 1953 from a hospital for epileptics to a state training school. 

According to the superintendent, the objective of the school is to offer a psychiatrically oriented program for mentally retarded chil-

dren. The program at present appears to have three separate and distinct functions: (1) a training school for mentally retarded children and youth, (2) a psychiatric treatment function, and (3) a custodial function for adult epileptics. The program for the mentally retarded emphasizes training children to acceptable social adaptation. The training stresses "units of experience" rather than curriculum content, per se. 

Within its concept of "social rehabilitation,” the school actively plans for retarded children between the ages of six and fourteen with an I. Q. between fifty and seventy. The full program, however, should accommodate children and youth between the ages of six and twenty-one who are of a lower mentality than an I. Q. of fifty, since this is the function accorded the school by the Legislature. 

On this point the superintendent states: children with an

I. Q. of forty or above are considered to be better able to benefit from the training program as established at Parsons; we do have numerous children with I. Q.'s below forty that we are continuing to try to absorb into our program. For those who cannot be absorbed after continuous effort and trial, we find ourselves very ill equipped from a physical plant or a staffing standpoint to continue their care custodially. It has been our understanding that it is the 

Department's wishes that this type of individual, as well as the multihandicapped, be made known to the Department so that administrative transfer might be arranged. This policy was outlined mainly because we did not feel that the facilities would be available at Parsons in the foreseeable future for this type of patient and that their continued residence at Parsons would not only jeopardize progress of our training program for the less severely mentally handicapped but would result in a large waiting list for individuals who were in desperate need of immediate acceptance into the training program so that they might be rehabilitated.” 

The superintendent prefers to administer a psychiatric state hospital for children and to admit children with neurotic problems, with behavior disorders, and with psychosis requiring psychiatric treatment. In his own words, "It is the superintendent's preference and his recommendation in our current Annual Report that the Parsons State Training School be changed both in name and mission to a Psychiatric State Hospital and Rehabilitation Center for Children. We not only would like to admit children with neurotic problems and behavior disorders and with psychoses, but are doing this continuously

The institution has begun a program of community education and is aware of the importance of this. It was working toward establishing a volunteer program in the community and seeking to establish supervised work placements in town for older youths who have been vocationally trained. 

During the past two years, the Training School has accepted on transfer 178 children and has admitted over 200 additional children into the program. The School receives applications for admission at the rate of sixteen to twenty-one children per month. As capacity is reached, admissions will of necessity be limited and many children awaiting institutional care will require additional facilities. Therefore, special classes in the public schools should be considered so that those not needing protective institutional care can be given training in their community. The group of adult epileptic patients should be transferred. However, their removal from the institution would not provide additional space for children inasmuch as their housing has been condemned. The particular needs of this residual group of patients put considerable pressure on staff who might otherwise devote more time to the Training School program. 

The relationship between the training schools at Parsons and Winfield is strained. One reason for this is the screening at the 

Parsons School of retarded children between the ages of six to twenty-one and its recommending for transfer to Winfield those who do not meet relatively high admission requirements at Parsons. 

The Parsons School requires children accepted into its training program be toilet trained, have an I. Q. of at least fifty, and be ambulatory. The staff at Winfield complained that it is being used as a dumping ground by the Parsons School. It is recommended, therefore, that the Advisory Commission on Institutional Management review the functions of these two institutions so that they may be made more complementary in their service to the severely mentally retarded child who cannot receive care and training in his community.

Topeka State Hospital, Children's Section 

The Children's Section at the Topeka State Hospital has devoted a considerable amount of its time toward the development of psychiatric treatment methods for children (between the ages of twelve and sixteen years) whose behavior is so primitive and whose reaction to a situation is so hostile and aggressive that they cannot be tolerated in family or open community living. For these children little is known concerning effective treatment methods. An

institution can offer them an opportunity to learn acceptable social ways of living in a nonpunitive, nonrejecting environment. The behavior of such a child can be more easily tolerated by an institutional staff because it can share the burden and no one person needs to bear the entire impact of the disturbed child all the time. 

Included in this group may be children whose behavior is dangerous to themselves and to others and who may have been or are likely to be apprehended for delinquency. They are hostile, mistrustful of the adult world, and others are regarded as enemies. 

They are basically fearful, anxious children whose external behavior surrounds a well of unhappiness which often is equal to psychic illness requiring the deepest understanding, tolerance, and scientific treatment within a controlled environment. These are children who cannot use community facilities. The industrial schools and other institutions frequently are required to take in such children though these places lack in large measure the proper psychiatric facilities for their treatment. 

A major purpose of the Children's Section of the Topeka State Hospital is to treat this group, and provision should be made to increase its capacity. These children require long-term care, and the Section is now filled. Expansion of facilities should also include semiopen and open units for children of lesser degrees of disturbance and provide for progression toward more open community living. Consideration might also be given to the establishment of an adolescent group home in the community under the supervision of the Children's Section of the Topeka State Hospital or the Division of Child Welfare Services for those adolescents able to tolerate the pressures of open community living in a minimally protected environment. Similar children in other institutions needing closed ward care on a long-time basis could be transferred to this section if facilities and staff were expanded. At present, some services are provided for such a group at the Boys' Industrial School. 

At this time the Section draws its psychotherapists from staff of the Adult Section of the Topeka State Hospital as well as from adjacent psychiatric agencies whose personnel may take responsibility for the treatment of one or two cases. This requires excessive supervisory time from the director. If the Section is to build a unified and effective therapy program, a full-time therapy staff should be added to carry the treatment responsibility within the institution. 

Additional facilities are needed for the treatment of psychotic children. There were thirty-four children receiving closed ward psychiatric treatment at the Children's Section. Of this group,

twenty-eight had been diagnosed as "character disorders" and six as "psychotics." All the other institutions asked what additional facilities would be made available to provide treatment for the psychotic children in their populations. If the Children's Section continues to concentrate its major emphasis on children with severe character disorders, the needs of the psychotic child and where these needs can be best met will continue to be a pressing problem.

After building plans were drawn and construction began, the Director of Institutions looked forward to the day the Center would be opened for service to the children of Kansas. He began to plan ahead for the soundest program possible in this new institution that would be consistent with existing child care services in all other children's institutions in the state. It was then that the Survey 

Committee was appointed to visit and appraise the total program of child care services in the seven state institutions giving residential care and treatment to children. On the basis of this survey, the committee here submits a projected outline for the organization of the new treatment center. 

The extent of the need for treatment facilities for children whose emotional, mental or social disturbances require residential treatment is not at present fully known. What is known is that it takes more than a year of continuous treatment for effective rehabilitation of children with such severity of disturbance requiring removal from their homes, according to the experience of other treatment centers. Another limitation in the law is the provision for only thirty beds. This is rather small in comparison to plans in the State of Minnesota to build residential treatment facilities for as many as three hundred children. We may anticipate two problems: First, how to provide for those children requiring residential treatment when the new Center is filled to capacity and a waiting list develops; second, how to deal with the child who requires treatment extending beyond the one-year limit.

These considerations may necessitate a differential diagnostic approach at the point of admission. Since treatment is time-limited, the admission procedure will need to differentiate and admit for residential treatment only those children whose condition is deemed substantially treatable within a year. Since the law provides not only for the treatment of such children, but also directs that research be conducted toward finding more effective diagnostic, prognostic and treatment methods, one of the research goals of the Center could be to determine criteria of short-term treatability. 

The legislators and their technical advisors in preparing the law were aware that badly disturbed children cannot ordinarily be treated and cured in the short space of a year or less. But they also knew that once beds in a treatment center get filled up, you tend to get a static population. This also leads to long waiting lists. 

To complete the State program, therefore, facilities should also be planned for long-term and chronic treatment cases.

Our survey of child-caring institutions in the State suggests the following plan of gearing the new Center with existing treatment services. We already have a long-term treatment facility for adolėscents in the Children's Section of the Topeka State Hospital. The 

1955 legislative session appropriated funds to double its capacity. 

For children twelve years and above requiring more extended intramural care, continuity of treatment will be possible at that Unit. 

Future plans for the Center, which now include increasing its capacity to a total of ninety children, should also provide a transition cottage for those younger children who at the end of a year are not yet ready for community living.

Effective implementation of the one-year treatment program of the Center also calls for continuity of treatment into the extramural phase. Our survey indicates that the biggest child care problem in Kansas today is not the lack of institutions. There is a deficiency of before-and-after-care services for children going into and coming out of our institutions. This is where our greatest opportunity exists for saving children and, in the long run, also saving money. 

Institutions are certainly needed. But institutions are not the whole answer, are not enough. To get the most return for our institutional dollar and treatment investment our institutions must be geared to good preventive and after-care programs. 

Successful operation of the new Treatment Center will very much depend on an active boarding home program for those children who, with treatment, are well enough to live outside, but are not yet prepared to go home. Such a program is a "must." The problem of supervision and follow-up after a child has returned to his own community is still another after-care consideration which will require careful planning. 

For those children who progress and move out of the institution, either into boarding homes or their own homes, an active outpatient treatment service will be needed. In order to conserve residential facilities and insure careful selective admissions, it is suggested that most children offered treatment at the Center first receive outpatient diagnostic study to determine exactly what their treatment needs are and whether these can best be met at the Center in one of its three component programs, outpatient, inpatient, or boarding home. 

A residential diagnostic unit, however, will probably be needed for a small group of children for whom outpatient study might not be feasible because of the nature or degree of their disturbance, custodial reasons such as transfer from other state institutions, or for

children of diagnostic complexity requiring extended diagnostic studies.

An immediate consideration in activating the Center is the kind of problems it will accept for treatment. In order to identify the nature and extent of child treatment services needed in the state it is suggested that continuous appraisal be carried on of the kinds of problems referred to the Center, so that a policy on admissions might be formulated in accordance with current needs. If the 

Center is to be a pilot plant for the development of experience leading to the establishment of similar treatment centers throughout the state, budget and personnel time should be allocated for keeping detailed statistics and records, and for research on the effectiveness of its program.

Most effective treatment takes place today in centers where teaching and research are also simultaneously carried on. It is suggested that, in addition to the treatment and research functions specified in the law, the Center also establish a training program offering both inservice and initial training to personnel in all child care categories. 

This would complete the triad of treatment, teaching and research, would strengthen the unit, and would make the Center more attractive to potential personnel in all professions. 

Activation of a new , the first of its kind in the State, will require close collaborative contact not only with the other State child caring institutions, but also with the 

Child Welfare and Public Assistance Divisions of the State De partment of Social Welfare, the County Welfare Departments, the Courts, the Schools, other public and private agencies. To facilitate such contact and collaboration, it is suggested that an advisory committee to the Kansas Treatment Center for Children be established. Such committee might be composed of representatives from the Advisory Commission on Institutional Management, the Kansas Council for Children and Youth, the State Medical and Bar Associations, the Legislative Committees concerned with social legislation, and representatives of public and other interested groups.

Children through age sixteen may be accepted for outpatient diagnosis and treatment as specified in the law. However, the outpatient age range should be studied in relation to the problems and children served in the outpatient clinic of the Topeka State Hospital Children's Section to avoid unnecessary duplication or overlap. 

10. Since the Kansas  is to be the first of a number of proposed centers throughout the State, it may serve as a training ground for all categories of personnel in the field of residential treatment of children with a view to providing cadres for other institutions. The Center may also want to consider offering in-service training opportunities to staff already employed in other state institutions. To provide such training opportunities, the Center should participate in the state training funds which at present can be used only at the three mental hospitals and two training schools for the mentally retarded. 

11. Although the Center originates primarily as a residential treatment facility, another important function will be its outpatient diagnostic and treatment services. Provision should be made for expansion of oupatient service in two directions: (a) as a relatively autonomous direct service, and (b) as the diagnostic avenue for most admissions and the take-up point for discharges from residence to continued outpatient treatment.

PART II 

Kansas Institutions for Children

A. GENERAL INFORMATION Introduction 

In the United States the idea of institutional care for children developed early in the eighteenth century as an expedient and financially inexpensive method of giving care to children who, for various reasons, were deprived of their families. With increasing numbers of children needing care and protection following the Civil War, state governments lent support to the movement and helped develop institutional facilities for children commonly on a local basis. Kansas followed this national trend by building institutions for homeless and dependent children. As in all states at that time institutional care was the preferred method of meeting the physical needs of children without families. Institutional care everywhere during the early years of the nineteenth century remained entirely divorced from contact with local community life. 

This fact alone tended to make children's institutions socially unique. The children, too, were usually shunned by the community to which they had no ties; they were stigmatized and marked by the segregated institutional life they led. 

Institutions continued to be principally concerned that the children committed to their care were fed, clothed, sheltered, and educated. 

But little thought was given to their emotional needs. In such an environment, they had little opportunity to be understood, nor were they helped to understand themselves or the society from which they had been removed and to which they would eventually return. They were given a smattering of education-seldom more than the three "R's." Although they learned how to work, the work they performed was geared to the needs of the institution rather than their own individual needs, and without much regard to how in the future, as adults, they were to find a place in the life of a community. Institutional living also completely severed them from their remaining families and relatives, as there was no one with responsibility for keeping alive the relationship ties between child and family. Having judged parents as inadequate or unfit, authorities feel justified in permanently removing a child from their care and custody. This concept of child care common at the (37)

beginning of the twentieth century still finds expression in the laws and public feelings of today. 

At the peak of institutional building by state and local governments at the turn of the century, a spotlight was thrown on the emotional damage to children who were thus deprived, often ruthlessly, of their parents, families, and relatives. This spotlight was the first White House Conference in 1909 which focused national attention on the dependent and neglected children in institutions. 

From this conference a new concept of child care was formulated which was to give impetus to the growth of community services for children and their families. The much-quoted important concept on which modern child welfare programs are based states that: 

Home life is the highest and finest product of civilization. Children should not be deprived of it except for urgent and compelling reasons. 

This statement expressed our convictions about proper care for children in Kansas, the common focus of all services for children, wherein institutional care has only a temporary objective and other social services gain reconstructive meaning. 

In our concern for an individual child's ultimate happiness it is often too easy to forget that the family is as much a part of him as he is a part of his family. To be removed from a family at any age is distressing. To a child the reasons for separation are often obscure. No matter what his family has done to him, no matter how they may have mistreated him, they are a part of his emotional life, usually representing to him the only security he has ever known. 

Separation of child and parents should take place only after careful exploration of the total problem in relation to the diagnosed needs of both child and family as well as the needs of the community. 

No matter how good the institution, it can be thought of only as a substitute for one's own family. No matter how we visualize the role of the "cottage parent,” he remains an employee with a stated working day. An institution is a synthetic community. Institutional life should both contribute to a child's growth and to his family's understanding of him.

The guiding principle is that wherever possible a child should remain in his own family; or, if separated, be returned to it as soon as possible. From this point of view all treatment of a child's problem should be family-centered. Services should be based on an understanding of the social, physical, and emotional needs of the family unit for the purpose of helping the family members together attain the best personal and social accommodation of which

they are capable. This view regards our institutions less as places of segregation and custody and more as transition points to receive dislocated and disturbed children; to care for and restore them as soon as possible to the social stream of life. It means that the best of institutional programs alone is not enough. For utmost effectiveness and value to the state, as much, if not more, thought, appropriations and legislative concern as we are now expending on institutions should go into establishing preventive preinstitution and reconstructive after-care programs for both the child and his family.

Administration and Management 

All seven children's institutions have a superintendent or director who is appointed by the Director of Institutions with the approval of the State Board of Social Welfare. The superintendent or director of the institution is responsible to the Director of Institutions for its administration, business management, and development of program. Under the Reorganization Act of the 1953 Legislature, the Division of Institutional Management through its Director of Institutions is admistratively responsible for carrying out the policies established by the State Board of Social Welfare. 

The Reorganization Act specifically gives the Director of Institutions responsibility for "treatment, education, care and housing" in the state institutions caring for certain categories of children and adults.

The Kansas children's institutions are located in five of the 105 counties of the state, principally in the northeastern and south eastern sections. Capacities and Populations 

The precise number of children individual institutions should properly care for is difficult to determine inasmuch as their capacities have been rated according to various standards. (See 

Table 3.) The Winfield State Training School according to stand ards set by the American Psychiatric Association, has a capacity of 971. By emergency standards its capacity is 1,450, as rated jointly by the institution and the Division of Institutional Management. However, these figures are for total population, both children and adults, and no figure is available at this time as to maximum number of children this facility can accommodate. According to a Housing Study made by the Winfield Staff, 368 children under twenty-one years of age were in residence July 31, 1954.

The state training school at Parsons has a rated total capacity of 740, including 350 children. In addition to its training school population of children between the ages of six and twenty-one, housing and care are still being provided for more than 300 adult epileptic patients. When these patents are transferred to other institutions or to boarding home care, the building they now occupy can not be used for children's living quarters, since the structure is condemned as hazardous and unsafe for occupancy by either children or adults. 

Capacities of children's institutions should be rated according to the number they can properly care for, not in terms of space for beds only, but also in terms of total facilities (classrooms, play and recreational areas, etc.) and staff. If high standards of care were used as a basis for rating capacities, very few of the state institutions for children would have the present high capacity figures shown in Table 3. However, since per capita costs are determined by size of population, institutions are always in a dilemma: 

To reduce population to the number of children whom they can accommodate with quality care will increase the bookkeeping cost for each individual child; to increase population at a sacrifice of the quality of care provided will be both an injustice to the individual child and a failure of the institution to properly fulfill its mission. At this time, the apparently overrated capacity figures inflate the actual number of children who can fully receive the kind of treatment the institution is supposed to provide.

B. INSTITUTION RELATIONSHIPS WITH THE COURTS, THE COUNTIES, THE STATE DEPARTMENT OF SOCIAL WELFARE The Courts 

The juvenile court, created by the 1905 Legislature, has exclusive and original jurisdiction in all proceedings affecting dependent, neglected and delinquent children under the age of sixteen, except children who commit a felony while in a state institution. The judge of the juvenile court is the probate judge; however, in the hearing of cases involving children his jurisdiction is separate from the probate court.

The court must take action in any case on the basis of a petition filed by any resident of the county. In all proceedings concerning a delinquent or dependent and neglected child the court must also appoint a person to look after the legal interests of the child and to bring the rights of the child to the notice of the court. This person is a temporary guardian of the child during the course of the hearing and is usually an attorney. He is called a guardian ad litem. 

The Kansas law gives the court almost complete freedom of action: It may return the child to his home under the supervision of the court with such restrictions and conditions as the judge directs; it may commit the child to the care of a reputable citizen; or it may commit the child to any child-care agency willing to receive it, or to an institution. Any delinquent under the age of sixteen may be committed to a state industrial school. However, in the case of "any girl under the age of eighteen con- victed of an offense known to the laws of the state and punishable by imprisonment, the court or justice, as the case may be, before whom such convictions shall be had, may at his discretion sentence such girls to the State Industrial School for Girls." 

Few restrictions are placed on this broad commitment authority. 

If the child is declared delinquent, he may be committed either to a state industrial school or to any institution providing care for delinquent children. In the case of a dependent and neglected child, the court may commit to any "suitable" institution. Interpretation of suitability, however, is determined by the judge, and may result in the court's changing the charge of "dependency and neglect" to "delinquency" so that the child may be committed to a state industrial school. Since the Kansas law does not prohibit the commitment of very young children to the state industrial schools, these schools have suggested the courts should not commit

to their care children under the age of either eleven or twelve. 

However, children as young as eight are often sent to a state industrial school.

Only children made state wards because of dependency and neglect may be committed to the Kansas Children's Home. Such commitment which severs all parental rights gives guardianship of the child to the State Department of Social Welfare. Restoration of the child to his family is possible if the State Department of Social Welfare, through the Division of Child Welfare Services, determines that the parents or other relatives have recovered their ability to provide for the child. When a child is committed to the 

Kansas Children's Home he is automatically made a state ward and the Director of the Division of Child Welfare Services is designated legal guardian of the child. Although committment is ordered for the child, the Division of Child Welfare Services has the final authority and determines whether the child should be received at the Kansas Children's Home or should more properly be placed in a foster family or a group care home. This discretionary procedure is in contrast with commitment to an industrial school which is direct from court to the institution. The child remains the responsibility of the State Department of Social Welfare until he is discharged from the industrial school, but commitment of delinquent children does not sever parental rights.

When jurisdiction over a child has been acquired such jurisdiction extends, at the court's discretion, until the child reaches the age of twenty-one. The court may amend, change or extend any of its orders affecting a child until the child attains the age of twenty-one. 

However, if the child is committed to an industrial school or other institution for delinquent children, he is then supervised by that institution and not by the court. When the court does not retain jurisdiction over a dependent and neglected child, the law provides that jurisdiction may be reasserted at any time prior to the age of twenty-one, providing the child has not been adopted or placed in an institution for the period of its minority. Under these provisions, the court's interest and authority following commitment may extend until the child has attained majority. 

When a child has been found to be a dependent and neglected child, the court may make an order awarding the child to the care of any agency or individual. In such cases, the child becomes a ward and is subject to the guardianship of the agency or individual. 

The agency or individual may then place the child in a family home

and may assent to the adoption of the child in regular adoption proceedings in the probate court. However, no order or decree permanently depriving a parent of his or her parental rights in a child may be made unless such parent is present in court or has been notified.

As in all juvenile courts in the nation, proceedings or judgments of the juvenile court are not deemed or held to be criminal proceedings. The steps taken by the court are for the purpose of protecting the child and represent an exercise of the parental power of the state. Within its protective jurisdiction, troubled children, whether dependent, neglected or delinquent, are recognized as individuals needing parental care, guidance or correction.

In the words of the founders of the first juvenile court: The fundamental idea of the juvenile court law is that the state must step in and exercise guardianship over a child found under such adverse social or individual conditions as develop crime It proposes a plan whereby he may be treated, not as a criminal, or one legally charged with crime, but as a ward of the state, to receive practically the care, custody, and discipline that are accorded the neglected and dependent child, and which, as the act states, “shall approximate as nearly as may be that which should be given by its parents." 5

The fundamental concept is that a person who is not an adult should not be treated as such, for his own welfare as well as for that of the community. Instead the child is to be treated by the state under the doctrine of parens patriae, in which the state acts as parent or guardian for the child.

The juvenile court, however, is a court of law, having its roots in equity rather than in criminal law. As such, it carries a heavy but traditional responsibility to act "upon each case as in a measure unique" and to adapt its actions to the individual cases. Although in many respects juvenile courts are similar to social work agencies, they differ in one respect: that is, in being part of the judicial system and in individualizing each child according to judicial principles. However, as the juvenile court idea has grown and expanded, more and more reliance is placed by the juvenile court judges on professional social work, psychological and psychiatric services available to them in individualizing each child and in securing his proper care and treatment.

Because of the lack of professional services in many communities to determine the problems and type of treatment required by

Probation officers attached to a court or the county welfare worker assigned to the court make a social investigation of the child's home situation, his relationships with family, friends, and community, and the kinds of problems he has had or is manifesting in his daily living. 

Such a social investigation can be helpful to the juvenile court judge in determining the needs of the child and the kind of treatment required to fit these needs. Probation officers also have the responsibility to supervise those children who are placed on probation either in their own homes or in foster homes. It is important therefore, that these social services provided in the community be integrated with the total welfare plan for the child so that goals set for the child by the institution are shared with the county welfare agency. 

Few courts in Kansas have probation officers. The larger juvenile court usually has one, but only in a few of the counties. Division of Public Assistance 

The State Department of Social Welfare is the administrative unit responsible to the federal government for the four federal programs administered by the county welfare departments. The four programs directed to specific "categories" of needy people are known as Old Age Assistance (OAA), Aid to the Blind (AB), Aid to Dependent Children (ADC), and Aid to the Permanently and Totally Disabled.

Old Age Assistance is for needy individuals who have reached the age of sixty-five.

Aid to the Blind is for individuals whose sight handicap is so severe as to meet the legal definition of blindness.

Aid to Dependant Children is for children deprived of their normal parental financial support who live in their own home or in the home of close relatives. 

Aid to the Permanently and Totally Disabled helps individuals more than eighteen years of age who have become permanently and totally disabled.

Recapitulation of the State Department of Social Welfare 

In the Reorganization Act of 1953, public assistance and child welfare services were made the responsibility of the Division of Social Welfare; institutional and hospital services were made the responsibility of the Division of Institutional Management. 

These two divisions of the State Department of Social Welfare differ in authorities, duties and powers. The Division of Social 

Welfare carries only indirect responsibility for social services to those in need, through a supervisory and consultative relationship with the county welfare departments to insure that federal standards underlying the federally sponsored programs are met. The Division of Institutional Management, on the other hand, has very broad direct administrative powers over each individual institution.

Activities of these two separate programs should be co-ordinated for the best welfare of the individual, his family and the community.

County Welfare Departments 

The Reorganization Act of 1953 locates the administration of all public welfare services in each county welfare department. This means that each county welfare department, not the State Department of Social Welfare, provides all social welfare services with the exception of institutional care. Since the county welfare worker is the staff person who actually carries out all the programs, these varied and diversified services are integrated within one job function which now includes the public assistance programs, child welfare services, and services to the blind.

The county welfare department is most occupied in administration of the financial aid programs according to standardized procedures for determination of need, investigations of eligibility, and provision of money to people for their maintenance. 

In addition, the county welfare worker now also provides all child welfare services. These include social casework with parents in behalf of children who have difficulty adjusting or are emotionally and/or physically neglected by their parents. County welfare departments may also take protective action, such as placement of children in foster family homes or in institutions. Besides these specialized social work services to children, families or foster families, county welfare workers also provide: social studies on adoptions for the probate courts; referral service for children with special needs which cannot be met locally; service as probation officers for

courts handling children's cases; service to unmarried mothers and children born out of wedlock; the social services requested by other counties or states involving planning for children in Kansas or for children considered for placement in Kansas; and social services to children committed to Kansas Children's Home, the Girls' and Boys' 

Industrial Schools. 

County welfare workers also provide a valuable service to children and prepare the social history prior to a child's admission to a state institution. After admission, the county welfare worker is expected to continue casework with the family so that, when released from the institution, the child may be returned to his own. home. The worker helps parents retain interest in their children, aiding them in planning visits with the child, and plans with the institution staff for the child's visits home. When a child returns home, whether on a temporary visit or probation, the county welfare worker supervises his stay and helps both family and child adjust to demands of their renewed relationships and his restoration within the community. 

In every agency where social workers give service to children and families it is the worker who carries primary responsibility for making critical decisions. Social workers in the county welfare departments make judgments about need for treatment of a wide variety of childhood disorders, about stability of the family and its ability to meet the child's emotional needs, and are also frequently called upon to make decisions or recommendations regarding placement outside the home. This is an awesome responsibility for most county welfare workers who, at present, have not had professional social work training. A further complication is the fact that the public welfare worker's assistance caseloads are excessively large, and do not allow sufficient time for child welfare assignments. 

Good child welfare services require professionally trained social workers. This fact needs emphasis since some of the public still think that child welfare service can be rendered by anybody with average intelligence and good intentions. Preventive and protective services to children, more than others, call for skilled, sensitive and trained professional personnel. 

The county welfare worker also covers Services to the Blind. 

Help is provided to parents of preschool blind children to promote wholesome growth and development.

Summary 

A major problem is the lack of follow-up services to children and their families in the communities. The county welfare department should send the institution a social history on the child prior to his admission there. The institutions report that frequently the history is received too late, after the institution's study of the child has already been completed.

The county welfare department has responsibility for securing foster homes for children in the institutions. The institution staffs feel that these services are not pursued with sufficient vigor and that children remain in the institutions beyond the maximum period beneficial to them.

County welfare workers seldom attend institution conferences on children because sharing of plans between institution and county welfare agencies is too complicated and time consuming. 

Parents frequently do not receive enough help in resolving their negative feelings toward the agency which removed the child from them as well as toward the child who seemingly had caused the trouble. When the child is ready to return home parents are often unable to give him the understanding and encouragement needed to find a new place for himself in his home and community. 

Many of the institution staffs expressed the belief that children are unwisely separated from their parents, that institutional placement is a poor substitute for the kind of services the child and family truly needs. Some felt the institution is being used as a "dumping ground" for children who present needs which might better be met at home if psychiatric services were available in the community. 

The county welfare workers are overburdened with service to their many programs. They frequently are unable to give more than passing attention to child welfare considerations of excessively large case loads.

The staffs of the institutions suggested several possible solutions to these problems. One suggestion is that the welfare workers in large counties be given responsibility only for one specific program, thus relieving them of the burden of multiple client-demands in the five public assistance programs, child welfare services, and services to the blind. In those counties with large child populations some workers might be given responsibility for child welfare services only.

A second proposal which seemed less feasible was that the institutions increase their own staffs to provide those child welfare

services which the county workers are unable to cover, such as securing foster homes, adoption services, supervision of children returned to their homes or placed in foster homes, work with the families of children in residential care. For institution staff to take on these additional functions would be a duplication of services for which the county welfare agencies are already specifically responsible.

Other lacks in the total welfare program require consideration. 

Many mentally retarded children now being institutionalized could remain in their own homes if proper schooling were available. 

Community-sponsored work programs are needed for the mentally retarded adult who has been trained for a job by which he can partially or wholly support himself. Mental health clinics are needed for parents and children whose individual and mutual emotional problems are not serious enough to warrant separation.

Kansas Institutions for Children, Their Organization, Personnel and Program

INTRODUCTION 

This part of the report presents a summary of the functions of each institution, its staff and program at the time of the survey visit. 

The seven institutions are grouped on the basis of their legal functions. The first group includes the four institutions for the residential care of dependent or neglected, and delinquent children: the Children's Home, the Receiving Home, the Girls' Industrial 

School, and the Boys' Industrial School. The Receiving Home merged with the Kansas Children's Home shortly after the survey was completed.

The second group of institutions includes the two state training schools for mentally deficient and the multiple physically handicapped.

The third classification has one institution, the Children's Section of the Topeka State Hospital. 

A. LEGAL FUNCTIONS AND PROGRAM OBJECTIVES 

The following are generally accepted considerations in the development of institutional programs for children: 

1. Institutions are usually for school-age children who need separation from their family or cannot be retained in their own home. 

2. The institution should be an interim step toward family placement and restoration to the community. 

3. Each institution should be designed to help children, through group living and individual treatment to be better prepared for the future. 

4. The institution's program should contain many elements which may be individually selected and differentially used for children. 

5. Individual children can find relationships with one or more adults and have opportunities to relate at their own pace to staff and other children in an accepting, nonpunitive, realistic environment. It should be a place where a child who is fearful of close relationships can be helped to reach out when he is ready; where a child whose relationships are distorted or confused can be helped (70)

to learn that adults can accept him and that he can be safe in his own worthwhileness. 

6. The institution is not an end in itself. A good group care program depends on other placement resources being easily accessible to the institution so that the institution does not become merely a holding place for the child. Ideally, an institution should be integrally connected with other placement services to avoid the usual "refer, transfer" pulling and hauling between agencies to which placed children are often exposed.12

The first institution in Kansas established especially for the feebleminded dates from 1881 when the Kansas Asylum for Idiotic and Imbecile Youths was established in Lawrence. B. CONTROL and ManagEMENT 

All the institutions for children operate under the Division of Institutional Management and headed by the Director of Institutions.

Certain relationships among the institutions, the courts and other state and county departments, impose some limitations on a superintendent's authority to control certain aspects of his institution, such as admission and, for some institutions, release and discharge. 

Management, organization and operation of the institution is, otherwise, the authority and responsibility of the superintendents. 

Authorities in the field recommend, as minimum requirements, that the superintendent of an institution for children should have special training in social service, psychology, psychiatry, education or related fields, and that he be an able administrator. Training of the superintendent must also be considered in terms of the institution's primary function. Table 4 presents the institutions' functions, the professional or vocational training and experience backgrounds of the superintendents at the time of the survey. 

The organization patterns of the institutions follow functional lines with related activities grouped in departments or other units for major program areas. Nearly all have lines of authority proceeding from the superintendent down through each department head to the personnel. Despite its value in communication and

policy determination, the staff conference does not seem to be widely used because of time pressures. Not all institutions or schools have regularly scheduled meetings of their cottage or quarters personnel.

Laboratory work for the School is also done at the Snyder Clinic. There are two full-time psychologists and one part-time psychologist. The director of psychological services has a Ph. D. and training in clinical psychology. He is assisted by a psychologist with an undergraduate degree. The part-time psychologist has a Ph. D. and training in clinical psychology. The psychologists hold psychotherapeutic interviews when requested but these are mainly in response to ward incident reports, as well as in response to voluntary requests for counsel or advice in relation to a problem. The psychology department also works closely with the departments of education and adjunctive therapy, tests all prospective ward employees, and gives in-service training lectures on mental deficiency and its various types. 

The School has a professionally trained social worker as director of social services. Topeka State Hospital, Children's Section 

The staff includes a clinical director who is a psychiatrist responsible for both inpatient and outpatient clinical services, two medical social workers, a psychologist, an adjunctive therapist, two schoolteachers, six psychiatric aides, three of whom are assigned to each ward on eight hour shifts. Summary of Personnel 

In all the institutions visited their most pressing and persistent problem is that of obtaining and holding qualified personnel. In each of the institutional reports examined, the number of employees who left in the biennium 1952-'54 exceeds fifty percent of the total employees at the institution. In Winfield Training School the number of total personnel loss is over seventy percent. Turnover among cottage and ward personnel is particularly handicapping to the institution. At Winfield, the number of hospital attendants lost in this two year period was approximately seventy-three percent; at the Girls' Industrial School the number of guidance and home supervisors lost was almost forty percent; at the Boys' Industrial School the figure is ninety percent.

In the category of professional staff, most of the institutions fall short of minimum standards. Although at least one staff psychologist was employed at each institution, their qualifications varied considerably: only three institutions (Kansas Receiving Home, Boys' Industrial School, and the Winfield State Training School)

The personnel problem in institutions for children is one of quality as well as quantity. Age is an important factor among institution staffs, particularly for those (cottage personnel and teachers) who regularly come into close contact with the children. Because of the tensions inherent in institutional work with children having mild and serious emotional problems, because of relatively low wages, inadequate staff quarters, and similar factors, institutions have difficulty obtaining younger, qualified people for such jobs as cottage parents and teachers. Many applicants are retired or elderly persons seeking security and companionship in their later years. 

Yet, the pressures of institutional life, especially for cottage personnel, require persons physically and mentally "young," with considerable energy.

Adjunctive Therapy Aide III Co-ordinator Adjunctive Therapies D. PROGRAM OBJECTIVES AND PROGRAM PLANNING 

Differences in the functions of the institutions are determined by the kind or category of child in need of institutional care. In all the institutions the purpose is stated in terms of "to provide for" or "care for," "study and diagnose," "train,” “educate," "treat," "rehabilitate."

One common objective is to prepare the child for return to normal community living.

Program philosophy in the institutions is less clearly defined. 

Clear spelling out of general program objectives in terms of underlying philosophy is admittedly a difficult task. The survey had only a limited time to discover the basic concepts which are often revealed only after extended discussions and examination of the institution. Nevertheless, the impression is gained that most of the institutions operate toward objectives that are not sharply conceived, without a clear statement of policy for the guidance of staff. 

The term "rehabilitation" is often used but too seldom defined in operational terms. The professed concept of diagnosing and treating the particular condition of the individual child is often not reflected in program objectives. 

Although the Children's Home emphasizes the temporary nature of a child's stay, it was disheartening to learn that the average length of stay is four to five years. The staff expressed concern that they had no authority over the child's length of stay, since their authority ends with recommending further placement to the Division of Child Welfare Services which has the responsibility for putting such recommendations into action.

The professed objective of residential placement for diagnostic purposes at the Receiving Home is too vague and ambiguous. Children are retained in medium security units in the average case four months, during which time there is no program-educational, vocational, or recreational-to meet either individual or group needs. 

No such program was thought necessary for the diagnostic function. 

The service seemed geared more to filling a legal procedure rather than meeting the needs of maladjusted children removed from their families and communities.

Neither of the two industrial schools has a philosophy which is punitive. In a general way the staffs are aware that their goal is to help the child readjust. But the method by which this goal is to be achieved is less clear. Both institutions seemed baffled by the multiple problems in a child's diagnosis, the nature and meaning of overt behavior which is rebellious and nonconforming. 

In a comparison of the two training schools for the mentally deficient, the difference between the established program at Winfield and the less clearly defined program at Parsons is noticeable. When the chronically disabled and the less-able mentally deficient were made its concern, the Winfield school continued to experiment and revise its program to meet the changing needs of its altered population. The program at Parsons cannot yet be fully evaluated since

it has been in existence only two years and is still in transition from a hospital for epileptics to a school for the mentally retarded. Its present training program is designed for the "higher grade" or more trainable child.

With a capacity for only thirty-four children, the Topeka State Hospital Children's Section is filling a sorely felt social need, the treatment of psychotic children and those with severe character disorders. The care and treatment which it can offer to this limited number of children is psychiatrically sound. This unit has taken the additional objective of developing methods for the psychotherapeutic treatment of the severely disturbed adolescent whose symptomatology is primarily that of aggressive and delinquent acts. 

These disturbed adolescents constitute a major treatment problem all over the nation. The staff of the Children's Section is performing a valuable service to the state in its effort to find effective methods for treating these children who seem unresponsive to normal social efforts to incorporate them into community life. The equally necessary function of also caring for psychotic children should not, however, be weakened by over emphasis on aggressive character problems.

Most of the institutions have established, insofar as they are able, facilities and activities consistent with good program standards. 

They have not, however, achieved the level of program development which allows children to be treated with simultaneous emphasis both on the child's individual needs and the needs of the group as a whole. With some exceptions, children are in general fitted to programs rather than having programs flexibly planned to meet the needs of individual children.

Each of the institutions is making efforts toward individual programming. All have diagnostic procedures. Psychological tests are given, and children are seen by staff or consulting psychiatrists. 

Social histories are secured which offer some understanding of the individual child and his family. Staff conferences are held where the results of examinations and interviews with the child are read and interpretations are made of the child and his needs. Children are assigned to selected academic and vocational programs and to selected cottages or dormitory groups. These efforts to organize to meet individual needs, however, are limited by lack of alternative courses of action. Although all institutions subscribe to "treatment” and to the general idea of “milieu therapy,” seldom do these ideas go beyond the clinical staff, nor are they sufficiently integrated in the total organization.

At the Winfield Training School a staff survey was made to determine appropriate program content for its population. Attempts have also been made to evaluate the School's effectiveness in training. Most of the institutions recognize the importance of such planning, but do not assign primary responsibility to a specific individual for it because of lack of available staff time to carry out such an assignment. No follow-up studies of those who leave the institution are done in any of the institutions visited, although these were frequently mentioned as desirable. 

The planning for the future is something that institutions cannot do alone. The basic research involved in developing institutional programs designed to meet the variety of needs of children who come to the attention of official agencies and state institutions requires resources which the individual institution does not possess. 

They do not have authority to create the kind of reporting system needed to gather meaningful statistics on successful community adjustment. Statistical analyses is an essential tool of program planning. Meaningful analysis of statistics is currently being improved in the Division of Institutional Management. 

The appropriateness of all program activities should be continuously under review in the light of changing needs of children and new concepts and methods of treatment. Unless the task is specifically assigned within the Division of Institutional Management, this is not likely to be done. 

E. ADMISSION PROCEDURES Institutions for Dependent and Neglected and Delinquent Children 

These institutions receive children from the juvenile courts. Admission to the Children's Home, Girls' Industrial School and Boys' Industrial School is by court commitment only. Admission to the 

Receiving Home may be by court commitment or referral from agencies or individuals.

Admission to the Children's Home is by court commitment and, by this commitment, the child becomes a state ward. Therefore, every court commitment to the Children's Home is automatically channeled to the Division of Child Welfare, the unit of the State Department of Social Welfare which takes guardianship. The 

Division of Child Welfare may or may not follow through with actual placement of the child in the Children's Home but may instead place the child in a foster family or a group-care home. Of 

224 children committed by the courts to the Children's Home during

the biennium 1952-'54, the Division of Child Welfare Services actually placed only forty-eight, or one fourth, in the Children's Home. These forty-eight represent children who are most difficult to place in a foster home because of physical handicap or mental retardation. Foster homes for children of Negro or mixed racial background are only sparsely available. These children at the 

Children's Home will in all probability remain there for several years before other plans can be made for them. Under this procedure, the superintendent of the Children's Home has no control over admissions and must accept any state ward placed by the 

Division of Child Welfare Service. 

The Receiving Home has control over which children it will admit, since there is no intermediary agency with authority to require admission. The major deciding factor is availability of facilities. 

The Boys' and Girls' Industrial Schools accept children under sixteen and eighteen respectively on direct commitment by the court or on administrative transfer from another state institution. There is no lower age limit of commitment to these schools. Both institutions accept all children committed to them by the court, whether delinquent, dependent, or neglected. A delinquent child includes any under the age of sixteen. But "any girl under the age of eighteen-convicted on an offense know to the laws of the state and punishable by imprisonment" may also be sent to the Girls' School.

Training Schools 

Children and adults may be admitted to the state training schools on application by parents without the formality of court commitment. The parents or parent surrogates apply to the admissions officer of the State Division of Institutional Management. If the individual for whom application is made is under six or over twentyone years of age, the application is referred to the superintendent of the Winfield State Training School for disposition by its admissions committee. If the child is between the ages of six and twenty-one, the application is directed to the superintendent at Parsons. The application contains an agreement signed by the parents to pay $12 per week for the care of the individual at the institution and to remove him at request of the superintendent. 

In addition to the application, the following materials are required: a signed statement by a physician, a report of psychological testing if this has been done, and a social history of the child and his family.

When the application is made through the county welfare department, the social history is taken there. The superintendent of the training schools may postpone admission because of lack of facilities. 

Applications are screened first on the basis of age. They usually contain a minimum of diagnostic information. Children between the ages of six and twenty-one may be admitted at Parsons and later transferred to Winfield because of severe or multiple handicap conditions concomitant with the mental deficiency because the Winfield School also has an additional function to "provide care for physical rehabilitation" of those whose age or handicaps limit their educational program in the community. For this group, usually referred to as nonambulatory or multiple handicapped, there are no age limits for admission.

The Parsons State Training School prefers the following qualifications for admission to its training program: children should be ambulatory, capable of following instructions, able to feed themselves, and be toilet trained. The Parsons School also prefers that all children should have an I. Q. of at least fifty, although at the beginning of the program it was decided not to exclude those with an I. Q. of at least forty. These criteria for admission were set by administrative decision; such I. Q. specifications are not set in the law. They are deemed prerequisites for good adjustment to the training program. Children between the ages of six and twenty-one unable to meet these qualifications are recommended for transfer to Winfield. This in effect makes the Parsons school the admissions center for the Winfield school in relation to some children between the ages of six and twenty-one. To the staff at Winfield, such procedure seems untenable between two state training schools with similar functions.

At Winfield, each application is reviewed by an Admissions Committee composed of the pediatrician, psychologist, social worker, director of education, and co-ordinator of adjunctive therapy. Applications are evaluated in terms of (1) age eligibility and state residence, and (2) the needs of the applicant. If it is decided that institutionalization is indicated, the parents are invited to visit Winfield to discuss the child's problems with the social work and medical staffs. If the applicant lives at considerable distance from Winfield, or if the medical record clearly reveals the nature of the handicap, the parents are directed to bring the child for diagnostic observation. If the child's disabilities are such that institutionalization is optional or not required at that time, encouragement is given the parents to keep the child in the home as long as possible.

Additional social information may be obtained at this time by the social worker to supplement the social history received from the county welfare department. The principle is followed that no child should be admitted for institutionalization under the age of six unless his condition is such that it imposes severe hardships on the family. Many parents would keep their children at home rather than institutionalize them if it were possible to secure help in their training at home. However, lack of community resources usually make institutionalization the only course of action. The superintendent and staff feel that unless institutionalization is absolutely necessary for the child and the well-being of the family, no child under the age of four should be admitted at Winfield. 

At Parsons, admissions are determined by the superintendent. 

A preadmission screening procedure has been instituted in the recently established outpatient clinic. 

At Parsons the child is received into the hospital for initial quarantine, and diagnostic appraisal by the clinical director and the psychologist. The parents are seen by a social worker for the purpose of answering any questions they might have about policies and procedures, and to give parents some assurance of the care their child will receive during his stay there. Staff at Parsons try to limit the child's stay in the hospital to no more than three days. 

Complete diagnostic appraisal extends over several months of observation and study of the child's ability to adjust to the training program. Forty beds in the hospital are set aside for "individuals who are multi-handicapped and who are obviously so low in their mental endowment that there is little or no possibility for them to benefit from the training program and their lot must necessarily be that of continued custody." The school had a list of approximately 100 children waiting for admission. 

At Winfield, after screening by the admissions committee and a visit from the parents either with or without the child, if it is decided that institutionalization is the best solution to the situation, the child is admitted directly to the hospital for study and diagnosis. 

The pediatrician at Winfield stated that in approximately one third of the cases admitted definitive etiologic diagnosis cannot be established. Of course, accurate diagnostic conclusions are basic to skilled interpretation of the child's limitations and capacity. 

But absolute diagnosis is difficult to arrive at, since some times retardation is confused with functional disturbance of a psychogenic origin or with schizophrenia. The admissions committee has noted that since it began functioning, admissions have changed to

include more severely retarded and multiple-handicapped patients. 

The physical handicaps presented are multiple and severely incapacitating. Not every child can be trained and nothing can be given beyond simple supportive physical care. After a preliminary study and tentative diagnosis, the patient is assigned to a cottage or to a hospital ward with a schedule of education, training, or medical treatment.

The Winfield staff called attention to a need for differential diagnosis prior to admission at the school. At this time, preadmission screening is not easy to do. After a child is admitted, even on a study basis, it is difficult to discharge him back home. Some children admitted are later found to be only pseudo-retarded and suffering from severe mental illness. 

Topeka State Hospital, Children's Section 

A child may be admitted to the Children's Section in one of three ways: (1) by commitment through the probate court for hospitalization, (2) under a temporary restraining order of the probate court for examination and diagnosis, or (3) by voluntary application of parents or guardian following such a recommendation by the staff at the completion of an outpatient diagnostic study.18 

Children may be admitted on voluntary commitment by parents. 

But no administrative transfer is made from another children's institution without court adjudication of "insanity." Although the admission policy is to accept inpatient treatment for children between the ages of twelve and sixteen who are seriously disturbed and require confinement, there are children in residence both below and above this age range. Outpatient treatment is offered children of all ages.

Summary of Admissions Procedures 

Institutions operate most efficiently and effectively if their populations are made up of children with similar needs. These needs must be defined to include all the elements lacking in the child's physical, social, emotional, and intellectual make-up. It is the function of each institution to establish a program of treatment designed to supply these needs. This is a tremendously difficult task even under ideal conditions. It is most difficult when intake and admissions are not guided by adequate diagnostic services.

Admission requirements are usually expressed in terms of age, sex, I. Q., or legal category. Not reflected in admissions requirements are the nature and degree of emotional disturbance. The

factor of age alone may complicate program planning. The Children's Home has an age span of approximately ten years. Both industrial schools suggest setting a minimum age of commitment of children to their programs, but this suggestion need not be honored by the courts. At Winfield, children and adults are intermingled in the same program. 

Admission requirements based on "mental deficiency" acquire meaning when stated in terms of degree of deficiency. Parsons prefers to admit children whose I. Q. is fifty or above. Winfield reported no admission requirements with respect to I. Q. 

Both industrial schools occasionally accept children who are neglected and dependent (not adjudged delinquent) along with those who are legally delinquent. There is no restrictions on the court's commitment of nondelinquent children to industrial schools. 

Some courts commit seriously disturbed boys to the Boys' Industrial School primarily because of its psychiatric treatment resources. 

However, such children might be better placed in the State Hospital Children's Section, or in a training school, if a diagnostic study were made prior to commitment. The court is frequently the only agency to which parents of aggressive children can appeal when concerned about their child's mental or emotional health and there are no mental health facilities in their community. 

We have noted that commitment practices require some institutions to admit all children committed regardless of their disturbance. 

This places a tremendous burden on the institution or school to accommodate to widely different and changing program requirements. In effect, this procedure creates "all-purpose" institutions. 

Yet, the necessities of administration compel standardized program patterns and techniques. Modifications and adjustments within reasonable limits are necessary and often entirely possible, but separate specialized staffs and facilities within one institution for significantly different types of treatment problems are beyond reasonable expectation.

Serious consideration should be given to assessing the kind of problems requiring institutional placement. Institutions need control over their admissions, regardless of legal commitment. Because such control over admissions might infringe judicial prerogatives, consideration should be given to the possibility of having all commitments made to the State Department of Social Welfare or the Director of Institutions for determination of the appropriate treatment resource.

F. ANALYSIS OF INSTITUTION PROGRAMS 

Programs and services are developed according to the institution's concept of its purpose, the needs and characteristics of children served, and in accordance with program tradition. Not always can one find continuous program planning by an institution. Frequently one finds the reverse when attempts are made to meet minimum standards through the addition of program units which may have little or no relation to the institution's ultimate goals. 

Kansas Children's Home 

The purpose of the Home is to provide care and treatment for children through constructive experiences in group living while helping them prepare for home placement. The Division of Child 

Welfare of the State Department of Social Welfare has responsibility for making permanent placement plans for a child committed to 

Children's Home. 

The program of the Home is designed for the child who is awaiting foster placement. The philosophy of the Children's Home is that institutional living should provide only temporary child care and that every effort should be made to secure a foster home placement for the child who has been deprived of his parents. 

DIAGNOSTIC AND TREATMENT SERVICES. Limited psychiatric diag nostic service is available from a psychiatric consultant. When the child is admitted, a social worker is assigned to him throughout his stay. A case history is secured from the county welfare department. 

Instructions from the Division of Child Welfare Services are specific regarding parents visiting the child, letters, and other contacts and communications between the Home and the child's family. 

Many times, however, plans being made by the Division of Child Welfare Services for the child are not shared with the Home so that the staff is unable to help the child with problems involving his parents and in being separated from them. 

Casework with children in residence is the principal treatment service available to all children. In addition, University of Kansas pediatric students, who are fulfilling requirements for courses in psychiatry, devote one full day a week to "play therapy" with children having emotional problems. It was recommended by the superintendent and ap-

For those children with specific learning problems, there is an ungraded class. A music teacher is employed on a part-time basis. 

Mental retardation is not in itself considered reason for not sending a child into the public school if he is acceptable, for it is believed that community contacts are needed by all the children, regardless of their level of intellectual functioning. 

VOCATIONAL PROGRAM. There is no vocational program. 

RECREATIONAL PROGRAM. A recreational director is employed on a part-time basis for planning afternoon and evening activities.

However, there was little to indicate that the individual interests of each child are met through individual activities apart from the group. The Home has a well-equipped gymnasium and swimming pool. The day rooms or lounges in each of the cottages or dormitories were sparsely furnished, with a few pieces of overstuffed furniture, a television set, a phonograph, and little else. 

RELIGIOUS PROGRAM. Protestant chapel services on the campus are provided every Sunday morning by local ministers, and religious instruction is available once a week. Only the director, the psychologists, psychometrists, and educational diagnostician attend the staff conference.

VOCATIONAL PROGRAM. None. 

RECREATIONAL PROGRAM. 

None on a regularly planned basis.

Boys' Industrial School 

The Boys' Industrial School interprets delinquent behavior as symptomatic of emotional disturbance, and conceives its purpose in terms of diagnosis and treatment. 

DIAGNOSTIC AND TREATMENT SERVICES. These are provided by a clinical staff supplemented by psychiatric residents of the Menninger School of Psychiatry who are assigned to the school for limited periods of time. Usually the psychiatric examination of the boy is made by the resident psychiatrist. A battery of psychological and educational achievement tests is given by a psychologist and the educational diagnostician. Social histories are provided by the

welfare department in the county of commitment and are supplemented by whatever additional information the School case worker secures from parents, relatives, and others who visit the child during his stay. A staff conference is held on each child. After presentation of reports from the examiners, the child is usually interviewed before the conference group by the staff psychiatrist. The reports and the interview guide the staff in assigning him to a program of scheduled activities.

Group therapy is the principal method of treatment provided, although limited individual psychotherapy is available for some seriously disturbed boys. One or more groups in therapy are assigned to individual members of the clinical staff, including the educational diagnostician, the chaplain, social workers, and others. 

Additional therapists are recruited from other agencies in the community. The group therapy program and all therapists are supervised by a consultant in group therapy from the Menninger Foundation. The acting clinical director and staff psychiatrist had, no responsibility for this treatment program and, although dissatisfied with it as the major treatment method, seemed without authority to modify or change the program. 

Two special treatment units have been developed at the school, one for borderline psychotics, the other for boys with severe character disorders. These facilities for special treatment accommodate only a small number of the 150 boys at the School. 

EDUCATIONAL PROGRAM. This program is state accredited. Three of the nine teachers have special certificates in teaching emotionally disturbed children, and four others have taken specialized training in this field. Removal from the classroom is the method used when individual boys become difficult to handle, and the problem is then taken up in the discussions of the cottage committees. 

The academic program has conventional classes, from grades one through ten. The only remedial work provided is for a group of from thirty to forty boys with reading difficulties. No additional remedial work or special classes could be offered without increasing the teaching staff.

Almost all boys are required to attend school a half day. Specific assignments in the educational program take into account each boy's stated vocational preference and the staff conference treatment recommendations.

VOCATIONAL PROGRAM. This program offers three areas of training: (1) work experience which includes such things as shoveling

coal, reading dials and yard work; (2) prevocational training in which the focus is on helping the boys become acquainted with tools and techniques for jobs such as meat cutting, laundry, carpentry, barbering, and catering; (3) vocational training in a printing shop, auto shop, and other kinds of shop work. The objective of these programs is to provide adequate vocational training to all boys committed to the school so that they may be able to maintain themselves in a job when they are discharged and thereby refrain from further delinquent or antisocial behavior. 

At least half of the boy's time is taken up in some kind of vocational training. The School tries to give the boy a vocational assignment of his own choice.

One of the major problems in a vocational training program is the conflict which frequently results when maintenance needs of the school are considered more important than training needs of the child. Many of the vocational "teachers" are maintenance personnel, and the boys assigned are used as helpers rather than as learners.

All boys are asked to indicate their recreational interests, and activities are planned to meet these interests. G. RELEASE, DISCHARGE AND AFTER-CARE 

A fundamental principle of all forms of child care is that a child should remain with his family whenever possible. If separated, he should be returned home as soon as possible. Few children are in state institutions because they are orphans. In the emergence of problems which result in institutional placement, it has long been recognized that both the child and his parents are very often interlocking contributors and participants. Such child's difficulties can best be resolved by institutional placement only when the parents are also helped toward some modification of their former influence upon the child, so that in their resumed care of the child they will have acquired more appropriate understanding of his needs. 

Determination of the optimum point of readiness for release or discharge should flow naturally out of progress in treatment. The decision for release is made usually on the basis of the adjustment the child has been able to achieve. The child who has actively worked on his problems has also earned the right to speak for his own readiness for release and should be a participant in planning for his after-care. In some of the institutions, particularly the Girls' and Boys' Industrial Schools, there is a gradual movement toward full release through the policy of temporary probation while on prolonged visits with his family. For all institutions, the adjustment the child makes while visiting his family is a method for determining readiness of the child to return to the community. 

No amount of careful preparation can take the place of sustained after-care supervision. Preferably, the caseworker should be the one who has had contacts with the institution and has knowledge of its program and personnel. This means not merely periodic checking to see how the child is getting along with his parents or foster parents, but rather a rehabilitative effort which continues from the contacts the child has had with the clinical staff of the institution. During this process, the child is helped to reassess his responsibility and capacity for community living, to test new attitudes and concepts about himself, and to translate new skills and work habits into further schooling or a job. 

Parents and child need help to understand and readjust to each other in the new light of changes which have occurred during their separation. The after-

care worker is an important objective outsider who can help interpret each to the other and thus relieve accumulated tensions within the family. Obviously, treatment and rehabilitation are not necessarily complete when a child leaves the institution. The institution, for some, may be only one step in treatment. 

The following analysis is based on statistics reported in the institution's biennial reports for the period 1952-'54. In addition, the Committee compiled and analyzed various statistical data requested of and made available by each institution. 

Kansas Children's Home 

Of the 182 children separated from the Home during 1952-'54, eighty-two percent (148 children) were placed in foster homes. 

It was stated that such placements were frequently made by the Division of Child Welfare Service without consultation with the institution staff. Some of these children had been in residence a number of years. Many had had previous placements away from the Home but had been returned because they were unable to adjust to the foster home placement. 

Foster home placement is not the simple process of finding a home and placing the child in it. Many factors are taken into consideration, such as (1) the child's need for an intimate relationship with two adults who can be accepted by him as substitute parents, (2) his age and phase of development, and (3) his ability to meet the demands of a new home and community and to "fit in" with the cultural and social values of the foster parents. Persons are selected as foster parents after careful study of their individual backgrounds, their relationships with their own parents, their motivations for becoming foster parents, their social values, their understanding of the growth needs of children, and their ability to help the child find a satisfactory way of life. 

When children are indiscriminately placed in foster homes, many are unable to adjust, and foster parents demand their removal. 

When this happens, the child suffers emotional damage. Repeated failures to find acceptance in various foster homes create additional problems for the child who then bears the pain and anguish that he is not wanted by anyone. Many children who have gone through poorly-considered placements constitute part of the chronic population of institutions for dependent and neglected children. 

Of the 182 children separated from the institution during the biennium, only six were discharged and returned to the care of their parents. Why are not more children returned to their own

homes? The Home's professional staff suggested the following reasons: (1) inability of the county welfare departments to offer intensive rehabilitative services to help parents provide care in accordance with community standards; (2) permanent severance of parental rights based on limited legal evidence and without sufficient consideration of the social and emotional factors. It would seem that before parental rights are severed by the courts, more effort should be made to rehabilitate the family and prevent separation. 

In the community's zeal to protect children from parents who seem unable to give them the kind of physical care and guidance deemed desirable, it is easy to forget that "children have an amazing capacity to tolerate bad parents, poor teachers, dreadful homes, and communities." 19 Is institutional placement ultimately the best answer? This question cannot be answered in the affirmative for of the children we saw in the state institutions. many In comparison with other states, Kansas is among those reporting a high proportion (approximately two thirds) of children receiving child welfare services while in institutions and foster family homes. 

The goal of child welfare services is not to separate children from their parents but rather to provide the kind of service necessary to enable parents and children to continue to live together comfortably and within acceptable standards of child care. 

We studied data on sixty-nine children admitted to the Children's Home prior to 1953 who were still in residence at the time of the survey. These children were between five and nineteen years; the median age was thirteen. Over twenty-five percent were Negro or of mixed racial background. Over half tested either as borderline defective or mentally defective. About one third had been tried in one or more foster home placements, only to return to the Home after several months. All suffered some emotional damage believed directly related to their overlong institutional stay, to separation from their families and relatives, or to physical and mental limitations which handicapped placement. It appears that some kind of group living arrangements would be necessary for children who seem unable to accept or be accepted in foster home care.

The Industrial Schools 

The length of time spent by a child within an institution is an important statistic. Although this was requested, the only figures available from the Biometrics Section covered the period from the child's admission to the time of his discharge from jurisdiction, a figure which includes both the time spent in the institution and time on parole. From the data supplied it was therefore not possible to compute the average length of stay. 

The Girls' Industrial School reported no direct discharges during the 1952-'54 biennium; 73 girls were discharged while on probation or authorized leave. Six girls were discharged while AWOL (absent without leave); 119 girls were placed on probation. There were fewer girls (11) reported as runaways during the fiscal year 1953-'54

as compared with (42) those reported as having run away during the previous year. 

Between July 1, 1953, and January 1, 1955, the Girls' Industrial

School discharged 49 girls. From analysis of data, assumed representative of the total School population, the following is noted. 

Girls between the ages of 11 and 18 were committed for a variety of offenses, including truancy, incorrigibility, sex offenses, and theft. 

The median length of jurisdictional responsibility (time spent in institution plus parole, if not directly discharged) was almost 3½ years; the range was from less than 1 year to 7½ years. The median age at commitment was 14.9 years; median age at discharge was 

18.5 years. A few of the girls were over 21 years of age when discharged. Almost half the girls discharged during this period were paroled or discharged to their parents or other relatives. More than one third were placed on probation in foster family homes or in work placements. Girls who successfully escaped from the 

School and were still AWOL (absent without leave) at the time of their twenty-first birthday were automatically discharged.

Of the girls discharged, 75 percent had records of previous commitment to the School. Such commitment does not mean that these girls actually were previously admitted to residence. Courts may commit children to industrial schools but defer placement on condition that there is no further violation. Such high proportion of repeating offenders points sharply to the lack of preventive community services and to the lack of adequate supervision of children while on probation status with the courts. 

The Boys' Industrial School statistics for the biennium 1952-'54 indicate that 206 boys were discharged while on probation and 18 were discharged directly from the institution. During this same period, 460 boys were on probation away from the School and ostensibly under the supervision of county welfare workers. The number of boys AWOL increased from 190 in 1952-'53 to 209 in 1953-54. 

Among boys discharged from the Boys' Industrial School in the 

18-month period, July 1, 1953 to January 1, 1955, stealing constituted the major reason for commitment. It accounts for 61.8 percent of the boys discharged during the last six months of 1953 and 79.4 percent discharged during 1954. For those discharged during this 

18-month period, the median age at commitment was 14.4 years.

The age range was between 8 and 18 years. The average length of jurisdiction over the boy was two years.

Length of jurisdiction seems to be associated with the age of the

boy at the time of commitment rather than the reason for commitment. For example, of the boys discharged in 1954, the median time for boys under 14 was 2.8 years in contrast to the median time 

1.6 years for boys 14 years of age and over. Such difference in length of time for these two age groups raises the questions why younger boys are kept longer, and what rehabilitative factors are considered the basis for discharge. 

Of the boys discharged, between 70 and 75 percent were discharged after a period of parole, while home on pass, or on direct discharge from the institution. Some of these boys went directly into the armed services. Authorized leave or discharge to enter military service may account to some extent for the shorter length of time for the older boys.

Almost 15 percent of the boys discharged during the last six months of 1953, and 12.4 percent of the boys discharged in 1954 were transferred to the Kansas State Industrial Reformatory because of repeated runaway. Such transfers were by commitment by the courts on the School's recommendation.

The new superintendent appointed in 1956, commented on these observations: ". boys admitted under the age of 14 do stay somewhat longer than boys older than 14. Further, younger boys usually stay on parole longer than the older boys. Another factor of 'total' length (residence plus after care) in the older group mentioned is that direct discharges and commitments to KSIR substantially reduced the over-all length of time. 

Training Schools 

Admission to a training school is voluntary, without legal commitment. Students may be discharged in one of the following ways: (1) request by parents or relatives to have the child home; (2) foster home or work placement in the community; (3) AWOL; (4) administrative transfer to another state institution.

A child discharged from the Parsons School cannot be readmitted there if he has attained the age of 21 when an application for readmission is made. Although only children between the ages of 

6 and 21 years are admitted to Parsons State Training School, they may remain past the age of 21.

Both training schools encourage parents to have their children home for short vacation period. At Parsons, it is hoped that through this method, when the family and community find the child improved and adjusting satisfactorily discharge will be requested.

Many children are admitted at Winfield because of severely handicapping mental and physical conditions. Considerable encouragement is given parents and relatives to visit these hospitalized children, some of whom can be physically rehabilitated after a period of medical treatment and possibly returned home. 

Although Parsons has been operating as a training school for only a short period, use of community resources has begun for work placements for the older youth. The City of Parsons at present is in an economic recession and the School is experiencing difficulty finding work placements. The institution itself becomes in many instances the main resource for giving paid employment to students it has trained. 

At Winfield, the program provides a progression of training which will lead to eventual discharge or community work placements under supervision for those classified as having potentialities for such responsibility. The school has developed its own outside work placement program, selecting those positions which can be handled by a person with limited intelligence trained to perform a certain job well. However, the population is becoming custodial with the transfer to the School of the less potentially able. The work placement program may not be continued because of lack of individuals capable of being trained to take such responsibility. 

Boarding home care for people over the age of 65 now being provided by the county welfare departments may be one new resource available to the institutions for reducing some of its population load. Sheltered workshop placements might also be developed by welfare agencies so that those who are able may go into supervised community living outside the institution. Consideration might also be given to the development of week-end foster home care for youngsters who could benefit from such placement in their growth and development. 

Topeka State Hospital Children's Section 

Ultimate authority to discharge patients from a state hospital is vested in the State Board of Social Welfare, but in practice is delegated to the superintendent under regulations established by the Board. Recommendation for discharge is made by the treating psychiatrist in consultation with the clinical staff. This recommendation is then approved by the superintendent. Since the opening of the Children's Section, 50 children have been admitted for treatment. Fifteen of these were classified as psychotic and 35 as nonpsychotic. The patient turnover has been relatively low inas-

much as most of the children admitted have required long-term treatment. Of the 50 children admitted for inpatient treatment, 18 have been discharged. Eight were authorized discharges; the others were withdrawn from treatment against medical advice, or were transferred to another institution, or were discharged while on escape.

There are no semiclosed or open wards to which the children may gradually move in their progress toward returning to the family or a foster home. Parole is the commonly used procedure for progression in treatment in accordance with the patient's ability to take responsibility for himself. The section has experienced difficulty in having foster homes made available by the counties and by the State Division of Child Welfare Services.
