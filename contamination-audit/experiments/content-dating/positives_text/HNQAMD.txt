THE PILGRIMAGE 

of 

SIRE BEOWULFE DE MANNEVILLE 

Sire Beowulfe de Manneville 

Was a knight of doughty skill,

Many a purple scar he bore 

From many a goodly fight; 

And when the flesh from bone he tore

While at the trencher-board, he swore 

None more than he loved better gore

When it was gravied right. 

Of all the knightly train that travelled off to wager war None more than good Sire Beowulfe did such a state abhor, Albeit he was the stoutest man who ever took the field 

With morion and basinet, with buckler and with shield.

There on his armored charger 

None ever towered larger, And the poor beast with his weight 

Beneath the burden sagged. (It's at this point I'd like to state 

An observation: At this date

A horse was something more ornate 

Than those slim beasts that generate 

The spindly shanks and giddy gait 

Of those which Aintree dominate.)

But a horse that at a gang-plow might show some strength & skill, Or even at hauling tree-trunks with all its worth and will, Found it a different matter with a mounted de Manneville 

And invariably looked fagged.

And when Sire Beowulfe encased, In all his armor strapped and laced, Girt about with sword and lance, And all his jingling metal,

He looked, it might have been a chance, Or something purely happenstance, Just like the bulging, round expanse 

Of a great big kitchen kettle.

With rerebrace and back-plate, taces, fald and jamb, Gauntlets, tasset, sollerets, pauldron, cushe and vam, To give the hosts of Heathendom the proper sort of slam 

He was in the finest fettle. 

A right good Christian knight was he,

But most incautiously he made 

A vow to go with some crusade, (A debt of conscience to be paid) To harry the hosts of heathenry, To wrest from grasp of Infidel 

The Holy City of Christendom; 

And though the war ranks he could swell 

It needed more than jnst a crumb 

To serve as his solatium,

Or keep his insides quietsome, And food was at a premiu:n 

In the land of Heathendom 

That near outpcst of Hell.

But having made his solemn vow, so rashly, as I think, Sire Beowulfe was not the man to hesitate and prink, Or let procrastination halt performance on the brink 

Of ardent resolution, or in slothful languor sink,

Nor at the thoughts of derring-do in trepidation sink, Or I'd have no tale to tell. 

He called up his retainers 

And all his old campaigners. 

With swords and pikes and lances 

He armed them to the chin; 

And across the draw-bridge prances, And down the road advances; 

He turns just once and glances

Back to his tower wherein

Lay all his goods and chattals that made for him his home, Where he had lorded it with such magnificent aplomb = To receive the Papal blessing he first would visit Rome. 

Sire Beowulfe was not a silly man, Even if he resembled a billikan, And knew, though he might illy plan,

That where man has the will, he can; 

And he was a de Manneville, And many a valiant feat of arms

Had he performed in war's alarms = 

At least he'd dreamt of such in calms 

Of sleepy moments when the charms 

Of a full interior gently balms 

The quick and volatile.

He, the last de Manneville, the Paladin would down, With his mace he'd mangle him, or paste him on the crown;

In either case achieving a resoundable renown - Forgetting that he hadn't yet quite reached London Town. 

They went along the country road, The charger and its weighty load. 

And all the fighting train.

Through the forest, over hill, Babbling brook and rippling rill, Adventuring with a canny skill 

Each dale and open plain. 

Another hill, and right in sight, As a crow flies in its flight, Straight before him, glittering bright, All the towers and steeples white

Of London Town ascended. 

Just one lonely wood to pass 

With its leafy, vine-clad mass; 

But at this rural spot, alas! 

The pilgrimage suspended.

Now Guy of Gisborne was a rogue who had escaped the gallows, An outlaw ravishing the land with many of his fellows.

They dressed in motley browns and and dirty, mud-like yellows, And acted all the time as if each evening was All-hallows. 

Here, within this very wood, Oft besmired with grime and mud; 

Sometimes, sad to say, with blood,

They sheltered from the storm and flood. 

Guy was poor, a circumstance, More or less unlucky chance, As for a pilgrim's gay romance, He'd never got as far as France.

Fair Fortune but a fickle jade is, Wooed and won ere one is old. 

Since Guy was young he could be bold 

Not for him a far crusade, his

Pillaging his purse consoled; 

No matter how or what one's trade is,

II

The one necessity is gold; 

But Guy was careful, I am told, Never to molest the ladies.

But Guy would tackle any force when by hunger driven, Nor direful felt lest he be left to die alone unshriven. 

Sire Beowulfe, all unsuspecting, thru the forest rode, When, from the trees above, there fell a stinging, barbèd goad On to his languid horses flank, Into the quivering flesh it sank; 

So swift and sudden fell it,

The maddened beast broke in a run, Not a twig, or a branch, to shun; 

An arrow's flight, its course begun, No straighter flew; and quite undone,

Sire Beowulfe upon it spun 

Unable quite to quell it.

He bobbed about just like a cork upon a rolling wave, 12

His maddened charger flew along as if the devil drave;

A branch that hung across the road, hanging somewhat low, Caught the knight across the paunch, and stun'd him by the blow. 

His vassals fled 

In sudden dread; 

Each dunderhead 

Homeward sped, To leave Sire Beowulfe all alone 

Hanging there upon a tree,

In a forking branch to moan 

Doubled up in misery; 

Emitting now and then a groan 

Through his plated armory.

But Guy had followed in his wake, thus right soon he found Sire Beowulfe upon the tree within its branches bound, He grabbed his lance and poked at him, the branches all around 

To release the tightly fastened limbs and get him safe aground. 13

Sire Beowulfe could not do much 

Helpless within his armor's clutch 

To aid Guy's efforts to unloose 

Him from the twining branches' nocse, And scarcely felt the lance's touch, As Guy, to this unwonted use, Sought, here and there to introduce

It's pointed tip, till with a crash, Inside his armor badly battered, Like an unexpected flash, To the ground he loudly clattered, With the metal's ringing clash 

As on the sward the pieces scattered.

But what the forking branch had done to stupify his brain 

And take his breath, this sudden fall had brought it back again. 

How this was so I cannot tell, I don't profess to know 

Why what is knocked out with a clout can come back with a blow! 14

"Now knight," quoth Guy, "at least a meal

I've earned, and do not have to steal; 

My need of one I don't conceal, My face itself the facts reveal." "But," quoth the knight, "I haven't got 

A meal about me, steaming hot. 

What d'ye think I am, a pot?

At times I look it, perhaps, somewhat; 

But I assure you, I am not." "Well, how about a ransom then,

There's room within this very glen 

To bury you and all your men,

Though most of them are out of ken, If you can't pay up quickly when 

I get you prisoned in my den." "My Sacred Heart," Sire Beowulfe quoth in a righteous rage, "In this I see the ending of my holy pilgrimage. 

May crows peck at that bag of bones that brought me to this pass,

I might have known the proper beast to ride on was an ass." 15

"My hat," said Guy, "So that is why 

You were all dressed up so spry! 

All, in truth, were on your way, All that hang-dog, cowardly crew, All that wretched retinue 

And panicky purée, To take a trip 

On a ship; 

All to end because a quip 

Causes you to lose your grip 

On your horse, and so to slip 

Into my hands: a common dip; 

A half-starved cut-purse, for good or ill 

A cursed outlaw, what you will?"

Sire Beowulfe at this declaimed All subtleties of thought inflamed 16

By the curious turns of chance, Explaining then his reason:

He was on his way to France, From there to Rome, and so advance 

To where the heathen horsemen prance; 

On them (a well-known circumstance) 

There was an open season.

Restsicted purely by a sort of Papal regulation, And now and then they gathered up the knights of To form a gallant company with no capital flotation 

To knock off blackamores, a really noble occupation. 

Guy groaned, then moaned, And bitterly he thus intoned: "Sir Knight, delay, I pray; 

Contrite my plight to-day. 

Away- to-night! 

I'll be your lance, and so 

Give me a chance to go every nation 17

"Nay, nay," replied Sire Beowulfe, ""Tween you and me there is a gulf = so fixed = how does it go? 

So fixed 

You should know, yes, you should know!

Because this gulf, of which I wot, Is not because I'm like a pot 

And and down and sideways got up Dimensions equal to a dot, Measuring each way to a jot. 

But something else more misbegot; 

You can't deny it, what is what, That you're a poet and I am not.

Now it is known and understood For troubadours and such-like kine 

It never does them any good 

To take a trip to Palestine. 

The reason being, as you're aware, There are too many ladies there! 18

"Now by the twins of Gemini 

Why should I trouble femini?

By sex perplexed, annexed and vexed, When you're the first then I'll be next."

Out-bubbled Guy as from a text. "But let us not be so supine, Need either go to Palestine?" 

The Devil's advocate was Guy,

He knew his business; but not I, For I know not his reasons, why

He'd urge Sire Beowulfe, without a sigh, Unhesitatingly defy

A vow, and damn his soul thereby. 

But Sire Beowulfe his bones did ache, His quivering muscles each did quake 

With tender spots and sore. 

Ah, could he bigger swell? 19

While sundry pains their tremors tore, His courage eked thru every pore,

His fighting spirit seemed no more = Where gone to none could tell. 

But a novice on a rapid horse traveling for a spell

With every bone inside of him cooked into a jell, Or from a hanging branch in a suit of armor fell Could understand Sire Beowulfe, could understand him well. "Come," said the knight to outlaw Guy, I see no valid reason why 

You should not come with me and be 

My captain of the guard. 

That base-born crew, to homeward flee 

And leave their liege-lord in a tree! 

Any evening you are free 

You have permission right from me

To smack 'em round the yard. 20

Thus after many weary days 

Footing it through country ways,

They came unto the castle's wall Both sturdy knight and sinner. 

His villains and retainers, all

Were eating there inside the hall, Even his horse was in its stall; (Only the knight was thinner.)

Thus Guy became the captain. Beside the guard-room fire 

Told kitchen wenches saucy tales, in time became a sire.

And our good knight, Sire Beowulfe, to fight did not incline; 

So the Heathen hold the city still in Holy Palestine. 

At last there came that final day,

Sire Beowulfe he passed away; 

And many an eye was tearful, too, Including that of Guy's.

His form, with years, so vast it grew, 21

They had the church doors widened to 

Get his rotund body through, And twenty husky bearers who

Found it all that they could do 

Staggering down the avenue 

To carry him into his pew; 

Then rested there likewise.

There within the chancel vault they laid him all alone In an alabaster casket with his effigy in stone. 

He lies there still, as you can see, passing by that way, And that's the reason Palestine is Infidel to-day. 22
