JUNIOR CHURCH OF THE FIRST CHURCH IN CHESTNUT HILL, MASSACHUSETTS I. WHAT IS RELIGIOUS EDUCATION? 

Before entering upon a detailed discussion of religious education, we must first ask ourselves what we mean by the term or even more generally, “What is religion?"

A doctor is one who cures diseases; a lawyer is one who concerns himself with the establishment of justice in the relations of men; a business man makes it his affair to supply the needs of people. But what is a religious man? At one. time we should have said that he is one who reads the Bible as a divinely inspired book, who knows, believes, and recites a strict theological creed, who is a regular church attendant, and, with faith, accepts the plan of salvation. But no longer can we acknowledge these outward actions as evidences of a religious man. It is much easier to scan a roster of our friends and say, "This man I should term a religious person," than it is to describe him. 

A man's religion is indicated by the quality of his tastes, the nobility of his ambitions, the nature of his purposes. It has to do with his sense of self-respect, of honor, and with all the other characteristics which form what we term his personality it concerns the ethical quality of his character. But even granted the most ethical personality, there must be another characteristic to enable us to designate a man as religious. 

With this thought we are carried back to the inner spring of conduct from which ethical behaviour proceeds. We are dealing with the nature of motives. 

That man is religious whose dominant motive is a sense of living relationship with and to some spiritual reality,-The One, Humanity, Life, or as I prefer to describe it, and shall continue to do so in this paper, God, from whom all the talents and powers of his life are derived. In response to that thought of God, his life is controlled by the motive of obligation or by a sense of responsibility for the fulfillment of His purpose of righteousness. 

Because it is known through long experience that it is possible to have a religious motive which expresses itself in harmful and unethical conduct, it is essential that character training should be a prominent part of religious education. 

The next question, then, with which we are immediately confronted is, "How are good characters developed?" The first and most obvious answer is, by the habits which we form in childhood and the experiences which strengthen our moral powers by demanding the best that is in us. Habits are formed and moral

strength achieved partly by instruction, but very much more by our reaction to the exigencies of daily life and the human relationships with which we are inevitably surrounded. The child's reaction is naturally strongly influenced by the adults who direct it. Therefore, every child should have men and women as parents, nurses, companions, and teachers who know children, who are themselves of high moral and spiritual calibre, and are wise enough to help the children discover their own possibilities and improve their tastes. In the most formative years of character training, these are the adults who constitute the principal circle of his acquaintance. 

If, then, character education is mainly the result of daily living and our reactions to it, it seems perfectly obvious that the most important institutions for religious education are the home and the school, for it is in the fellowship of these two societies that the children pass practically all of their days. I should make the statement here, then, and it will become clear as we discuss the responsibility of the home for religious education, that the church, while it has a vital part to play in the religious education of the children, cannot be called upon to bear as much of the burden in the ethical development of character as either the home or the school.

Religious education, then, means to develop high character, to grow in the ability to know unseen values, to use spiritual powers as faithful stewards entrusted with great treasures dedicated to the fulfillment of high claims, and finally to strengthen the faith that the values which we honor are essential to a far-reaching Divine Purpose. A religiously educated person is one whose life is dominated by the consciousness of God. 

II. THE HOME AND RELIGIOUS EDUCATION 

It must be remembered that the separate responsibilities of the home, the school and the church are neither exclusive nor clear cut. But there are certain distinctive emphases which each is bound to give by the nature of its organization and its relationship to the child. 

I shall consider first the share of the home in the building of character and its special contribution to the growth of the religious consciousness. 

The family circle is the first training ground of motives, purposes, tastes, and sympathies, which are taught not only consciously but inevitably, also, by the subtle effect of the family atmosphere. Obviously the influence of the constant, day-by-day attention of the family to consciously exerted discipline devised to draw forth the child's own sense of moral responsibility, self-control, consideration, and the host of other attitudes which must be encouraged is a potent factor in the formation of character. More important than this carefully directed training, however, is the unconscious and powerful influence which proceeds from the atmosphere of the home. The misfortune is that too often we minimize this vital force.

The orderliness and aesthetic taste of the household, the quality of the literature, music and art with which the children are constantly surrounded are factors of this influence. Inevitably, standards are determined by those to which the children have always been accustomed, and high morals follow usually hard upon the heels of high tastes. When the children appreciate the discrepancy between inferior standards of living and those which they have always taken for granted in their home environment, we usually can trust them, in the long run, to shun "that which is not done" in their family community. Furthermore, [2]

careful attention to the aesthetic life of the children turns their faces toward all that is best and highest, and opens vistas before them which lead into eternities of inexhaustible spiritual values. It starts them upon journeys which they can pursue throughout their whole lives without reaching the end of the road. 

Another factor of the influence of the family atmosphere upon children is the attitude which they acquire toward people outside the family circle, and toward those who are living under different social or economic circumstances. 

Nothing is more fascinating to talk about than people, but it makes a vast difference in what spirit we discuss them in the presence of our children. Behind what we say, they sense and adopt our true attitude. A spirit of fair judgment toward, and the ability to live co-operatively with other people are very vital parts of religion.

But the most fundamental part of religion results from the emotional and deeply spiritual adjustment to life. In the same way that the child needs a sense of physical security, so also must he have, for his spiritual health, a sure consciousness of moral and spiritual security. The child's first world is bounded by the limits of his own home. The younger members of the family should discover that a trust in the support of such qualities of life as honor, truth, kindness, consideration, sympathy, and love, brings the richest spiritual rewards not only to themselves, but to the family life as a whole in an increasing happiness and a growing sense of the reality of these unseen values. 

Three other factors in the atmosphere of the family life are vitally important for the religious development of the children: the absolute confidence of their own relationship to their parents and other members of the family; the quality of the parents' relationship to each other; and the parents' own sense of spiritual security in the larger world of which they are a part. 

Children should never have a shadow of doubt that they are held in the surrounding embrace of a strong affection. Out of a fear that one is not cared for, or from the sure knowledge that one is not loved arise some of the worst moral and spiritual complications in children's lives,-complications which are so fundamental and so persistent that not only their early existence, but even their entire lives may be twisted awry.

Secondly, we are inclined in our callousness, to discount the importance for children of the quality of the marital relationship of the parents. Any strain, or lack of loving comradeship here may reflect itself in spiritual insecurity in the lives of our children. The more "en rapport" a husband and wife can be, the greater is the chance that the adjustment of their children to their world will be correspondingly confident.

And finally, parents ought to have a definite personal religion. Those who are confident that there is an abiding and supreme spiritual Reality,―a God,whom they continually honor with the highest loyalty of their lives, are always strengthening in the lives of their children a similar confidence. Too often we are very reticent about interpreting life in definitely religious terms. And yet for the children and for ourselves, too, it would be better if we were less cautious about the use of the word God when we are in the presence of beauties and experiences which are closely associated with the Divine Life. 

Another form of religious training in the family comes from a familiarity with the Bible. Here are many passages both appropriate and interesting even for young children, and couched in religious language with which we should familiarize them. In reading them, however, my own feeling is that it is helpful, unless we prefer to do our own editing, to use some good children's version of the [3]

Bible, one in which the stories have been rewritten so that duplications and uninteresting parts are omitted, and yet the language of the King James translation retained. Personally, I feel that the children should know the dignified and majestic rhythm of the King James English, which has never been surpassed. Although the modern translations are, of course, more in accord with the mood and phraseology of the original Hebrew or Greek, they can be reserved until the children are concerned with the precise meaning of the stories. At this earliest stage, the children should hear the stories simply as stories, and the reader can usually and properly omit interpretations until the children themselves begin to raise questions. When the children first hear the Biblical attempts to give answers to some of the questions which are still baffling the efforts of the greatest thinkers, they will be classed in their minds with the tales of Homer or the Norse sagas,-parts of the religious inheritance of the race. Later, we can interpret the stories, and give them their proper historical setting. From this first reading they will acquire at least the vocabulary for the expression of their own thoughts, and will also be in sympathy with a people who understood and interpreted the whole of life in the light of the thought of God. 

The family should be responsible for giving the children outward forms with which to express and cultivate religion by family prayers or grace at meals. 

Through my association with children I have become convinced that most of them have a genuine interest in their relation to the universe and think much about it. Perhaps the fact that their lives are less concerned with the mechanics of living than are those of adults leaves them freer to pursue such thoughts. 

With the natural tendency to personalize all relationships, they desire to establish a more active association with the universe than that of speculation. They need some channel for their thoughts and feelings. Some parents write simple prayers for their children, but in most instances they teach the Lord's Prayer, which provides a medium of expression when children are either unable or do not wish to formulate their own. Even though the separate phrases may not be thoroughly understood, nevertheless, the prayer serves as an outlet for the child's religious feeling. Later, the deep implications of these phrases will become evident. The value of saying grace is that it makes God, as the source of all life, a familiar part of the children's daily experience, and therefore they are conscious of their dependence upon Him. But above all things, one must avoid the mistake of allowing prayers to become perfunctory exercises. If the family succumbs to this error, I believe that more harm than good is accomplished by such formal religious education. This cannot happen, however, if the family is one in which the spiritual values are the controlling influences of life.

To summarize, in the home the child is consciously disciplined, unconsciously moulded at the very center of his life by the spiritual environment, and given the language and the habits of expression for his feelings and thoughts. 

Here the most effective religious education is accomplished. 

III. THE SCHOOL AND RELIGIOUS EDUCATION 

There is in most states a statute which definitely forbids the public schools from giving religious instruction. This limitation of the curriculum is also observed by most private schools, except those definitely under denominational control. But it is impossible for a child to be under the influence of the school during so much of his life without receiving from it some of the important elements in the development of his religion. [4]

Here, as in the family, there is the conscious discipline not only for the correction of faults, but also for the establishing of right attitudes toward work and people. And, as in the home, the moral and spiritual calibre of adults has a marked effect upon the child's religious education. When a teacher takes an intelligent and sympathetic interest in children and keeps in mind the quality and needs of the personality of each pupil as well as the information to be transmitted, it is possible to help greatly in character development. The school has become increasingly concerned with the stimulation and expression of the aesthetic interests through art, literature and music. In providing children with definite techniques for the outlet and strengthening of their sense of the beautiful, it exerts a powerful influence on personality, which, as I have said, is of the utmost importance for the religious life. 

Although the school thus shares some of the responsibilities of the family, it also has its own special contribution to make because of the nature of its organization and the purpose of its existence. As soon as the child enters the community of the school, he finds companions who have quite different ways and standards from his own, and teachers whose sense of values may well be at variance with that of his parents. In short, it is his first real introduction into the life-long problem of living together with other people to whom he is not bound by ties of affection. This involves the question of the conflict of loyalties. 

The intellect has a large part to play in our religious life, and the work of the school is mainly concerned with the training of the mind. The school teaches the child to use his mind in an impersonal, logical manner. It teaches him to test information before he accepts it. Tested knowledge is one of the means of conquering the still unsubdued wilderness of unexplored mystery which surrounds our little clearing of human knowledge. Although the religious interpretation of knowledge by exegesis is definitely avoided in all schools except those under denominational control, teachers who have a genuine religious conviction of their own cannot help transmitting it to their pupils through the medium of whatever subject they may be teaching. Here is another potent unconscious influence. But the child is left to make his conscious interpretations in accordance with the particular religious point of view which he has acquired from such other sources as his home or his church. 

To summarize, the school provides-besides moral discipline, spiritual atmosphere and aesthetic opportunities-the important influences of social relationships and mental training, both of which are of great moment for religious education.

IV. THE CHURCH AND RELIGIOUS EDUCATION 

We come now to the peculiar responsibility of the church for religious education. In accordance with the theories thus far outlined, which give so much responsibility to the home and the school, it might seem that there is no place left for the church. Furthermore, the time allotted to the church is ordinarily only one hour out of the entire one hundred and sixty-eight of each week, and that for only nine months of the year in many communities. 

There are two possibilities open for the church in the use of the precious time at her disposal. On the one hand, she can strive to duplicate the method and work of a school, and pattern her organization and her regimen as closely upon that model as possible, with the addition of the deliberate inter- [5]

pretation in terms of religion of all the knowledge acquired. At the best, however, it seems to me that most churches are bound to be only weak imitations of the original. They cannot secure the type of teacher who is to be found in the best schools, nor are they able to provide a physical plant which is either as adequate or as well equipped as that to which the children go five days a week. Inevitably, then, by comparison in the thought of the children, the church school is bound to suffer and lose their respect. 

Besides this practical difficulty with which the majority, though not all of our churches are confronted, there are more serious disadvantages which are of even more importance. 

For the church to play the rôle of an imitator seems to betray, in the first place, the false assumption that the only influence in human behavior, and especially in religious development is that of utilitarian experience. In reality, however, the quiet interpretative meditation upon remembered experiences, and experiences which are ends in themselves both have a lasting influence on the development of human personality.

In the second place, eagerness to adopt the methods of a school connotes a lack of confidence in the institution of the church as a vital factor in developing religion. Because I am confident that the church and its method offer an unique contribution in religious education, I am convinced that we must function as a church rather than as a school. This unique contribution is the service of worship.

Let me remind you what part a church service should take in the development of religion. Its first purpose is to orient our emotions and thoughts so that we consider and judge all of life's experiences by their effect upon the furthering of moral and spiritual, rather than material, values. In one sense, then, the service is a preparation for the sermon which interprets either personal experiences or social trends in the light of moral and spiritual realities. 

But the service is not only a preparation for the sermon. Since it is a unit including the sermon, and as such has a distinctive function of its own, we are reminded that its second purpose is to give us the definite consciousness of the deep, fundamental impetus of human life toward truth, goodness and beauty,the purpose of God. In other words, it is to give us the sense of "communion with God." If these purposes are true for adults, it is certainly reasonable to assume that they are equally true for children. In support of that assumption, it is rather interesting that a questionnaire sent out recently by the Junior League of New York to a large number of children revealed that many of them preferred the church service to the church school. This indicates, it seems to me, that unconsciously the worship of the church was fulfilling a need in their lives which the school cannot and does not attempt to meet. 

Specifically, what do we wish to give children when we adopt the method of the church rather than that of the school for their religious education? 

There are four objectives. First, to teach children churchmanship. They must be convinced that if this institution is to survive from one generation to another, it can do so only because individuals take a definite personal responsibility for its continuance. Furthermore, they must know the possibilities within the present forms of organization and worship in order that if later the need arises to adapt those forms to new situations and conditions, it can be done intelligently without sacrificing acknowledged values in an eagerness to realize new ones. Hence, we have organized our Junior Church upon the pattern of our church organiza- [6]

tion and given to the children the actual responsibility for its functioning. Details of this plan are described in the next section. 

The second objective is to introduce the children into the abiding values and growing idealism of our religious tradition, that they may gain strength and inspiration from its power. Of course, that tradition is complex and much of it they learn about from studies in school. Literature, history, science, art-all these flow into the rich flood of religious tradition. The church is peculiarly responsible for transmitting a knowledge of the Bible, the main sources of the Christian tradition. The attempt is made to show how to read the Bible from the point of view of the ages out of which its different portions have arisen, rather than from the point of view of the Weltanschauung and standards of the twentieth century. Inevitably, therefore, the children perceive the progress of religious thought as it is reflected in the Bible. Hence they are prepared to meet with equanimity changes in their own religious thought, and with understanding sympathy the wide variety of opinions held by their contemporaries. This approach to the Bible also emphasizes the idea that religion is a growing and vital reality in the lives of individuals and races, as its fundamental principles are applied by each generation in turn to its own time. As a corollary to those purposes in Bible teaching, we point out to the children the influence of the Bible upon art, literature, music and daily experience. 

Our third objective is to preach sermons which, as all sermons ought to do, interpret the lives and experiences of the congregation in terms of the highest spiritual values and with the reality of God as a background. In these sermons we strive to arouse an enthusiasm for the life which is motivated by religion,—to make the thought of God a power. 

And finally, the supreme objective which alone justifies approaching the problem of religious education from the point of view of the church rather than the school is to teach children how to worship. One cause of the present indifference to churches is, I believe, that many people have never learned how to enter into a service of worship in such a way as to contribute to it for the benefit of others on the one hand, and to draw from it value for themselves on the other. As the modern educator is truly reminding us, the best way to learn is by doing. 

Therefore, we have a service of worship every Sunday, modelled upon the worship of the church. The very frequency of its use, the regularity of the repetition of its words and phrases charges it with the values of familiarity, and gives it a power which is cumulative in effect. Through this means, the church broadens the foundation of spiritual confidence laid by the love and moral discipline given by the family. Worship should convince the children that they are spiritually secure, not only in their homes, but anywhere and everywhere when they trust themselves to moral principles and the pursuit of spiritual values. In the service of worship, they find both an opportunity and a means of affirming and strengthening their consciousness of the spiritual Unity of the Universe,-of God. And if, as is often the case, the responsibility of giving the children not only explicit language, but also forms for the expression of their religious sense has not been accepted by the family, the service of worship has a doubly important meaning in their lives.

To summarize then, the contribution of the church to religious education is to develop churchmanship, to impart a vital knowledge of our traditions especially as embodied in the Bible and church history, to illuminate life by preaching with the light of the thought of God, but above all to fulfill its unique function:-to provide the opportunity for public worship. V. THE ORGANIZATION AND PRACTICE OF THE JUNIOR CHURCH 

The one thought to keep always in mind is that we have a church and not a school. With this distinction clearly made, the organization and practice are easily understood.

A. ORGANIZATION 

All children from the ages of eight to fifteen inclusive are eligible for membership. The organization in general is patterned upon that of the adult church. Every child in the course of the year serves on some committee. The committees, which are formed in order to meet the actual needs of the functioning of the Junior Church,—are the following: Standing Committee; Flower 

Committee; Ushers; Bell Ringers; Church Committee; and Book Committee.

We also have a Clerk and Treasurer with the usual duties of those two offices. 

Only the children from thirteen to fifteen are eligible for the Standing Com mittee and form a group of Aides who perform various duties before the service. 

A gowned choir under a paid director sings an anthem at our service about twice a month.

The Standing Committee meets as occasion demands and decides such matters as the uses to which the offering shall be put, determines policies, considers or makes any suggestions for the welfare of the Junior Church, and plans all parties and meetings. The Flower Committee arranges the flowers on the altar, lights the candles, and may take some of the flowers to members of the Junior Church who are ill. The Ushers show visitors to seats, give them orders of service, and take the offering. At the close of the service they remove the 

Junior Church hymn books from the pews, and put them away on the shelves in the parish house. The Bell Ringers ring the church bell fifteen minutes before service and again two minutes before the service begins. The Church Committee puts the hymn books in the pews before the service. The Book Committee replaces the note-books in alphabetical order on the shelves in the parish house. 

I shall describe these note-books and their use later. These are the committees we find necessary at present for our situation. In another church, however, some of these might be found unnecessary and others might need to be added. 

We are very insistent that the committees shall take genuine responsibility. 

A chairman is chosen for each committee and given a list of the committee members and the dates on which they are to be on duty. The chairman telephones the child assigned to duty on the Friday before his or her turn comes, and if the child cannot be present, sees that some other member of the committee substitutes. In case of the failure of a committee to function, no one else except the chairman is allowed to do its work. It happens occasionally, therefore, that for example, we have no flowers on the altar. Or when the 

Church Committee has failed to put out the hymn books, which contain the Order of Service, we have only the three parts of the service which the minister conducts alone: the Introduction to the Scripture, the Reading and the Sermon. 

Even when we have visitors, we make no exception to our rule of giving entire responsibility to the children. In this way, we are trying to teach them what we have referred to before as churchmanship-a sense of responsibility for the organization itself and the consciousness that unless each faithfully does his part, the church cannot function. And since our committees change twice a year, in October and February, all the children sooner or later will be familiar with the duties of every committee for which they are eligible. Our hope is [8]

that we are thus training a group of loyal and responsible church members for the present Junior Church, and also for the future church. We find that the children enjoy and fulfill responsibilities which we are frequently told by adults they will not perform.

Some adult, either the minister or an assistant, must do the detailed work of arranging the committees, changing them at the middle of the year, and being at the church on Sundays to give the children advice, if they ask it, or to help in other ways. As the years have progressed, however, the children have naturally needed less assistance.

B. THE SERVICE 

Remember, again, that we are conducting a church and not a school, and therefore, the service of worship is the most vital element of our plan. There are no classes here, just as there are no classes in the adult church. The children, like their parents, come to church on Sunday morning for the worship of God. 

Our service is held in the church itself from half-past nine o'clock until quarter past ten. The church organist plays, and the minister conducts the service. The children enter by the main door of the church and sit wherever they please. We merely ask that they sit in front of the ushers, whose places are in the sixth row from the back, one on each side of the middle aisle, thus concentrating the congregation at the front of the church. When the children enter the church, we take for granted the same quiet reverence which we desire from the adults at their service. If the children come early, their laughing and talking is confined to the parish house. 

The service of worship should, as we have said, be patterned upon the regular morning service of the church, so that the children will become familiar with that Order of Worship. At the end of this account are two Orders of Worship, each modelled upon the worship of the parent church. One is that of the Junior 

Church I conducted at Taunton, and the other, the one being used in Chestnut Hill. The marked difference between them is caused by the fact that in Taunton, we had the Order of Service which is found in most of our Unitarian Churches. At Chestnut Hill,2 on the other hand, local tradition has given us a service modelled upon the worship of King's Chapel in Boston, which, in turn, is a revision of the Episcopal service. It will be noticed that in the latter, the 

Exhortation and the General Confession have been rewritten so as to retain the mood of the original, but to meet the interests and the experiences of children, The prayer and musical responses also vary from those used in the adult church. 

The responsive readings in both cases are from the Book of Psalms. If desired, however, Psalms or other passages which the children have learned by heart can be substituted for the responsive reading. The Introduction to the Scripture which appears in the Junior Church services is not in the adult services, but will be explained under the section Instruction below. 

We find that the atmosphere of worship minimizes disciplinary problems. 

At times the minister has found it necessary to look directly at a child who is inconsiderately inattentive. If this has not had the desired effect, a pause in the service will almost always suffice. The most stringent measure ever used was to speak directly to a child by name. Such measures, however, have been used only a few times, and are becoming constantly less necessary as the

C. INSTRUCTION 

Our principal aim is to teach our children how to enter into a service of worship, and to accustom them to the religious point of view. For this reason, our Junior Church service is our main responsibility and our chief interest. 

I repeat, our technique is that of a church and not of a school. Naturally, the understanding of the service will be increased by knowledge of our religious tradition. Quite properly the church should be held responsible for transmitting it. Hence, we strive to give the children a knowledge of this tradition through that part of the service called the "Introduction to the Scripture." 

Our distinctive tradition begins with the Bible. The first year, therefore, a course of readings is arranged from the Old Testament about the main characters, including the most significant stories and passages which reflect the progress of religious thought. Each Sunday, one of these stories or passages is read as a Scripture lesson. Before it is read, however, the minister spends about ten minutes giving the children the historical, social or intellectual background. 

It is not unlike introducing the Scripture reading in the adult church, or giving an exposition of the text at the beginning of a sermon to be sure that the congregation is thoroughly familiar with the context. Here also is an opportunity to point out to the children what especially to notice in the passage to be read. 

They are also told of the connection of the story with art, music, literature, and daily experiences. This is not a monologue, for questions are asked and answers given. Occasionally the children make observations of their own which are valuable and helpful.

During the second year, the same procedure is followed with the life and teaching of Jesus. The third year, the Book of Acts is used as the basis for the 

Scripture reading. The fourth year, church history from the New Testament days to the present is surveyed with special attention to the history of liberalism and Unitarianism. The passages chosen for the Scripture reading in the fourth year are from different parts of the Bible. Passages are read which are appropriate to the individual or incident under consideration. Thus, there is a cycle of four years, at the end of which we turn again to the Old Testament. In the next cycle, different material (except for the most important incidents) but the same method is used.

To summarize: since our fundamental thesis is that we are conducting a church and not a school, I wish to emphasize that the instruction must be an integral part of the service of worship. 

D. THE SERMON 

The sermon, like every sermon, interprets the lives of the congregation in terms of religious ideals and the thought of God. Here, in simple language and within the range of experience of children, are discussed not only ethical problems, but also such great universal questions as those of death, immortality, the nature of religion, and the meaning of God. My experience has shown that children are more keenly interested in philosophical and metaphysical questions than they are in ethical instruction. In the sermon, as well as in the “Introduction to the Scripture" there is a certain degree of informality, for the children sometimes help to build the sermon by questions, answers, observations, and illustrations. The sermon is very seldom cast in the form of a story, because we feel that stories tend to limit the ability of the listener to make definite personal applications.

E. MISCELLANEOUS OBSERVATIONS 

Around this central purpose of worship, and within this form of organization many other interests can be developed such as committees or clubs. There can be week-day meetings of Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, Community Clubs, or any others similar to the Laymen's League, the Women's Alliance and the Young People's Societies which are affiliated with the adult church organization. We frequently hold Junior Church parties and entertainments for which the children themselves are responsible.

The distribution of the money received in the offering is a method of awak, ening the children to community responsibilities. Some of it is used for the expenses of the Junior Church and some for various charities. The children choose the charities and causes to be helped, but we advise them to support different types,-as for instance, an educative institution, a remedial one, one which is concerned with the responsibility for social reform, and one which is charitable in the direct sense of that term. Representatives of whatever agencies are thus supported are invited to the Junior Church to tell of the work which is being done.

When the new committees assume their responsibilities in February and [ 11 ]

October, a simple service of dedication becomes part of the regular morning worship. At the close of the prayer after the sermon, the congregation stands and repeats with the minister the following Act of Dedication:

We, the officers and members of the Junior Church of the First Church in Chestnut Hill, dedicate ourselves to the special duties of our positions and pledge our loyal support to the welfare of our Church which is gathered to help us to lead pure, reverent and useful lives by seeking together the love which quickeneth service and the truth which maketh free.

The last part of this Act of Dedication is the Covenant of the Junior Church which each new member signs as he or she is received into membership by the minister who, on behalf of the Junior Church, extends to him or her the Right Hand of Fellowship. At this time, also, we present each new member with a copy of the Bible, which is usually brought to Junior Church each Sunday, so that the reading of the Scripture may be followed, thus increasing the familiarity of the children with the Bible.

Another custom is, since we found that some of the children were reading the Bible by themselves during the week, to publish monthly a list of other passages and stories besides the ones specifically chosen for the Scripture Reading, coming from the same period, or bearing on the same theme. 

In our church it is the custom to celebrate the Symbolic Communion Service on the first Sundays of November, January and May. This service also forms part of the Junior Church worship, being included just prior to the singing of the last hymn.

A word should be written about attendance. On the wall of the parish house is a large sheet of cardboard with the names of all the members alphabetically listed, and a square for each Sunday. Each child records his own attendance. A cross indicates presence, a circle absence because of illness, and a blank space an unexcused or avoidable absence. Under no conditions do we ever urge attendance, or try to entice the children to come. We even discourage undue pressure on the part of the parents. We want the children to come of their own accord because they find it valuable and interesting. Our records show that we have had better attendance than the church school under the system of constant urging and pleading. We have had during five years an average attendance of twothirds of our membership, varying only in tenths or hundredths, and exclusive of our Christmas and Easter congregations when no separate record of the children's attendance is kept. We hope that the children come with only mildly mixed motives! We have the custom of presenting a book to those who have had perfect attendance throughout the year. We never mention this book in the course of the year and even seriously consider dropping this recognition of faithful effort.

Twice a year, or more often if necessary, there is a Junior Parish meeting at which matters of business upon which the Standing Committee desires the opinion of the entire group is held in the parish house. This meeting is frequently preceded by a Junior Parish Supper. In the arrangements for this, we have some assistance from the adult Religious Education Committee. 

This paper has described the procedure of but one Junior Church. Others have been established for some years with interesting and valuable variations from the practice in Chestnut Hill, in churches of our own and other denominations. Local situations dictate different needs; varying personalities of ministers [ 12 ]

or leaders reflect themselves in different ways of conducting the Junior Church. 

Within the limits of the church organization and procedure we are constantly finding new possibilities. Every year we have added something here, or altered something there which has made an increasingly effective children's church. 

VI. CONCLUSION 

In final summary, the home and the school have definite responsibilities for religious education. Each has its own method. The church has an equally important responsibility. It should have its own method which is neither that of the home nor the school. Experience convinces us that this method is that of the 

Junior Church. I am confident that for children, the Junior Church is the right means of fulfilling a deep and ever-present need of man-the need of worship. [13]
