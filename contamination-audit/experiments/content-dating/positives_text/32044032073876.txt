10 ON NE of the chief reasons for the greatness of the American newspaper is the fact that newspapermen themselves believe in their papers, in their mission and responsibility as editors and publishers. Because of their confidence in the worth of their papers, they have often been overconfident concerning the place and power of the press, inclined to be too much satisfied with the progress the daily and weekly press has made.

Now and then a progressive publisher stands off on the sidelines and views the publishing department of the newspaper industry objectively. When he does, he commands the attention of everyone interested in newspaper making. 

Fred I. Archibald, president of the New York State Publishers Association, did this at the 1944 meeting of the New 

York Circulation Managers Association. Mr. Archibald is president and publisher of the Albany (New York) Times Union and speaks with authority when he talks. It is for these reasons that I am glad to include Mr. Archibald's address in the Syracuse University School of Journalism Publications as a foresighted and farsighted "Peek at the Road Ahead" in newspaper publishing.

A Peek at the Road Ahead by 

FRED I. ARCHIBALD President, New York State Publishers Association President and Publisher, Albany (New York) Times-Union 

N DISCUSSING my subject, "A Peek at the Road Ahead,” I must

IN realize that in going from one place to another, a traveler nearly always has a choice of roads and, of course, must decide which road he is going to travel in order to take a peek along the right road. 

I am reminded of two roads that run from Nice to Monte Carlo and beyond, known in France as the Rue de Haut and the Rue de Bas-the high road and the low road. The high road is a scenic and beautiful road, always sunny because it runs along the base of the Maritime Alps -smooth, straight, and wide-and affords a wonderful view of the Azure coast. The Rue de Bas, the low road, is the one favored by taxi drivers and natives because one does not have to climb to get started on it. It is slightly shorter than the Rue de Haut and consequently uses less gasoline, but it is bumpy and somewhat narrow, with many crooked turns. 

Throughout life it seems there is always a high road and a low road. This fact can be turned into a metaphor. The high road can be thought of as broad and associated with broadmindedness. Its straightness brings to mind a straight and honest policy. Its smoothness can be associated with pleasant public and labor relations; its freedom from bumps reminds us that business peaks and depressions are not a part of it. Too, it has a firm base, comparable to sound rates for circulation and advertising.

The high road, they say, passes through cities with most attractive names, such as Progress, then through Achievement and, later on, Happiness. The natives say it eventually goes to Utopia, which, of course, is far away.

The low road is usually narrow, suggesting narrowmindedness. It is winding, which might mean a vacillating policy. It is bumpy-with troubles, I guess; and even though it is shorter, it passes through uninteresting, undesirable places named Confusion, Turbulence, and Disappointment. And again the natives say this road eventually leads to a dreary place called Bankruptcy.

From the foregoing, it seems desirable to choose the high road for our peek, and if we, as an industry, are to get on it and stay on it, there are many things we must examine and resolve firmly to do. 585115 www

The questions seem to divide themselves into six groups: (1) What about publishing costs? (2) What about circulation distribution? (3) 

What about advertising selling methods? (4) What about mechanical methods? (5) What about content and format? (6) What about public acceptance and confidence? 

Let me take Number 1 first-What about publishing costs? 

1. Publishing Costs 

It is generally agreed that publishing costs are going to be on a permanently higher plateau. Apparently there is not going to be a change in the trend of wages within the next several years. Certainly the Canadians are going to resist strenuously any reduction in the present print paper price of $58 per ton. And it is generally agreed that none of the other goods and services that newspapers buy are going to be materially reduced.

Newspapers, therefore, are going to be in a vulnerable position if, by any chance, circulation and advertising rates or volume should materially decline. Nearly 100 per cent of our operating expense is more or less fixed by labor contracts and high established prices for goods and services. Consequently, it will be most difficult to make a substantial reduction in operating costs to meet a declining income. 

For years the newspaper business has been, figuratively speaking, the poor relation of the communications industry. As we know, radio and magazines have been able to steal newspaper talent almost at will. 

State and national political organizations have been able to shanghai our best editorial and reportorial people, to make them executive assistants at double their former salary. All in all, we have been wide open for raids. Our competitive media have been so financially plump that they could toss vast sums into huge promotional benefits to promote their respective media and could man this effort with brilliant minds to give the campaign teeth and a punch to make it effective. 

To meet these threats and to keep our great industry on the firm basis it merits, we must do two things: (1) We must not let a declining cycle of circulation and advertising rates develop. (2) We must not revert to our former position where our financial strength and progress were so dependent on the volume of advertising. 

2. Circulation Distribution Methods 

IN THIS group we probably have our greatest opportunity. Manpower shortages have bedeviled the newspaper industry, but have undoubtedly hit the circulation department hardest. This should automatically correct itself soon after the cessation of hostilities.

To traverse the high road successfully, we must do many things: (1) We must provide adequate and on-time deliveries at the time and in the place where the reader wants his paper. When manpower problems have passed, the war lethargy of branch managers, supervisors, and carriers must be overcome and the whole circulation system galvanized into a proud, efficient, smoothly functioning organization. (2) Carriers, dealers, and street salesmen must be amply paid. How long can the reader expect a carrier to bring him a paper every day, through storm and ice and rain, for about a cent and a quarter a trip? Our own government charges ten cents for a special delivery; a parcel service delivery charges a quarter. Dealers and street salesmen must also eventually receive a fee commensurate with the service they give. (3) Circulation branches, stands, and trucks have great promotional value and must reflect the ideas and ideals of the institution of which they are a part. There are too many dingy branches and muddy, unpainted trucks. With the end of the war we must make assets of these potent promotional possibilities. (4) We must constantly enlarge our newspaper frontiers. No city or town has a greater trading area or radius of influence than the diameter of its newspaper's circulation. It should be very much to the interest of the merchants of your city, your chamber of commerce, and all civic minded citizens to see that circulation areas are constantly expanded. (5) Above all, circulation rates must be based on the worth of the product-not as heretofore, when low circulation rates were a habit and a deficit was avoided or a profit hoped for through the sale of advertising. 

Newspapers at double their present circulation prices would still be the world's greatest value, considering the world-wide news and picture services, the educational information, and the clean entertainment they provide. 

3. Advertising Selling Methods 

HERE there must be many complete changes of thought and practice: (1) We can no longer militantly sell The Wahoo Wasp first, uber alles, but must sell newspapers first and our particular paper next. Think of the thousands of capable newspaper advertising salesmen that would be behind the selling of newspapers as a medium if this procedure were followed. (2) We must have a strong, superbly functioning Bureau of 

Advertising, adequately and brilliantly staffed and amply supplied with funds. (3) We must sell more than white space. We must sell our customers pride in the fact that they are newspaper advertisers. We must do everything possible to continue and increase results from newspaper copy. We must give advertisers full color and brilliantly printed blackand-white facilities. (4) We must simplify and unify classified national

4. Mechanical Methods 

THERE has been an appalling lack of progress in printing methods and machinery during the past thirty to forty years. We have practically stood still while nearly every other industry has made startling, even sensational improvements. The web press of today is nearly the same old noisy monster its grandfather of 1910 was. The typesetting machine is still big, intricate, cumbersome—an awful lot of machinery to produce a little line of type. Practically the only recent improvements have been the multiple magazines and automatic melting pots. 

We could analyze most newspaper machinery and find a terrifying lack of technological progress. But I am not criticizing each machine individually nearly as much as the whole cumbersome process we go through to get indentations in a page matrix. Why hasn't some bright soul in the newspaper business or the allied equipment industries invented and developed in the past sixty years a quicker, cleaner, more efficient way to produce a newspaper mechanically? Why hasn't someone improved or revolutionized the photo-engraving process? There must be a more direct, faster, better way to reproduce photographs. To my knowledge, one newspaper group has spent nearly a quarter of a million dollars in experiments on photographic reproduction. 

These problems should be problems of the newspaper industry. 

The best inventive talent of the nation should be working constantly in laboratories financed jointly by the newspaper industry and newspaper equipment manufacturers.

War always makes great technological strides. Decades of mechanical progress are made during a few years of war. Every industry but ours seemed to benefit mechanically from World War I. Let's hope that some of the sensational strides in technology during World War II will find their way into newspaper mechanical methods. 

It is a responsibility of every executive in the industry to help bring this about.

But back to our subject, and peeking again along the high road. 

I feel sure you will see the following developments in the next few years: (1) Color facilities in nearly all dailies, with full four-color printing in the larger ones. (2) General and nation-wide use of electronic transmission of news and pictures. (3) A great advance in picture-taking and -making equipment and methods, due to war inventions. (4) Transmis-

5. Content and Format 

OUR editors are learning much from this war. For one thing, they are now sure of the value of maps and pictures, and are becoming equally sure the reader would like very much to have the map or picture a part of, or immediately adjoining, the story it illustrates. 

Another thing they are learning is that there is an accelerated return of public interest in the editorial page—with greater mass education, assisted in part by magazines, radio, and the movies. Our readers, generally, have an awakening interest in civic, national, and international questions. There is also a gentle revolution going on in their minds. On most subjects they don't want to be led around. They want all the facts from us, with decisions left to them. 

Then, too, the newspapers are providing more and more entertainment for the whole family. Comics, short and serial stories, informative and instructive columns and features are bringing to millions the clean, varied, wholesome entertainment they want and can get from no other source. I think entertainment features will grow in variety and improve constantly in quality.

Brilliant and skillful condensation of news is gaining steadily in public favor. Long, verbose stories are boring generally, and are interesting to a relatively small percentage of the people. Newspaper rationing has brought about the necessity, but editorial departments have been quick to improve the technique of condensation. It is generally believed that condensation will continue after rationing and will improve greatly as high-grade specialists are developed to handle it. 

The newspaper format, generally speaking, will undergo great changes in the next few years. Still more improvements will be made in easily-read type faces for body matter. New styles of make-up will develop, providing a cleaner, more orderly, and much more appetizing appearance.

And let's remember that the progress of newspapers down the road ahead depends on only two factors: first, how good editors make them; second, how far circulation managers take them. 

6. Public Acceptance and Confidence 

CALL it promotion, public relations, or whatever you will, it seems to me that newspapers, generally speaking, have done the world's worst

job of selling themselves. For years they have sold everybody's toothpaste, automobiles, and breakfast food with remarkable efficiency and energy, but have permitted radio, the magazines, and other far less efficient and far less reaching media of communication to steal the limelight and public attention.

Glib radio commentators adroitly take the three newspaper-built, world-wide wire services, weave into these factual reports shadings of meaning, guesses, and interpretations of their own, and the public in a recent poll gave them a higher vote of confidence and interest than it did the newspapers.

Look through leading magazines today and you find that the great majority of name writers got their start on newspapers. We couldn't hold them because, at that time, we didn't have the "moola" to make it sufficiently attractive. 

Local radio advertisers smile and beam with pride when they listen to their broadcast; but let them try to sell a specific item at a specific price over that program, and they will listen in vain for the jingle of the cash register.

Public acceptance and confidence are by no means unobtainable for newspapers as a whole. They are, in fact, easier for newspapers than any other group because we already have the two necessary factors: (1) a total situation that honestly deserves acceptance and confidence; (2) the medium that reaches farthest, firstest, and oftenest to tell our story. 

Then, too, we have dozens of intelligent, well-educated, stable citizens who are an important part of every community and who, at the same time, are the executives of our newspapers. No other media have that favorable situation, and if all of us in all our daily contacts talk, think, eat, and live the inherent honesty, the desire for service, helpfulness, and the high ideals and aims of our nation's newspapers, half the battle will be won.

The other half is a matter of promotion-the daily telling and retelling in a dramatic, interesting way of the hundreds of helpful human acts newspapers do; of their great part in civic, state, and national activities, and their genuine desire and determination to be accurate, friendly, and fair at all times. 

Let's make a firm, solemn resolve that this association and the hundreds of other fine groups get on the high road, stay on the high road, and definitely put newspapers permanently in the high position they so honestly deserve.
