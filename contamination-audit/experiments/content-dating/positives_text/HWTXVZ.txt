FOREWORD 

In this small volume I have attempted to bring together many pertinent facts, figures and experiences concerning the Soviet Union. My aim has been to arrange these in brief, condensed, disconnected paragraphs, with a minimum of detail and a maximum of clarity so that they may be quickly read and easily understood.

The facts and figures used herein are taken from the inner control figures of the Soviet Government which were supplied by their leading authorities. Because of my extensive investigations and travels this information has been viewed from many angles and I believe it to be as accurate as it is possible to make it.

The views and expressions given are the results of my personal, undirected observations and studies, which cover a period of eleven years. During this time I have made nine trips to the Soviet Union, most of them in recent years, having returned from the last one this past summer.

These economic studies of the Soviet Union have led me to become personally acquainted with the President and practically all of the highest government officials, as well as most of the presidents and Board members of the various buying Trusts, factory and commercial organizations. My travels within that country during all periods of the year and under most varying conditions, have covered more than 65,000 miles. 4 It is with this background that I venture to present this modest volume, hoping that its contents will be of help to those seeking quick and ready facts on the Soviet 

Union of today..

The Soviet Union has within its borders about 200 different nationalities and races.

INDUSTRY 

13 

The Soviet Union has come into existence at the opportune time. She is profiting immensely both in time and money by taking advantage of the latest developments in methods and machines of the most advanced industrial nations. This, together with her abundance of natural resources, her nationally planned economy and the energy and virility of her workers, makes it absolutely within reason to say that the

Soviet Union should and will become an industrial nation in twenty years, or in about half the time it has taken the most advanced nations of today to arrive at their present state of industrial perfection. 

14 New Soviet factories resemble huge laboratories for the display of the world's most modern machines and technique. These plants are located where they should be, with due regard to the future and are planned with the aid of the best foreign specialists available, be they Americans, Germans or English. The most modern machinery is imported. To begin with, when a plant is opened many of the key workers are foreigners but the Soviet workers, eager for knowledge, learn quickly and soon take over operation of the plant. 

15 

Huge sums of money have been expended yearly by the Soviet Union for the develop-

Industry ment of her basic industries. During the initial building period it was hard to justify these expenditures with words and promises.

Now that these large factories and undertakings are coming into production at the rate of over five hundred a year, their products speak in convincing terms. The old saying, "The proof of the pudding is in the eating" has great significance in the Soviet Union. 

16 

Despite the phenomenal industrial growth of the new Soviet factories they cannot supply the demands of the people which are created by the rapidly increasing standards of living. There is always a shortage of everything and this will undoubtedly continue for many years to come.

During the last few years Soviet scientists have been working out methods for the manufacture of synthetic rubber. The first plant for this purpose has been built in Yaroslavl and began to function during the latter part of this past July. The success of this operation is encouraging and the construction of two additional plants for the

Industry manufacture of synthetic rubber is now nearing completion. A new plant called tau-sagiz was recently discovered in the mountains of Kora-Tau in Kazakstan, which contains 40% of high-grade rubber. 

More recently a new plant called kok-sagiz has been discovered which contains up to 12% of rubber. Many experiments are being carried on with these plants. These, together with the previously known plants, the Mexican guillo and the hondrilla, are now being cultivated on a large scale. The 

Soviet Union expects that with the development of its synthetic rubber industry and the great extension in acreage devoted to the production of these rubber bearing plants, it will very shortly become independent of the rubber markets of the world.

42 There is no unemployment in the Soviet 

Union. Its "liquidation" has dispelled a great human fear-that of becoming jobless. Likewise, the fear of illness and old age has greatly diminished, due to the granting of insurance and old age pensions.

44 

Unions exist in all trades and as a rule all workers belong to a Union although membership is not compulsory. Their efforts, therefore, are not dissipated in strife between union and non-union workers or between employers and workers, but are wholly directed towards improving their conditions through better and more economical industrial production. All of which eventually raises the general standards of living of the masses and tends toward the elimination of privileged classes. 45 

The membership of the Labor Unions in

Soviet Russia has grown from 11,000,000 to 16,500,000 in the last three years. 83% of the industrial workers are employed seven hours per day with one day off in six. 46 

There is no compulsory labor in the

Labor Soviet Union. All prisons or workers' colonies as they are called, are provided with work shops of various kinds and all inmates are given a chance to work or learn a trade as a means of preparing themselves for better citizenship. If they decide to work, and practically all of them do, they are paid the regular union scale of wages and all working conditions must meet Union requirements. Part of the wages go to the institution for board and part to the prisoner's family. 

47 

The extreme dictatorial attitude of labor so common in the early years of the Soviet Union, has largely disappeared and labor discipline necessary to successful industrial production is now prescribed by the Labor 

Union Congress. 

48 

So-called "socialistic competition" which was first started in 1930 has become a principle means of increasing efficiency and production within the Soviet Union. The workers of one factory challenge the workers of another similar factory to enter into friendly competition in fulfilling and surpassing their industrial program for the year. Then they compete with each other for higher productivity. There are numerous competitive groups of workers and individual workers within factories also striving for better quality and increased output. "Socialistic competitions" take on many other varied forms. For instance, the residents on one side of a street compete with those on the other side in keeping them repaired and beautified. Competitions are also conducted between the different sections of a city and likewise between cities; in other words the forms of these competi-

Labor are tions are endless and much publicity surrounds them. Victorious groups awarded large sums of money running into thousands of rubles which sums are invariably used for some needed form of social improvement in their factories. Those workers who have demonstrated their willingness and ability to accomplish much good work are given special recognition of various kinds. They are honored at public meetings, they are given positions of increased responsibility and oftentimes are given trips abroad as well as free scholarships in leading Soviet educational institutions where they may prepare themselves for still greater usefulness. 49 

"Campaigns against waste" are employed to discourage carelessness which leads to waste and tends to decrease production. For instance, in some of the large factories there may be found what are called "Cemeteries of Waste." These as a rule are small spaces probably 20 to 40 ft. square, resembling grass covered graves and crossed by paths. 

These "cemeteries" are usually located in prominent places. On each of these "graves", instead of roses, may be found a collection of several stakes supporting defective parts of machines made within the plant. Each one of these defective parts is conspicuously labeled with an epitaph bearing the full name of the worker whose carelessness brought on the expensive waste together with a description of his faulty methods, etc. Workers, of course, greatly dislike to have their carelessness so exhibited and ridiculed by their fellow workers. 

50 

Because of the great demand in the Soviet Union for manufactured goods and the

Labor eagerness with which they are purchased, carelessness in manufacturing frequently creeps in. To overcome this and to place the blame where it belongs, large stores which are assigned these products for sale, often-times devote a part of their best showwindow space for exhibiting the defective articles. For example, stores which have received shipments of ready made clothing find several garments showing glaring faults in workmanship. These garments are so arranged in special show-window spaces as to clearly reveal their imperfections and in addition are fully labeled outlining the defects and giving the names and addresses of the factories which produced the garments, together with the dates and as many names of the officials of the plants in question as is possible. Newspaper reporters see these exhibits and are soon on the job at the factories where they succeed in getting more detailed information regarding the plants and their management. This is all given much publicity in the press and soon the Government Trust owning these plants begins an investigation which might result in removals and even punishment if it is shown that negligence occurred. 51 

The Soviet Union, with the adoption of the American method of mass production in its factories, has felt the extreme importance of correcting the habits of temporarily inefficient workers, which are so often brought on by drink. Campaigns of various kinds for the discouragement of drinking amongst factory workers have been given much consideration during the past few years. 

Teamwork is now essential, whereas in pre-war days the exacting mass production methods with the conveyor

Labor system of manufacturing were unknown.

One of the effective methods used today in many plants, is as follows: records are kept of individuals who come to work showing the effects of drink or other causes for delinquencies. Their names are sent to the paymaster. When they, with hundreds of other workers, line up at the paymaster's window expecting to receive their pay in the regular way, they are informed that it will be necessary for them to step to the "Black Window." This window is generally located a distance of from five to ten feet from the regular window. It usually has large border around it painted jet black and covered with various pictures depicting the evils resulting from drink, etc., such as writhing snakes, ragged, bearded drunkards beating their wives, starving children, ringing alarm clocks, asses, etc. When a few of these delinquents have gathered at the "Black Window" the paymaster closes his regular window in spite of the long line of workers still waiting before it, steps to the other window and pays off the individuals who have been directed there. In the meanwhile those at the "Black Window" have been derided and ridiculed by their fellowworkers. However, in the end they are invariably offered help and encouragement in order that they may do better in the future.

53 

The Soviet Union has a greater abundance and variety of natural resources than any country in the world. These resources are being studied and checked by a large number of geological expeditions with scarcely a month passing without new and valuable deposits being found. 54 The Soviet Union is expending more money for geological surveys than any other country. ELECTRIFICATION 

61 ―- Electricity is the new life blood of the 

Soviet Union. Regional electric generating stations are being built so as to ultimately provide a net work of electric transmission lines over the country, which will supply all cities, villages and factories with cheap light and power. In 1931 the Soviet 

Union ranked seventh amongst the countries of the world in the production of electric power. The extensive water power resources of the country are being harnessed. 

The Dnieper River has been conquered by the Dnieprostroy Dam and now the ancient Volga water-way is soon to feel the shock of modern science in the form of steel and concrete in three separate and distinct places. 62 

The recently completed Dnieprostroy 

Dam, March 1932, across the mighty 

Dnieper River in the southern part of the 

Ukraine, is the largest masonry dam ever built. It contains 968,000 cubic yards of concrete. Its length is 2500 ft. and its height 170 ft. which raises the water of the river over 120 ft. It will serve three purposes namely, to generate electricity, supply irrigation and facilitate shipping. The generating plant of the dam, when completed, will be equipped with 9 turbo-generators, each with a capacity of 84,000 HP. The

Dnieprostroy power plant has a normal generating capacity of 756,000 HP and a maximum of 900,000 HP. 

63 The irrigation project connected with the dam will use surplus water and also surplus electric power generated at night, for pumping water into large reservoirs on

Electrification elevated places. From these reservoirs water will be drawn for irrigating 150,000 acres of land through surface irrigation, sub-irrigation and the spray system of irrigation. The raising of the level of the river above the dam has submerged the great rapids which formerly obstructed navigation and divided the river into two sections, the first section being from the Black Sea to Dnieprostroy, a distance of 200 miles and the second being the one above the rapids, a distance of 1100 miles. With the use of the three locks at the dam the Dnieper River will now become navigable to 

Black Sea ships from the Black Sea to 

Dniepropetrovsk, 62 miles above the Dam and to smaller ships for a distance above the Dam of 1100 miles or practically the entire length of the river which is 1300 miles. 

This makes navigable a water-way linking the Baltic to the Black Sea. 

64 

Of the three large dams to be built across the Volga River in the near future, the one at Kamishin on the Lower Volga, will make the Dnieprostroy Dam look like an infant in comparison. Its electric generating capacity will be three times that of the Dnieprostroy Dam and its surplus waters will irrigate in the neighborhood of 8,000,- 000 acres of farm land. Thus this sleepy water-way trade route of the ancients will be awakened and put to work to contribute its share in feeding and clothing the millions of souls that live in its valleys.

A collective farm (kolhoz) is a voluntary association of peasants for the joint culti-

Agriculture vation of the land. The basic means of production (machinery, tools, horses) are owned in common, but the individual members retain their own houses and gardens and also whatever cows, pigs, sheep and poultry they may possess. 

78 

Modern machinery and methods of cultivation are employed. The Soviet Government supplies the collective farms with selected seed and with tractors and agricultural implements on long term credits and also loans them the use of such machinery through the state machine tractor stations.

The Government also supplies the collective farms with selected seeds and agronomical advice. The collective farms, on their part, contract to sell to the Government a certain percentage of the grain and other crops raised at a price which must not be below a fixed minimum. The share of these state grain collections in the total output of the collective farms has been greatly reduced this year and the collective farms are encouraged to sell the balance of their crops. on the public market. The net income of the farm is divided at the end of each year amongst the members and the return per peasant is in proportion to the quantity and quality of work that he performs.

A new type of medical institution developed in the Soviet Union is the socalled "night sanitarium" for industrial workers. 164 

The marriage procedure in Soviet Russia is exceedingly simple. All that is required of the bride and bridegroom is that they sign their names in the register book. No witnesses are necessary. Both parties are urged by the health authorities to submit certificates of health to each other. The fees charged by the Bureau for Registration of Marriages is very small, ten rubles being the highest sum. Workers receiving small wages pay nothing at all, others pay five rubles. Those who have an income other than wages pay ten rubles. 165 - 

There are no illegitimate children as such, in the Soviet Union. The Soviet Government draws no line of difference between children born of married or unmarried parents; in either case the responsibility is the same for both parents. If a married couple secures a divorce or an unmarried couple separates, both sides must carry equal responsibility in bringing up the children born of that union. If a child remains with the mother the father pays his share of the expenses and vice versa. A disabled and unemployed father can demand alimony from the mother of his child if the child is in his

Law - Courts - Prisons care and the mother has a reasonable income. In case a father and mother are deemed unfit to care for the child, it is immediately taken charge of by the state and both the father and mother contribute to its support. If the amount is not sufficient the difference is supplied by the state but the child must be cared for first. All children are deemed in need of support up to 18 years of age. 

166 

According to Soviet reasoning a couple living together inharmoniously causes an economic loss and should be given a divorce. 

This procedure is very simple where children are not involved. All that is necessary is to go to the Bureau of Marriages, Divorces,

Deaths and Births and announce the divorce officially. The fee is exceedingly small, just enough to cover office expenses. If one party wishes a divorce, the consent of the other party is not necessary unless a property division question arises.

Under Soviet law the sentence rendered by a court is "not the vengeance of society on a criminal" but the protection of society from a repetition of the crime in the future. 

The accused, therefore, often do not attempt to understate the crime they have committed but try to show the impossibility of its repetition and their "social harmlessness" as well as their possible helpfulness to society in the future. Consequently they give full assistance to the court in solving the crime committed.

The heaviest prison sentence under Soviet law is ten years. Murder rarely draws a death sentence unless it is classified as a political murder or is accompanied by other

Law - Courts - Prisons serious felonies, such as banditism, counterrevolutionary activities, etc. Thus, execution is not believed to be the supreme punishment but the highest form of social defence.

Prisons in the Soviet Union are not places of punishment but are called houses of correction. All inmates are urged to work and those who do so are taught a trade of one kind or another in the shops and factories. 

Members of the colonies receive wages for their work which, when account is taken of food, housing, etc., amounts to those prescribed by the Unions. The members work five days and are free on the sixth to receive friends and relatives. Cells are usually equipped with radio loud speakers. In most prisons Udarniks (especially qualified members who have demonstrated their ability as leading workers, and those on good behavior) are permitted excursions into the city alone without guards. Rarely do they fail to return. Every prison has its club where the inmates stage their own plays, play chess, read, play on musical instruments, etc. Members are permitted to take books to their rooms. Most prisons have classes for illiterates and technical courses for those who work in the factories and have their own court to judge their delinquents and slackers.

203 American Soviet trade has suffered a severe set back because of the absence of normal relationships between the the two countries. For some time it was the expressed belief of persons high in governmental positions in Washington, that the then rapidly mounting Soviet purchases in the United States would of necessity continue and without normal relationships. The 

United States at that time was getting its full share of Soviet purchases in competition with European countries which had recognized the Soviet Government, so why do more than fill Soviet orders? Time has proven the fallacy of the judgment used then as is clearly shown by the recent rapid drop in Soviet purchases from us. Had the 

United States followed up the great advantages which it had gained in 1930 it would now be well entrenched in the Soviet markets. Now we must do the whole thing over again and at a time when European countries have gotten a firm grip on this whole market. The United States and the Soviet Union are the two largest self sustaining countries on the face of the earth. They both possess great quantities and varieties of natural resources, which when wisely used, contribute greatly to human welfare and happiness. 

Nations possessing them in great quantities are certain to develop the highest standards of living and consequent world leadership.

Neither the United States nor the Soviet Union want additional territory and the desire of the two countries for universal education and world peace is well known. 

Their basic underlying desires are very similar despite their external forms of government and which after all, are simply means for accomplishing their purposes. We have fought them on their homeland and have helped them in time of famine; we have refused to discuss settlements of claims at the Round Table and we have told them their country is an "economic vacuum." Later we dubbed their Five-Year Plan, "Russia's letter to Santa Claus." Yet we have feared this "economic vacuum" as one of our substantial competitors in foreign markets and have dreaded the flooding of our own country with Soviet made goods. We have hated, feared, misinterpreted and misunderstood their motives and they have likewise misconstrued ours. We have also listened attentatively to the propaganda of our European competitors. We developed a very good market in the Soviet Union and then threw it away. All of these things because of a lack of sympathetic understanding of a country emerging from centuries of misrule

It is but "history repeating itself" in a more virile way. We must think for ourselves and neither be guided nor affected by unfounded stories such as emanate from Riga date lines and which can be gotten on any phase of Soviet activity, in any degree of bitterness, for one hundred dollars or less. 

It is time that we as a nation again develop a sense of international tolerance such as was characteristic of our country a few decades ago. The adjustment of our tangent policies and the resurrection of our true American principles will, without doubt, again start our nation on the high road to new heights and the United States, together with the Soviet Union, will then lead the world to higher and better standards of living and greater and more lasting world peace.
