MANUFACTURED IN THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA

THE ATLANTIC CHARTER 

In America we believe that after the war we shall be able to establish a world-order as outlined in the Atlantic Charter. This document, which emerged from a meeting held somewhere in the Atlantic between President Roosevelt and Prime Minister 

Churchill in August 1941, is brief and to the point. Quoted therefrom are the following principles underlying a future peaceful relationship between nations:

Point 3 (in part): "They (The United States and 

Great Britain) respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live;" 

Point 4 (in full): "They will endeavor, with due respect for their existing obligations, to further the enjoyment by all states, great or small, victor or vanquished, of access, on equal terms, to the trade and to the raw materials of the world which are needed for their economic prosperity." 

Point 7 (in full): "Such a peace should enable all men to traverse the high seas and oceans without hinderance."

Anyone accustomed to think in terms of world commerce knows how far we strayed from these principles in prewar days. 

He realizes the great value of the Charter and regrets only that its doctrines were not applied to the peace made at Versailles. One who knows nothing about world trade subscribes to the Charter because he understands that its precepts are diametrically opposed to the purposes of our enemies. [1]

Indeed the Atlantic Charter embodies an idealism in international relationships such as the world has never heretofore visualized. The very loftiness of its purpose gives it the qualities of all things great and good, namely simplicity, practicability, and permanence. Some individuals feel this by intuition and have no doubt that we shall adhere to the Charter and establish permanent world peace. Others reason that any form of idealism has no greater value than its practicability. Their faith in the Charter would be greatly enhanced if they could visualize a plan to make its ideals effective. This story is written for just such people. 

A STORY AND A PLAN 

There must be more than one way of establishing a worldorder which will bring harmony and happiness to mankind. Not that we should in any detail depart from the Atlantic Charter; on the contrary, we cannot adhere to it too closely. But a question so broad as the one of World Peace should have more than one solution. There should be different peace plans. 

This is a story about an American Statesman who presents one such plan.

The story is entirely imaginary and will accomplish its purpose provided the reader grasps the plan and believes it to be practical. Then there shall have been added one more soul to the ranks of those who have unlimited faith in the Charter. 

[TO THE INITIATED] 

If you are a student of world economics or have made international trade your livelihood you do not expect a peace plan to be presented to you as a story about a statesman or about any other personality. A few terse paragraphs — the simpler the better 

[2]

would be sufficiently enlightening. The story contains such paragraphs.

In any brief treatise on a proposed peace plan you would weigh every word. No language involving the technique of overseas trade would confuse you. You would draw your own conclusions and present them to a few of your friends who like yourself are among the highly initiated. 

Provided you and your friends considered the plan feasible, there would have been added to the small number of people who have a thorough knowledge of international affairs a handful of men and women with new faith in the Charter. This would be worthwhile, and yet would have its limitations. 

[TO THE UNINITIATED] 

In a democracy not the viewpoint of a few but the conviction of many rules. This accounts for the retarded actions of democracies. It takes time to convince the masses. And it will take time to convince them of the practicability of the ideals set forth in the Atlantic Charter. Its high purpose and promise will provide ample inspiration during the days of combat and then peace will come. Then the promise must be fulfilled in our own democratic way which must be understood by many. Therefore it does not make a great deal of difference how a peace plan is presented,in the form of a story or in any other way, as long as it is understandable to a great majority of the people. Certainly no plan can be presented too soon.

The story is intended to make interesting reading to the uninitiated. By giving it a touch of drama obvious repetitions necessary in presenting so broad a subject have been obliterated. 

Notwithstanding these repetitions, a few paragraphs will bear re- [3]

reading by those who really desire to grasp a mind picture of a future harmonious world-order.

In this spirit the story is dedicated to all those who sorrow and live in the hope that today's great suffering holds promise of abundant peace for tomorrow. 

HUMANITY CHASTENED 

Span the gulf of unmeasured time and imagine a period immediately following today's global war.

Humanity, like the prodigal son, after spoiling its birthright of plenty and having undergone untold hardship and suffering, has returned to its senses. All nations have suffered, some more than others -some infinitely more. But the stark realities of want and sorrow have left an indelible imprint everywhere. Selfishness, inconsiderate thinking and acting have been trampled underfoot by the legions of war, and out of the mire and bloodshed has sprung forth a compassionate desire for understanding among men. The sword of murderous aggression and the gold of unwarranted complacency alike have been reduced to ashes in the crucible of exhausting effort.

When devastation on land and at sea, and hunger and want covering two-thirds of the globe had reached its zenith, an armistice was declared. An armistice quite as sudden and unexpected, but very different from the one in November 1918. An armistice without hilarity, without washtubs tied to automobiles, without mobs shouting drunkenly in a desire to forget what they never understood War. This armistice brought about by exhaustion and desperation sent over the earth a wave of gratitude [4]

such as mankind had never before experienced. Gratitude that the slaughter had ceased, gratitude that the hunger would soon be stilled, gratitude for the dawn of a new world-hope. 

WASHINGTON PEACE CONFERENCE 

The armistice set into motion immediately the agencies for relief and was followed promptly by the Washington Peace Conference. And here from the far corners of the blood-soaked earth came humbled men. Men who in their homelands had seen the eyes of loved ones bulging with terror over the destruction of exploding bombshells and flames. Men with vivid memories of comrades, filthy beyond recognition, weary beyond death, left behind on a dozen battle fields. Leaders among men, daring in their conceptions, courageous in their actions, unflinching in their purpose to "establish" and not "negotiate" a world peace. 

Singularly significant throughout the war, in the United States of America, a sense of hatred had never dominated the actions of either the military or the administrative heads of the government. Therefore when the Peace Conference was called in Washington, old hatreds the damnable seeds for new wars were soon cast aside.

Early in the discussions it was argued that nationalism, akin to individualism, cannot be wiped out; cannot be forcibly moulded into any pattern; cannot be dwarfed nor inflated. So nationalism, not only as an irrepressible mass desideratum, but as one of the underlying principles of individual selfrespect received a great impulse. The Philippines came into prominence as an example of progress and achievement possible by a people whose nationalistic aspirations had received encouragement. Morever, had not the stipulations of the Atlantic Charter supplied the motive power [S]

as well as the purpose behind the great efforts of the Allied Nations? Had not the Atlantic Charter above all other documents encouraged subject peoples to seek to establish their sovereign rights? 

Great promise, great joy and jubilation marked the opening of the Conference for World Peace; and yet within a short time strong men became discouraged. Some were alarmed at the widespread desire of small units for separate self-government as they appraised the chances for peace among so many. Others, more economically minded, viewed with distrust the newly proposed boundaries and the threatening impediment to the flow of commerce across new borders. Was it not advisable to encourage the formation of large nations and run the risk of their building great armies and navies in violation of treaties? Was not the maintenance of armaments a necessary means of giving employment? Need weapons always imply aggression? These were pertinent and unanswerable questions.

INTERNATIONAL COURT OF JUSTICE 

The lofty idealism of a proposed International Court of Justice buoyed up for a time the hopeful spirit of the Conference. 

No doubt such a Court would have great persuasive influence among nations. But could it function with speed in an emergency?

Would its members be appointed in a manner which would make them more than mere servants of political or financial groups within nations? Granting that its decisions would always be just, how would or could the Court enforce its mandates? As the discussions centered on the question of international armies and navies to supply the League or Court with power, the costs of such armaments had to be considered. The cry of "haves" and "have- [6]

nots" among nations arose again and no one quite knew to what extent the greatly heralded prospect of International Peace was threatened with complete failure.

Wisely the American representatives at the Peace Conference had kept themselves rather aloof from the deliberations. They were also deeply concerned over the trend of affairs. Yet they played the role of hosts and kept hope and faith alive that their guests would accomplish tangible results so dear to the practical 

American mind.

A GALA OCCASION 

As hopes faded, as patient men hung their heads and impatient ones wrangled, the time arrived for a great occasion. The head of the American Delegation was to address the Conference. 

Elaborate preparations were made and the large assembly hall of the Conference was filled to capacity with men from all corners of the earth. To many the display of grandeur and splendor presented an overwhelming contrast to the starvation and suffering left behind in their homelands. In due time colored films of the scene were carried to the remotest towns to give to countless millions the opportunity to view the spectacle with awe and inspiration.

As the American Statesman rose to address the audience, his confident manner allayed scepticism. In easy flowing terms he eulogized the conditions of the Atlantic Charter to which the Conference was committed. He gave a realistic account of the faltering attempts of men throughout the ages to rule themselves under their own national banners, free to choose any form of government compatible with their own ideals. He emphasized his conviction that the Conference would bring about an en- [7]

lightened world-order under which there would be found in each nation a form of government chosen by the people of such nation. 

CONTROL OF INTER-NATIONAL SEA AND AIRLANES 

Then he turned his thoughts to the sea and airlanes. He pointed out the abundant reference in the Atlantic Charter to the "enjoyment by all states to the trade and the raw materials of the world" which must be carried over these lanes. He dwelt on the guarantee in the Charter to "enable all men to traverse the high seas and oceans without hindrance," and gave it as his conviction that under a new world-order there would be found no choice of freedom on the part of any national to roam the international sea and airlanes at will, with armed merchantmen or planes, with an unlimited or a limited battle fleet. 

His listeners who had so far in their deliberations concerned themselves principally with their land boundary disputes, eagerly followed the Speaker's references to the sea as he continued: "I love the land which gave me the light of life and, if such a thing is possible, I have greater love still for the sea,-for the oceans which link this land of mine to the brotherhood of nations before which I stand. I therefore follow but a natural inclination when I turn your thoughts to the vast expanse of ocean and sky which separate our homes. And if we are here gathered to unite these homes, if we are here gathered to see to it that the ties welding us into one grand brotherhood shall never again be broken by the shameful aggression of war, then we have but one task. 

One simple task to which we can apply our hearts and minds with directness of purpose. We can control the traffic on these wide [8]

waters and under these wide skies. Then they shall never again separate but shall henceforth always unite us." 

He went on to say that it was his carefully weighed opinion that land boundaries would adjust themselves in the course of time, especially through the helpful mediation of an International Court of Justice. There might be serious disputes but they would remain localized. There might even be armed disputes on land, but they would never again spread like a cancer over the entire globe provided the sealanes, internationally held and controlled, remained undisturbed by conflict. 

In a statesman-like manner he explained the ever growing interdependence of nations. He described the damaging economic consequences to any nation finding its commerce barred from the sea lanes as a disciplinary measure to prevent or stop uncalled-for aggression by land towards a neighbor. Going into greater detail he unfolded his plan:

ONE SINGLE OCEAN TRANSPORT CORPORATION 

"I propose that each nation possessing ships and sea planes, whether built for commerce or for combat, turn this equipment over to one large corporation, and henceforth assign to this single enterprise the sole right to carry the nation's commerce on the inter-national water and air routes of the world. I propose that in exchange for such equipment this large international transport corporation issue its own paper currency and agree to accept this currency for the services it renders." This last phase of the proposal was most timely because already some of the alert thinkers in the Assembly had visualized a stock corporation issuing stock certificates in exchange for a fleet, the control of the enterprise passing [9]

into the hands of those contributing the largest share of equipment. That the speaker had in mind no such selfish purpose and contrariwise had due regard for the aspirations of all nationals was made clear by his subsequent remarks: 

INTERNATIONAL PERSONNEL 

"I propose that the great sea and air armada so constituted be manned from the lowest post to the highest by men of all nationalities, possessing besides the needed technical qualifications, the spirit to work together as true internationalists." The Speaker suggested that academies be established in different parts of the world to prepare young men of all nationalities by a system of uniform education and training to serve the great fleet. These youths were to be selected annually by their national governments to enter upon their studies. Adopting a method of allocation based on the total amounts paid during the preceeding year for services rendered by the Corporation on traffic to and from the ports of each nation, all nations, according to their economic importance, would acquire numerical representation on the Copporation's staff.

He gave it as his conviction that it would be possible to find men of many nationalities who would immediately apply themselves to the services of the Corporation in a spirit of internationalism such as the system of training outlined was bound to perpetuate. He sketched in brief outline the responsibilities of these men in the technical ranks of the fleet as well as in the administrative branches and placed great confidence in those who would direct the Corporation's activities in the many world ports. 

Confidence above all in their character so that, breaking away from national selfishness, they would give to "all states great and [10]

small, access on equal terms to the trade and raw materials of the world." (Point 4 of the Charter) Equal terms at least in the matter of transportation cost and services. 

DIRECTORS OF THE ENTERPRISE 

Finally, on the subject of policy and management he said: "Place the directorate of the Corporation in the hands of men chosen from within and by ballot of the high-ranking personnel of the Corporation itself and you will perpetuate an organization to which all nations may bow with equal respect. This I mean literally because this directorate having received its sanction from yourselves, from the representatives of all nations on earth, shall have authority and power transcending that of any one nation. 

In my mind's eye I can see this small body of eminent men sitting in judgement on matters now baffling presidents and prime ministers. I can see them speed to the rescue of a nation in distress and with equal rapidity destroy the cobra-head of any nation which might dare coil itself to strike at a peaceful neighbor."

A wave of encouragement was sweeping through the entire assembly. New hope dawned. Men of all races were drinking at a common fountain of inspiration. The birth of a true Internationalism was in progress and it was clear that the speaker was loathe to depart from the subject of men to that of equipment. 

IMPORTANT DETAILS 

Concerning the participation of all nations in the construction of ships and planes to serve the Corporation's future needs the speaker reported that he had received different suggestions, all equally impartial. He assured his listeners that an adequate manner of allocating future construction contracts could be found and did [11]

not take time to go into details. He likewise assured them that the currency issued by the Corporation to cover its current expenses and to purchase new equipment would have a stable value in all world markets, and continued: "Let me make it unmistakably clear that I have proposed the formation of an enterprise constituted for inter-national service, devoid of any stain of seeking national or individual enrichment. For whereas it is true that this Corporation would demand payment in its own currency for all services rendered, it is equally true that it would use such currency to acquire only two things, namely, 'men' and 'equipment';-men of the character I have already described, and equipment,― transportation equipment more modern than you and I have yet dreamed of; and fighting equipment,― equipment to punish piracy large and small, to lay siege to the ports of any aggressor. Never again shall the cry arise of who shall be greatest. There shall be but one 'greatest,' but one power at sea, but one banner, but one and only authority. Some of us shall have to sacrifice, shall have to bow before an authority which heretofore we have considered our own only at the cost of bloodshed, turmoil and strife. And in the light of our past sufferings shall we not do so gladly?"

TAX RELIEF 

"Need I say, need I stoop to the conventional subject of taxation, to bring you to realize that by adopting my proposal you shall have lifted from the shoulders of your countrymen and of mine the great burden of building and maintaining competitive naval armaments?"

The subsequent roar of applause was very unconventional indeed. The mentioning of taxation brought no small climax and [12]

even during the applause newspapermen left their seats to cable this portion of the address to their offices at home. It took some minutes before the Speaker could close his oratorical speech in the following words:

THE BLUE CROSS 

"Gentlemen of this great Conference for World Peace, if you are willing to follow the road to international order and harmony as I have presented it in brief outline, there rests upon you an obligation which you can promptly undertake and which you alone can fulfill. Grant a charter to this great inter-oceanic enterprise. Grant a charter that this flag being unfurled behind me may henceforth fly alone and supreme on all seas. Grant a charter embodying your highest ideals of international goodwill and peace, the ideals of the Atlantic Charter and you will make them a reality. So help us Almighty God."

During the closing sentences of the address there was unfurled slowly behind the rostum a large banner of white silk, centered by a cross in skyblue. The Speaker's words were taken up by the roll of a great organ "Mighty God, the First, the Last, What are ages in Thy sight 

But as yesterday when past, Or a watch within the night?"

Men in deep silence hung their heads, not in despair this time, but in reverence to a new-born hope.

The spell of gloom which had befallen the Peace Conference had been broken. The newspapers in every city of the world gave [13]

free play to their imagination. Everywhere the BLUE CROSS became the topic of conversation and before long a committee was at work named by the Peace Conference to prepare the charter and draw the by-laws of the Blue Cross Corporation. 

IMPERIALISM SUBSIDES 

The vision of promised security for the sea lanes and of impartial treatment in the matter of freight rates the world over, made the delegates to the Conference more amenable to an adjustment of their differences concerning land boundaries. 

Some groups who before had been most insistent on separate sovereignties saw some advantage in consolidating their interests in order to acquire a more worthwhile representation in the ranks and possibly in the management of the Blue Cross. Others who had opposed the disintegration of empires saw the wisdom of tempering their opposition when they realized that the Blue Cross Corporation would have more power than their national governments had ever possessed to make their foreign investments secure. 

To them economy and fair treatment in the matter of freight rates, without the need of dependence upon their own national shipping, loomed large in favorably appraising the plan. 

A curious change in ideology concerning imperialism took place. Security in the matter of inter-oceanic commerce would of course stimulate rather than retard the development of backward or undeveloped countries. More than ever would men leave their homelands to engage in enterprise abroad. But need men intent upon making productive the waste places of the earth dream only of returning home to enjoy the fruits of their labors?

Did they owe allegiance solely to the land of their birth, and none to the land which gave them their sustenance? Should they not [14]

rather apply themselves wholeheartedly to the land of their adoption?

Clearly the Blue Cross once established would exercise considerable influence in bringing about a fair distribution of gain between producer and consumer on two sides of an ocean. The prewar system of combined consumer and transportation interest had done much to raise the cry of "imperialist" by the producer of raw material. His country had remained backward and poor. 

He had borne the brunt of destructive competition in world markets among manufacturers consuming his raw material. 

Would not the Blue Cross temper competition by a better distribution of the world's goods and thereby guarantee the producing countries better treatment?

INTERNATIONAL BANK 

Some of the members of the group studying this particular phase had felt that an international bank was the logical means of bringing about better distribution of manufactured products thus lessening destructive world competition. But after the experience of the war, arguments which put the power of money above the power of equipment were of little avail. The Blue 

Cross would have the equipment and this made the Blue Cross plan paramount.

LIMITATION OF SOVEREIGNTY 

Discussions of the Blue Cross committee were made generally known among the delegates to the Conference, who were now making excellent progress towards solving their previous disagreements. The much talked-of and much sought-after formula for "limitation of sovereignty" had been found. Pertaining to the [15]

sealanes, it would be applied to all nations on an equitable basis and entail no hardships to any. The idea of an International Court of Justice to settle disputes between nations now received enthusiastic acclaim since cooperation between the Court and the directors of the Blue Cross Corporation was considered a foregone conclusion.

FREIGHT REBATES SHIPS AND PLANES 

Our orator statesman remained a dominant factor in making his Blue Cross plan a reality. Before various committees he answered dozens of questions and his recommendations remained invaluable. One of his suggestions pertained to the building of new ships and planes. What nations were to benefit and was there to be competitive bidding on contracts? The Committee felt that in order to be assured of economy in the purchase of equipment the Corporation should have the right to call for competitive bids among all nations. Certain successful bidders would receive construction awards and others would not. Consequently certain nations would benefit by ship and plane building. Other nations would not receive such benefits and yet the freights paid on their imports and exports were a part of the revenue of the Corporation which enabled it to buy new ships and planes. 

Our statesman solved this inconsistency by suggesting that the Corporation declare annually a freight rebate representing a percentage of its total income from services rendered. Individual shippers or consignees would not receive any rebate which would be paid instead to the various national governments on an equitable basis. This basis would take into account benefits to nations engaged in construction work for the Corporation, by increasing [16]

rebates to countries not participating in the manufacture of new equipment at the expense of the former. 

BLUE CROSS DOLLAR EXCHANGE 

Many questions were raised as to the future stability in the principal world markets of the Corporation's currency, the Blue Cross dollar. Only a surplus or a shortage of these dollars could upset its stable value. The Committee argued that bankers would take into account the tangible assets and solid organization behind the Corporation's currency. They would not mind keeping Blue 

Cross dollars in their vaults. One of the vital services to mankind, the service of inter-oceanic travel and transportation could be purchased only in Blue Cross dollars and their value in the world's market would be secured by their demand. The naming of freight rates giving the Corporation a powerful lever of control over the return of these dollars into its treasury would in itself tend to stabilize the Corporation's currency. 

Only when payments were made for large amounts of equipment might there be a surplus. To prevent this the Corporation was authorized to issue interest-bearing bonds. These bonds were redeemable at the option of the holder in either Blue Cross dollars or at fixed exchange rates in the currencies of several countries. 

They turned out to be excellent investments and were largely redeemed in Blue Cross dollars because their current rate was generally higher than the one prescribed in the bonds. A shortage of Blue Cross dollars was not at any time anticipated because it could be so easily remedied by buying new equipment, by calling bonds or by retarding amortizations and reducing freight rates correspondingly. [17]

OPPOSITION 

As the work in committee progressed it was not surprising that opposition developed. Three distinct groups,-owners of existing steamship and airtransport companies, underwriters of marine risks, and large producers of raw materials in remote parts of the world desiring to maintain their grip on distribution by using their own transport services, seemed more interested in hampering than in helping the Blue Cross plan. 

Experience had taught them that without the assurance of a lasting peace their enterprises were hardly worth rebuilding. This in itself seemed a conclusive argument in favor of the plan. Moreover they were assured by the American member of the Committee that unless the plan was accepted the United States Merchant Marine would never allow itself to be driven again from the seas by foreign competitors. The United States could not afford to have its economic life ripped out of gear again under the pressure of building a fleet during a war emergency. 

The prospects of unrestricted competition among steamship companies supported by their national governments and the added uncertain aspect of plane competition were factors which could not be disregarded. The opposition to the Blue Cross plan gradually diminished and ceased when assurances were given that no one would suffer confiscation and experienced employees would find better opportunities within the Blue Cross organization than they had ever dreamed of before. 

The public's clamor for tax relief was overwhelming. The

World Press was on the public's side and after a while nothing could interfere with the execution of the plan. [18]

OF SPECIAL INTEREST TO THE UNITED STATES 

In the United States an additional circumstance speeded the success of the Blue Cross plan. During the war the American transoceanic air fleet as well as the Navy had been greatly expanded. Thousands of young men who before the war had never thought of making flying or going to sea their career, now were eager to man the Blue Cross fleet. They saw the stability of such a career, the chances for promotion, the high standards the Blue Cross would adopt in order to become truly a body of internationalists. America had never had a fair share of the world's shipping business. Standards of pay had been too low to attract the American youth. All this would be changed. 

SUCCESS OF THE PEACE CONFERENCE 

Its work almost done, the Peace Conference drew to a close. 

Where national ambitions were not fully realized it was felt that the future would solve existing problems and show forth more enlightened ways. The main object, the guarantee that never again would the world be torn apart by a global war, had been achieved by the Blue Cross plan. 

When the Conference adjourned, the International Court of Justice as an impartial body to study and advise on matters of nationally inter-related economic questions and the Blue Cross Corporation to dominate the seven seas, had become living realities. 

A final resolution of the Conference provided that representatives of all nations should meet again five years hence to review accomplishments of both the Court of Justice and the Blue Cross Corporation and to make such amendments to the Blue Cross 

Charter as experience might dictate. [19]

A NEW WORLD 

The ease and economy with which commerce began to move over the earth astounded experienced shipping men now holding important posts within the Blue Cross organization. No longer were ships of competitive lines traveling in ballast to and from the same port in search of freight. 

The Corporation maintained a powerful battle fleet and yet freight rates translated into national currencies were around prewar levels after taking tax economies into account. No longer did these rates include high amortization charges and profits. Gone also were the days when underpaid seamen seeking solace for their hardships in alcohol made spectacles of themselves in foreign ports. 

STRATEGIC HARBORS 

The American Statesman who had done so much for the inauguration of the Blue Cross plan served for a number of years as one of the twentyfour members of the Corporation's Board of Directors. He had the opportunity to see much of his vision realized. At his suggestion, the Peace Conference had made an extremely valuable study of strategically located world ports. 

Now under his influence great new harbors became the centers of distribution for the airships of the fleet. Here arose new naval bases to serve ships and planes alike. 

Here also the Corporation built compounds for its men small towns within themselves - where they could bring up their families in surroundings befitting their international standing.

OFFICERS AND MEN 

The ranks of the personnel in its varied branches were very [20]

similar to those in the former American Navy. Promotions depended on strict examinations and administrative officers were required to possess a thorough understanding of economics. Men in charge of activities at the principal world ports reached such posts only by outstanding ability and experience. And from among them, by their own ballot, the Directors were chosen. No wonder that in the hands of these twentyfour men the peace of the seas rested secure. 

By the charter of the Blue Cross all its personnel acquired an international citizenship which made them subject primarily to the discipline of the Corporation itself. Only upon retirement or resignation did men relinquish this citizenship to become again subjects of the country of their origin. And concerning retirement, a splendid plan enabled its beneficiaries to select homes where Blue Cross dollars would give either the greatest happiness or the most comfort.

TRAINING FOR PEACE 

Our Statesman gave most of his time to the dozen or more academies in different parts of the world, to the young men of all races and color upon whom no small honor was conferred by their governments when they were sent to the Blue Cross schools. 

He knew that upon them the future of the Blue Cross structure rested and he never lost sight of their training. He never failed to remind them of their international citizenship, of the respect they owed one another irrespective of race and color. 

He loved their youthful enthusiasm, their sports, their aptitude. 

He was satisfied that they would guard the peace, the peace promised in the ATLANTIC CHARTER.
