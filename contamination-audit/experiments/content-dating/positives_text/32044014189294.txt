INTRODUCTION 

The reader who examines the gradual development of European continental philosophy from Descartes to Kant will find an abrupt and baffling turning off from the established groove of thought in the faith and teachings of G. W. Leibniz. In the back of 

Leibniz's doctrines respecting almost every conceivable subject, the student will readily note a profound and versatile mind which has assimilated the thoughts of the great historical thinkers, and which is able in its versatility to detect the trivialities and reconcile the dilemmas, in which the philosophy and science of his day had become lost.

Perhaps no other person in history, Aristotle alone excepted, has possessed such an encyclopedic mind coupled with so creative an imagination. For Leibniz wrote on any and every conceivable subject. It has been well said of him that he gave the chief impulse to modern intellectual commerce. He was freighted with the intellectual thought of the ages, but he had so made over his cargo that to whatever port he came he appeared as both a discoverer and in many ways as a law-giver. In mathematics he shares with Newton the discovery of the Differential Calculus; in his Protogea (1693) he laid the foundations for the science of Geology. In Metaphysics he gave to the world a new theory of 

Substance, and such new expressions as the Monad, the Law of Continuity, the Pre-Established Harmony, and the Best Pos sible World. 

He knew and made it his business to keep informed about everything. Some of his correspondence with Spinoza deals with new methods for making lenses; to Père Grimaldi, Jesuit missionary to China, he communicates his researches on Chinese Philosophy. 

He addresses Louis XIV now on plans for a military expedition to Egypt, and again on the best method for conserving and promoting scientific knowledge. With the Landgraf of Hesse-Rheinfels and with Bossuet he has suggestions for a Union of the Catholic and Protestant Churches; with Privy Counsellor von 

Spanheim on the Union of the Lutheran and the Reformed. With 

Père des Bosses he discourses on Transsubstantiation; with Sam-

Hanschius on Plato; with the Queen of Prussia on Free-Will and

He touched no subject from which he did not grow in wisdom, and which he did not enrich; he has partaken of all the celestial banquets of Philosophy. To understand his ideas we have to understand their roots in the history of philosophical thought. And while many students, aware and appreciative of his vast scholarship and versatility, have accredited his doctrines with foundations upon Greek, Neo-Platonic, and Cabalistic sources, hardly any one has undertaken to examine the correspondence in any kind of detail. 

And many, too, have found Leibniz so indebted to his predecessors that they have deprived him of all independence of thought, all originality, and have stamped him as a mere "eclectic." But such is not strictly the case, as we shall see; he both borrowed from and enriched his sources. In many ways he opposes traditional Platonism; in other ways he transforms it and makes it pertinent to particular problems. It is the purpose of this paper to delineate in what manner and to what extent Leibniz is indebted to the avowed sources. To understand those correspondences and dissensions we shall try to slide the glass back and forth along the vast tracts of thought which the philosopher made the foundations of his own comprehensive system. 

The reader will find in the development of this thesis what may seem an overabundance of repetition. The repetition will probably be found pardonable when it is pointed out that Leibniz's philosophy is almost rigidly systematic in structure, and every department of it is vitally interconnected with and always pre-supposes that which follows it. Hence in any critical or expository treatment of the subject it is necessary to carry along the threads and their several colors in order to render visible the pattern and purpose of the whole.

CHAPTER I THE BACKGROUND OF LEIBNIZ'S PHILOSOPHY 

The fact seems to be long-established in the history of philosophical thought that no great thinker, strive as he will to be original, can set himself outside the fold of philosophic succession. 

The problems of philosophy, and consequently of the philosopher, seem to have been laid down with the creation of the human mind which thinks them. And it is in this sense that the problems of philosophy are perennial. Strive to create as the thinker will, his efforts seem only to result in forcing the rays of eternally established truth to a new refraction through the lens of his own perceiving mind, and then he sees others' problems and truths in a new light, with new implications for him to develop and old errors for him to correct.

If this be granted, then the true philosopher is indeed, in a sense, the eclectic,-whether his eclecticism be consciously or unconsciously done he who takes the mind-stuff of the ages and molds it anew by the sheer craft of his own genius. It is needless to add that the true philosopher is never a victim of his eclecticism. 

That Wilhelm Gottfried Leibniz, the philosopher and systembuilder, is such an eclectic, seems well attested both by his thoughts and his mode of expressing them. Great was his creative power, and marvelous his ability to integrate the thoughts of different minds; but Leibniz's basic philosophic opinions, as he often insinuates, were not spun out of his own brain. As Nourisson well remarks:-"The philosophy of Leibniz has not issued from his own brain, like a Minerva, fully armed from the head of Zeus.

While she is a child of his genius, she is also a child of the time and of tradition." While Leibniz was the father of his systemin the sense that he gave to it form and order-the mother who endowed him with progeny innumerable and incomparable, the mother who presented him with her problems and gave generously 1

He is deliberate in gathering together the legacies of philosophical experience into a harmonious whole. He can combine, as few before or after him, a vast erudition with a creative and critical faculty.

He seems always to have been dominated by the idea that his eclectic role was to have the purpose of allowing him to raise a philosophical system of his own upon fragments of an imperfect and historically inadequate tradition. Leibniz's philosophy is "critical" in the sense that it is constantly altered as the facts come to fit the truth as the thinker sees it, rather than as he accepts it on authority. Conscious of the imperfection of the mechanistic philosophy of Descartes and of his own contemporaries, Leibniz turned to find the means to give it life and consistency. To rebuild this system, he turned first to the Scholastics, and thence to the Greeks themselves, in order, as he said, to recover the gold from the dross,— "On taking due account of these traces of truth of the ancients, or in our predecessors, one draws out the gold from the mire, the diamond from its mine, and the light from the surrounding darkness, these, in truth, are the perennial problems of Philosophy.' 996 To erect his new structure, Leibniz would not dovetail the new edifice onto the foundations of the old; there must be both modification and reconstruction. He aims always to be consistent, and he is not afraid to borrow the building-bricks for the edifice to Truth wherever they are to be found. For this reason the master craftsman is also the master eclectic, and Leibniz can say of his own system, that "This system appears to unite Plato and Democritus, Aristotle and Descartes, the Scholastics and the moderns, theology and ethics with reason. It seems to take the best from all sides, and then it goes farther than any has yet gone. I find in it an intelligible explanation of the union of soul and body, of which I had before this despaired. I find the true principles of things in the Unities of Substances, which this system introduces, and in them harmony pre-established by the primitive Substance. I find therein a wonderful simplicity and uniformity, so that it may be said that this Substance is everywhere and always the same thing, differing only in degrees of perfection. In building a new system of his own, Leibniz seems to have assumed the role of integrator and arbiter of the opinions of the ancients and of his own immediate predecessors, as well as his contemporaries. For this role, he seems to have been fitted both by the keenness of his mind and the breadth of his understanding, and also by an eclectic tendency almost innate. This deliberate eclecticism is a marked feature of Leibniz's philosophy. As we come to examine his doctrines in detail, we shall be able to judge for ourselves the truth of his assertion, written as late as 1712, to the effect that his purpose was to make use in his own system, of whatever was worthy of being preserved in the doctrines of his predecessors.¹¹ 

From these considerations, there is at least partial justification for the accusation that Leibniz borrowed his system from his forbears, and also for the verdict that his work is consciously saturated with Platonism and Aristotelianism. Before leaving this subject, however, it must be granted that the accusation of having borrowed a monadology from Bruno is one not lightly dismissed. Many ideas fundamental to both philosophers have an obvious correspondence:-the conception of the universe as a system of independent realities, each differing from the other and each mirroring the universe from its unique point of view; each unit comprising the All in itself, and each an essential constituent of the All. In Bruno and in Leibniz, finite things are in constant flow, evolving, unfolding, each unit or Monad experiencing as it grows, the life of everything else. In both all things are good, judged from the point of view of the whole; in both Freedom and necessity are one, and belong to God's own nature. CHAPTER II PRINCIPLES OF METAPHYSICS-THE UNITY OF GOD AND SPIRITUAL PLURALISM 1. ON THE NATURE OF GOD 

Leibniz's doctrines concerning the Deity, in so far as his ideas are clothed in the language of the schools, seem to be a composite of ancient and contemporary notions, of metaphysics blended with formal theology, ethics, and logic. It is peculiar to his treatment of the idea of God that sometimes he speaks of the Godhead in terms empirical and rather materialistic, with all the flavor of anthropomorphism and the diction of the conventional dogmatic theology; and at other times his conception is phrased in the best Platonic expressions, and God becomes the ineffable, absolute, and inexpressible to hen of the Greeks. At one time God is conceived of as First Cause and Omniscient Creator, who chooses as almost a perfected man might choose, the best possible universe from a number of possibilities present before Creation. Then reference is made to His antecedent and consquent will, and to his social connections with man.¹ At other times, the idea of God is decidedly akin to the Platonic, and that Being is spoken of as Creator, Emanator,' the Principle of all 

Things, the Unity of the Universe, the Perfect Absolute Reality,5 

the Infinite, the Incorporeal and true Actus Purus, and also, in a sense truly Platonic, as we shall see, the Immanent and the Transcendent. 3

It would seem, further, that the Leibnizian dogmata, perhaps drawn from the well-spring of the ancient philosophy and made to conform to the new pattern provided by Leibniz's understanding, are used to give new life and signification to the prevalent theologico-philosophical views.

Thus it is, that however much he employs the language and the thought of the Platonists, Leibniz has always sought to discredit the idea of the existence of a world-soul. Here is made evident to us one of his "anti-mystical" tendencies, and one of the bases of his original departure from the Platonism of the schools. For this ultimate union of the imperfect with the perfect, this "flight of the alone to the Alone" which is the consummation of all the soul's existence, is characteristic of both the Platonic and the Cabalistic meaning of the immanence of God in His creation. 

Leibniz's universe is original in conception and in many ways divorced from the traditional ideas of past systems. His universe is an everlasting flood of creation, a tidal wave without an ebb; it is a continual emanation of Ideas from God. But he shuns pantheism and runs from Spinoza. All things created of God bear a divine birthmark which shall ever distinguish them, and they shall never lose their identity.

3. THE CREATED UNIVERSE AND THE HIERARCHY OF CONSCIOUSNESS 

The Cartesians had made the mistake of denying rationality and soul to all orders of life below man, thus imposing a sharp cleavage in nature which justified their mechanism and eliminated the teleological idea from the objective universe. Leibniz orders the universe anew, and according to a group plan which, like many others of his ground plans, has its countertype and parallel development in Platonism. For Plato and his followers saw the whole universe as ensouled, as everywhere a living image of Deity; saw every animalcule in travail to accomplish the destiny imposed upon it by divine law. Nature, Leibniz recognized, must not be divided dichotomously into beings conscious and alive and unconscious and lifeless; the consistent basis of classification is that of an organic scale of life of which Deity is one end and embodied or conditioned life the other, and the entire interval is enchained with living forms. CHAPTER III THE PRINCIPLES OF INDIVIDUATION AND THE LAW OF CONTINUITY 1. INDIVIDUATION AND THE INDIVIDUALITY OF THE SOUL 

No less than the early Atomists and nature Philosophers, Leibniz, too, coped with the problem: How are the many one? How can the continuous consist of indivisible elements? By what criteria is an individual to be discerned from all others? Yet his formulation of the problem and its difficulties is more all-embracing, and the solution arrived at is more comprehensible and more conciliatory than any subscribed to by other eclectics. 

Stated simply, his task was to reconcile the notion of substance as continuous, with that of substance as consisting of indivisible elements; to re-interpret the age-old problem of the relation of whole to parts so that the continuity and completeness of the whole should not be in open conflict with the real diversity of the parts. 

The history of thought shows thinkers siding with one outlook or the other. Spinoza's cardinal tenet that omnis determinatio est negatio amounted to an assertion that the unity and the continuity of the whole was real at the expense of the reality of the parts. 

Substance, thus, could have no real parts. And opposed to this outlook was the theory of the atoms and the void which Leibniz said at one time "charmed his imagination." And here, if atoms constitute the ultimate reality, the ultimate unity of all things is destroyed and continuity becomes an illusion. Leibniz finds that the shortcoming common to the Cartesians, for example, Spinoza, and the Atomists lies in their confounding quantitative and qualitative differences. Accordingly, the force of his argument is to show that the essence of substances is non-quantitative; that the relation of whole to part must be conceived as primarily intensive, not extensive. The key to his solution is supplied in the Monadology, where the principle of individuality is made the basis of the metaphysical world in which substances have their being. 1

3. MATTER, Space, time, anD MOTION 

Leibniz's understanding of such concepts pertaining to the phenomenal realm as matter, space, time, and motion, and his employment of these in the basic structure of his own system, betrays close kinship and in some cases, even indebtedness, to the systems of Aristotle and the Platonists. He uses Aristotle's definitions, and often assigns to them connotations that might be in sympathy with Plotinus' outlook. CHAPTER IV THE NATURE AND LIFE OF THE MONADIC ENTITY 1. THE NATURE OF THE MONAD 

The new units of substance-la substance simple-which Leibniz sets up to replace the "atoms" of his predecessors, he denominated "Monads," a term which is as old as European philosophy and full of varied meanings and applications. But the "Monad," in spite of its heredity and historical affinities, is uniquely Leibnizian; much as it may suggest earlier conceptions, it is in some ways radically different from them. It is clearly distinguished from the atoms, for its author calls it a metaphysical atom, without parts.¹ Further, it is a simple substance, and justifies its title in that it is unum per se.2 Leibniz makes definite his ideas in his reply to Bayle: "I regard Monads as atoms of substance, since in my opinion there are no atoms of matter in nature, and the smallest portion of matter still has parts." 

He himself saw close connection between the role of the Monad in his own system and that of the Idea in Plato's. The realm where each has its being is the same; the part played by the Idea as a unit of thought in the Divine Mind has for him a suggestive parallel in the position of his own Monads as thoughts in the understanding of God. 3. THE HIERARCHICAL SYSTEM IN NATURE 

Leibniz is consistent enough in his Platonism to include in his system a hierarchical principle based, as we have partly noticed, upon the degree of consciousness in the dominant Monad of every organism. And the Leibnizian conception of a hierarchical system, as we find it also among the Platonists, is implicitly evolutionary. It is the Life-Force, the Primary Entelechy, which informs at one stage the mineral or "comatose" realm, at another the animal, and yet again the human kingdom, and manifests itself in succession in each of those levels, carrying with it the accumulated experience of its previous manifestations. "The soul," says our philosopher, "is a Primary Force which is modified and varied by its derivative forces or qualities, and developed in its actions."47 48 He outlines for us, though not so painstakingly as does Aristotle, whose ideas he finds it fitting to use,18 the successive stages of the appearance of consciousness in organic life. 5. THE SYSTEM OF THE PRE-ESTABLISHED HARMONY 

Leibniz's doctrines seemed to be revolutionary innovations to his contemporaries, and very probably because they studied him apart from the sources in which he had been steeped. Knowing little of his background, they seemed to regard him as a bold innovator who spun out of his own mind these radical notions which aimed at a reconciliation of the modern with the ancient philosophy. Yet Leibniz's doctrines are hard to account for only when divorced from their Platonic and Cabalistic antecedents. He is both dependent upon and independent of his sources. He is so deeply imbued with them that they are in the background of his mind when he sets up his hypotheses. We have had ample evidence that often he transforms the Platonic teachings, and at times even reverses them in order to endorse and erect a consistent system.

CHAPTER V THE INTUITIVE BASES OF Knowledge: the CASE AGAINST EMPIRICISM 1. THE CONSTITUTION OF THE INTELLECT 

If we consider his theory of Knowledge in terms of its refutation and reconciliation of the extreme views of his contemporaries, then Leibniz may be regarded as seeking, and perhaps as having found, a via media between the opposing doctrines of Descartes and Locke. The former's doctrine, though not rejecting totally sense data, did nevertheless give essential worth only to that which was clear and distinct, drawing a line of demarcation between self-conscious and all other kinds of thought. There is no relative truth in the Cartesian philosophy; the only reliable knowledge is clear and distinct knowledge, and only "the things which are clear and distinctly conceived are true."¹ Confused and even sub-conscious perceptions, Leibniz's petites perceptions, are for Descartes mere error and confusion. Ideas and perceptions in any way confused or obscure are for Descartes "of no avail in affording us knowledge of anything outside ourselves, serving rather to impede it." In Leibniz's understanding, though, all perceptions represent the object according to a relative scale of epistemological perfection. Confusedness is to be taken as a low degree of distinctness, and the more perfect is the representation of the object in our perception the more distinct is the knowledge of the object. The difference among Monads is established upon this very idea of the varying degrees with which they represent the universe as an object of knowledge to themselves. 

But let us not overlook the fact that in Leibniz's system, each Monad, however imperfectly or indistinctly, does mirror the universe to itself, and being impelled by a constitutional appetition

The idea characteristically Platonic and Stoic, which makes all life to be interconnected, and which sees the entire universe as an embodiment of a divine idea and regards the various components of the universe as approaching closer and closer to the unity and splendor of their source, will bear essential comparisons with Leibniz's foundations for his City of God. As we have noted, to the human Monad alone, as an accompaniment to its self-consciousness, has God given the knowledge of eternal truths. 5. OPTIMISM IN THE PHILOSOPHY OF LEIBNIZ

6. ON PROVIDENCE 

The notion of Providence, in Leibniz as well as in the Platonists, is intimately linked with the idea of an eternal Sufficient Reason which governs and disposes all things, ordering all with reference to a purposed end. To begin with, it is God, Leibniz makes it abundantly clear, who is the original of the soul's ideas; God who is the Sufficient Reason of all things. "It is in God that I find the original of the ideas and truths which are engraved in our souls. . . . God is the primary reason of things."47

CONCLUSION 

From the comparisons and analogical representations in our study, it is demonstrably evident that in the philosophy of Leibniz are to be found elements in some cases comparable to and in others identical with, those in the Platonists, Aristotle, and the Cabala. Hence the observation of some commentators that Leibniz's philosophy is an amalgam of the doctrines of his predecessors is in some cases true and in others fundamentally inaccurate and unjust. In some cases the influence is so noticeable, it is true, that students of Platonism and the Cabala, such as Adolphe 

Franck, Nourisson, G. Rodier, and Bouillet, consider the presence of these elements beyond dispute. But while their presence must be granted, it is another matter to aver that Leibniz's philosophy, because of the vast erudition of its builder, is but an aggregate or composite of historical theories and systems. 

Leibniz was a system-builder, and while every system-builder uses the building-bricks which are the common stock of experience, what is of essential importance in a philosophical Weltanschauung is not so much the character of the bricks as the nature of the edifice constructed with them. Many of our philosopher's doctrines can be traced through the history of philosophical succession, but his application of them is original, and even exclusive. The materials of his system may be old, but its architecture and its utility are adapted to his own times and their philosophical, scientific, and theological problems. Leibniz's "eclecticism," if following precedent we must term it such, is of an unconventional order,-is always conciliatory and far-seeing, fresh, critical, and provocative, and has for its aim always to be logically consistent. It is for these reasons that out of the systems of his predecessors he rejects just as often as he selects. His was a fertile intellect, filled with all manner of knowledge, but never the victim of its own erudition. 

To be acquainted with certain ideas is not necessarily to be dependent on them or to plagiarize them. The ideas and thought 120

Conclusion 

121 patterns of the ancient schools are constantly in Leibniz's mind because they are his background in philosophy and its problems. 

But in spite of evident correspondences and parallels, there are radical differences between his philosophy and that of his forbears, and these we have tried to point out. Too often critics have been led to deprive him of originality and to declare him a plagiarist because his terms are traceable through a long history. But we must notice that he uses the traditional phrases with new connotations. His philosophy is first of all Leibnizian, and then 

Platonic or Cabalistic. 

Thus the historical problems receive at the hands of Leibniz a richer and more profound significance. It has been our purpose in this study to set forth the essential correspondences between the Leibnizian and the ancient systems of philosophy by indicating similarities and pointing out differences, and always to maintain the view that far from being unoriginal and a mere "eclectic" as the term is ordinarily understood, Leibniz was the eclectic par excellence-one who took the mind-stuff of the ages and molded it anew by the craft of his erudite, original genius.
