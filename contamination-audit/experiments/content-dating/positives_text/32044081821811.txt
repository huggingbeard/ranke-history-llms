Foreword

Between the time that the popular slogan "Remember the Maine!" originated and the more recent cry of "Remember Pearl Harbor!" fortyfour years have elapsed. The latter half of this period was a time of disillusionment for many, especially for young people.

Again we are in the midst of a storm of world proportions. I have witnessed the devastation of a tropical hurricane, when it seemed as if everything was gone. Then, almost over night, as if by magic, the enduring strength of the soil asserted itself. The fertilizing power of sunshine and showers wrought restoration. The naked trees put forth leaves, the gardens produced and the flowers bloomed. 

Darkness and storm foster disillusionment.

They hide for the time those enduring qualities of human integrity out of which a better world can emerge. The following pages were written "before Pearl Harbor." I hope they reveal an integrity of the human spirit, which in the darkness seems too little but is never too late.

CHAPTER I A Family Man Looks at His World 

One hundred thirty-four years ago my greatgreat-grandfather, escaping from a slavery economy, left the Bush River community in South Carolina, crossed the Appalachian Mountains and settled in what was then the Western Reserve, but which we now know as the state of Ohio. A hundred years ago, the second generation later, my grandfather left the fertile hills of Ohio and trekked into the ague-infested flatlands of Indiana. A generation later father and mother, with my four older brothers, journeyed in a covered wagon to a new home farther west.

This is mentioned not because it was unusual; such wandering was the usual and typical thing in that period of pioneer life in America. The striking thing is that the modes of travel and transportation of my ancestors and yours in the earlier years of the past century were not radically different from those used by Abraham when he trekked westward out of Ur of the Chaldees four thousand years ago. 

Since the day my father made that last migration to the west, marking in a way the close of the free land era, the frontiers of life have marvelously expanded. Our world has changed. Its

hidden resources have been seized and utilized with incredible rapidity. It is a new world. 

Viewed through the eyes of our great-grandfathers, it would be a veritable world of magic. It seems to us as we look at it a real world. But is what we see real or does it cover up reality? 

Will the world that is to be, the world of the grandchildren of you young folks, a century hence, be such that were we to see it today it would seem to us a world of magic? Probably so. For us nothing is more important than learning to pioneer, to explore the reality of a changing world.

To help in solving the problems of living, to point the way, if possible, through the maze of this old but ever new world, I propose to tell in simple language what are some of the realities of the world in which I live as I see them.

Allusion was made to the migrations of my ancestors. As a further necessary background 

I want to tell you of my own migrations, of which some have been geographical but more what may be called adventures of the soul. 

On a Saturday morning, more years ago than I like to think, I first looked at my world. I do not remember that day, but I am sure that I was there and that I looked. It must have been very limited but very wonderful and mysterious, that jumble of brightness and shadow and color. My world still fascinates me. I find that the colors have now woven themselves into some sort of pattern;

I see more and I see farther; but the mysteries deepen and the quest quickens. 

I was born in Kansas. I came there the year the grasshoppers left. This was not cause and effect; it was supply and demand, as you will see. 

The disappearance of the grasshoppers was followed by years of plenty. Good wholesome food and outdoor life, with long rides and walks and work in the open fields, gave me a strong body for which I have always been thankful. In winter we had the district school. On the north the schoolhouse was protected by an osage hedge. 

On the south the prairie invited us to use its open spaces for town ball and prison base. To us boys the school existed for the games and sports which centered about it. Study and recitation were a necessary part of the rules of the game, to be observed in order that the time on the playground might be rightly appreciated and utilized. 

In the spring we had the glorious prairie fires. 

The howling of the coyotes always gave us a thrill. Each season had its attractions. 

On First Day and Fourth Day we were taken to meeting. At first it was in the old oblong meeting house situated some miles away, with its facing benches of the gallery.

A story which had its setting in this meeting house at Tonganoxie is told by a Mr. Cadwalader, whose name betrays his Quaker ancestry but whose feigned ignorance of the Quakers gives point to the story as he told it. "I have always had Quakers for neighbors," he said; "they have

been mighty good neighbors. But I have noticed one peculiar thing about them. Every Wednesday morning just before eleven o'clock, no matter what they were doing, they would leave it and go over to the meeting house and stay there for about an hour. I often wondered what they did there.

Now it occurred to me that there was no reason why I shouldn't go over some Wednesday morning and find out. They are my friends and neighbors. Why not go to Quaker meeting? So one

Wednesday I quit my work and went. The 

Quakers were standing around talking about the weather and crops and such things. Suddenly just at eleven o'clock Uncle Jesse Blair got spunky about something and went up on the facing bench and sat down and began to pout. Then all the others as if seized by a common impulse scattered out over the room and began to pout. They sat there and pouted for about an hour. Finally 

Uncle Jesse Blair got ashamed of himself and reached over and shook hands with the one nearest him. Then they shook hands all over the room and made up and began to talk about the weather and the crops and such things just like other folks."

Memory still keeps for me the image of the faces of men scattered about that meeting room.

I remember when Dr. Samuel Coffin stood up in Quarterly Meeting and expressed a concern which he and others felt for the building of a Quarterly Meeting school of secondary grade to be known as Friends Academy. The only high school then in the county was in the city of 

Leavenworth. The thrill for me rose from the thought that some day I would be big enough to attend that academy. That came about in due course of time, and I did my college preparatory work in this Tonganoxie Friends Academy.

Long before I attended the academy, a new meeting house, which was known as the Stanwood Church, was built in our community. My father took the initiative and did much of the work of building with his own hands. I have always had a feeling of deep appreciation for the rural church. Even today when I have occasion to drive through the country on Sunday, as I frequently do, I seek out a country church where 

I can join in the worship.

One difficulty which country churches have to meet is giving a faithful pastor adequate support -a sometimes intolerable burden, though one which they impose on themselves. Now for many years I have attended and found satisfaction in a nonpastoral meeting. If the country groups are

not satisfied with the unprogrammed meeting, they might at least work out for themselves an order of worship. If it should be difficult to get helpful vocal contributions to the meeting, then let them not be afraid of silence. In the distraught life of today, group silence comes as a healing balm. The expenditures for a pastor can helpfully be used in building a community program. The rural church, even more than the city church, has reason to be the center of neighborhood activities.

The four years I spent at Penn College were thoroughly enjoyed. The comradeship was stimulating and there was a chance to do things I delighted in doing. What with football, Y. M. C. A., oratorical contests, intercollegiate debates, extemporaneous discussion contests, a laundry agency to help pay expenses and, last but not least, courting the girl I afterwards married, I am now amazed that I found time for mere academic studies. Perhaps I did because the girl was an "A" student and I just had to make good with her.

During my freshman year I roomed at the home of Oliver E. Dixon, an "old grad" whose stories made a link with the past for me. One story he told was of the days of President Benjamin F.

Trueblood. At that time, the Hutchinsons were prominent in the community and in the college. 

The Hutchinson boys had as their recreation the raising and training of hounds and the hunting of coons. For some unknown or forgotten reason,

If we look for them we can find shortcomings in every sort of institution. College is no exception. 

When young people go to college they are taken out of their normal environment and placed in an artificial community. To be sure, training in community living is all to the good, and college has developed a sense of social responsibility in

many a young person. But its artificiality is a barrier to carrying that sense of responsibility to the problems of earning a living, establishing a home, accepting obligations as citizens and functioning in a religious setup. It is heartening that while educators nowadays are setting themselves to the serious task of fitting students for the world that is to be, they are also preparing them for the kind of life that they must live here and now while creating that brave new world.

The phenomena known as college pranks seem to be an attempt to break away from the artificiality by making it ludicrous. On the other hand, they may be an attempt to guard inviolate those community artificialities. As a student, I took part in a prank or two. For three years I roomed just across the street from the main building of the old Penn College. In this building, called 

South Hall, we organized what was known as the "Fire Department." Its purpose was to watch for couples who, especially in the springtime and on moonlight evenings, would come out from the town and sit on the college steps. This was an infringement of our rights and we had a remedy for it. As soon as the alarm was sounded, proclaiming that a couple was on the steps, the fire squad would detour behind the building and enter by a rear door, sometimes by the coal chute. We would seize a bucket, fill it at the basement faucet, and carry it up four flights of stairs to the front window, just above where the mooning pair was sitting. An outstanding characteristic of young people is loyalty. At least they want to be loyal if we give them a chance. Loyalty to "Old Siwash" doesn't need cultivating. It is at its best when it springs up redolent of the soil that has been fertilized by some unselfish service, done only for the joy of doing it. I remember a group of students who spent many a precious evening in the home of an aged couple, Mr. and Mrs. Presler, on North C Street, singing old gospel songs for no other reason than that the old folks enjoyed it so much and were lonely. 

While the loyalty of young folks is marvelous, I have sometimes felt that no one can be quite so cruel if there is a feeling that one of their number needs to be "taken down." I suppose every college generation of thirty or forty years ago had someone who was taken on a "snipe hunt" in some form or other. Well do I recall when the ebullient Willie Wickersham was taken miles from the college and then persuaded to stay alone under a railroad bridge in the dead of night and watch for the snipes and catch them while the others presumably scattered out fan-

One group who failed to mix pity with their plans was the "Roast Chicken Club," a secret order improvised for the benefit of a certain freshman who continually and loudly boasted of his prowess. One thing that happened in my college days moved me profoundly. During my sophomore year the physical suffering of the people of Cuba in their struggle for independence was impressed upon my mind. For three years a resolute band of Cuban citizens had carried on guerrilla warfare against their Spanish overlords, enduring untold hardships. A good many Americans of an adventurous type had gone to the island to join the insurrection. The case seemed hopeless. 

Patriots like José Marti and General Antonio

Maceo performed prodigies of daring and valor, but were cut down in the midst of their successes. 

Then General Weyler was sent out from Spain to create order. If General Weyler were alive today he would be called a Fascist. He believed in total war. He believed that the way to subjugate the Cubans was to cut off their resources. 

He burned their homes and cane fields, destroyed

their crops and herded them into concentration camps, where they had insufficient food. Later on, when I went to Cuba, I saw children whose distended stomachs showed that they had been fed too much of the wrong kind of food, doubtless to stay their hunger, and their spindling, rickety legs were pitiful evidence of undernourishment. 

I remember that day in February 1898 when as a representative of Penn College I attended a student convention in Cleveland, Ohio. On the morning of February 16, I set out for the convention hall. Newsboys were crying extras on the streets. In large headlines, unusual in that day, were the words, "Battleship Maine Blown 

Up in Havana Harbor. War With Spain Is Imminent." The slogan "Remember the Maine!" swept the country from coast to coast. Day by day the propaganda for war mounted. Congress authorized President McKinley to issue a call for 50,000 volunteers. War was declared in April and a call was made for 300,000 men to help free Cuba, an army of what was then considered immense size. 

I was twenty-three years of age and without dependents. Two seasons on the first team in football showed that I had physical endurance. 

But I did not volunteer. I could not volunteer and keep faith with my conscience. I wanted to help free Cuba; I wanted to do something about it; yet anything I could do seemed futile.

However, the war was on. Within a few months the campaign was over. We thought, as a nation, that we had freed Cuba, only to find that we had loosened her hands but bound her feet. Cuba mortgaged her fertile soil to an absentee landlord.

In February 1900, the American Friends Board of Missions decided to open work in Cuba and appointed May Mather and myself to service in that new and promising field. May and I were classmates. We were married shortly after graduation and later the same year we proceeded to 

Cuba in company with Emma Phillips, an experienced missionary to Mexico, who was transferred to the new field together with two Mexican young people. A considerable part of the support for my wife and me for the first year was supplied by our fellow students at Penn College. At last the opportunity had come to do something about the situation in Cuba that had caused such distress of mind. In all we spent about twentyseven years in Cuba.

In the following pages are narrated some of the peak experiences of more than a quarter-

century of living as aliens among a kindly people -living a life that was fuller of opportunities than I deserved or could adequately match.

CHAPTER II Annals of Cuba 1. Witnessing the Birth of a Nation 

The independence of Cuba was established during the early part of my seventeen years' residence in Gibara, a seaport in northeastern Cuba.

In those flourishing times this town was a thriving mart. Wholesale houses, busy as beehives, lined the water front. The streets were thronged with country folk, in town to purchase supplies. 

Around the warehouses and market places beasts of burden, great in number and variety, stepped about with quiet restlessness while their masters bargained with the merchants.

Down the road came trailing lines of horses and mules bearing upon their patient backs the produce of Cuban soil. Glancing at them one would say that they were trained to walk in single file, but a closer view showed that the animals did it under compulsion, for the halter rope of the hind one was tied to the braided tail of the next ahead. Thus eight or ten beasts would be obliged to follow in train the leader upon whose back, in addition to the burden of fruits, vegetables, sugar or grain, mounted the muleteer who guided his animals to the proper warehouses, where he

was credited with the correct number of mule loads of produce.

Gibara and its hinterland had access to the outside world only by the sea. In order that the retail merchants and planters might be supplied with goods, ocean-going vessels called at the port. 

Regular liners made calls from Havana, New 

York and Barcelona. Tramp steamers brought jerked beef from South America, codfish from 

Norway and rice from China. Often schooners with three and four masts, full rigged, would come sailing majestically with the wind and tide and cast anchor in the broad harbor.

Now this has all changed. Gone is the busy mart; the ancient warehouses stand vacant; the docks are falling in decay. The industrial revolution passed the town by. Over-night, almost, I saw the change take place. Four of the greatest sugar plantations in the world were established in that region, each with its own harbor facilities. 

The building of railroads set up new trade routes for the Gibara hinterland and diverted travel and commerce accordingly.

Gibara was one of the few walled towns of the Western world. All that now remains of its once impregnable defenses is two blockhouses and a dozen rods of the old wall maintained as a memorial. Only the natural beauty of its situation and the tropical loveliness of the surrounding country remain, a fitting framework for glories that exist only in memory. It is said that on a November morning in 1492 Columbus sailed into the Gibara

Another hour of glory for Gibara was that May morning in 1902 when President-elect Don Tomas

Estrada Palma disembarked there to set foot on Cuban soil after long years of exile abroad. 

Amid the joyous acclaim of a liberty-loving people, he proceeded by circuitous journeyings to Havana to take up the burdens that would fall upon the first president of the republic newly joined to the sisterhood of nations. It is a memorable event to see a nation born. One of the most signal honors I have had in my lifetime was the distinction of being one of the committee that received the president-elect upon his return to assume the high office. 

Don Tomas Estrada Palma was a man of simple habits, lofty ideals and deep devotion to his homeland and to his fellow men. He gave his all to his country and asked nothing in return. 

In these days when men demand as a right that a living be given them by their government and too few are willing to serve and sacrifice for their fellow men, the story of President Estrada Palma is worth recording. In 1868 he was a youthful but prosperous plantation owner near Bayamo, in the central part of eastern Cuba. When Carlos 

Manuel de Cespedes sounded the clarion call for independence from Spain on October 10, 1868,

Estrada Palma joined other plantation owners who freed their slaves and took up the cry for

national freedom, giving the last measure of their resources to the cause. During the tragic ten years which followed, Estrada Palma became president of the Junta, the revolutionary government. As such he was captured by the Spanish forces and taken in chains across miles of wilderness to the nearest seaport, Gibara, where he was embarked for Spain, a prisoner of war. When the 

Pact of Zanjon ended the hopeless struggle, the erstwhile revolutionary president was freed. 

He did not return to Cuba. At the time Cuban independence was established a current rumor was, I remember, that Tomas Estrada Palma had sworn an oath not to set foot on Cuban soil until its freedom had been achieved. However that may have been, he did not return to Cuba, but found his way to Central Valley, New York, where for twenty-five years he followed the career of teaching. Being without a country of his own, he became an American citizen. He was known to his neighbors as Mr. Estrada.

Although keenly interested in events relating to his native land, he did not figure prominently in the New York Junta, formed to seek support for the insurrection of '95. His home was a rendezvous for Cuban patriots, who sought his counsel not only during the war but also during the four subsequent years of American intervention.

The struggle for independence had produced some outstanding leaders. José Marti, a man of brilliant intellect, great courage and unquestioned devotion, more than any other one man had both inspired and planned this last appeal to arms on behalf of freedom. Abandoning his role of advocate of the Cuban cause before the American people, he gathered about him a band of patriots and sailed with them on a filibustering expedition to the Cuban shores. Soon after landing his forces, he contacted the enemy at Dos Rios and with more valor than wisdom he charged them on horseback, thus exposing himself to their rifle fire, which cut him down. The Apostle of Cuban 

Freedom was gone.

General Antonio Maceo, a Negro, was another valiant leader, the ablest strategist produced by the conflict. He led machete charges which struck terror to the hearts of the Spanish soldiers. He led his band of insurrectos from eastern Cuba in the incredible feat of passing the supposedly impregnable line of blockhouses known as La Trocha, and spread consternation by appearing in the very suburbs of Havana, only to be trapped in western Cuba and slain together with his aidede-camp, the youthful son of General Maximo Gomez.

General Calixto Garcia was a stern but magnetic officer. His men idolized him. He achieved a marvelous mobility for his troops. The enemy

would think they had him trapped, but he would disappear as if by magic, to reappear with his troops in some unexpected and perhaps distant quarter. When the Spanish-American War broke out his whereabouts was unknown. He it was to whom President McKinley addressed the "Message to Garcia," made famous in the Roycroft pamphlet by Elbert Hubbard. It was characteristic of General Garcia that instead of co-operating with the American army in the capture of Santiago, he crossed the island and freed the town of Gibara. One of the town's principal streets, up which he led his troops to take possession of the Municipal Building, is now called Calixto Garcia Street. This doughty general, with the scar of a pistol shot in his forehead, survived the war only to succumb to pneumonia in New York.

With the passing of General Garcia but two possible candidates for president of Cuba remained. One was General Maximo Gomez. A native of Santo Domingo, he fought both in the 

Ten Years' War and in the insurrection of '95. He was the nearest approach to a field marshal of any general who served in the Cuban forces. He was so well-beloved that the Cuban Constitutional Convention inserted a clause in the constitution which made it possible for him to serve as president of the republic in spite of the fact that he was not a native son. The other possible candidate was Don Tomas Estrada Palma, the schoolteacher of Central Valley, New York.

before the election, General Gomez made a visit to his old friend at Central Valley. What happened at that visit intrigues one's imagination. 

One morning the two men appeared together at the office of the clerk of the court of this quiet village. The clerk looked up from his desk. It was General Gomez who spoke in somewhat broken English. "Good morning, sir," he said. "I come to be with this old friend of mine, Don Tomas Estrada

Mr. Estrada. But he let it go at that in his eagerness to hear what this strange foreigner had to say. "I come," continued General Gomez, "to witness the renunciation by my friend of his American citizenship and to make it a matter of public record, in order that the citizens of his native country, in the free exercise of the suffrage, can elect him president of the new Republic of Cuba."

The clerk was dumfounded. Only the quiet poise of his friend and neighbor, whom he knew as Mr. Estrada, made it possible for him to produce a document satisfactory to the stern old warrior.

It was a dramatic hour, with two great souls as principals and only a bewildered clerk as audience. When the recording angel reveals the roll of those choice spirits who refuse honors and emoluments because the happiness of their fellow men requires it, the name of General Max-

imo Gomez will be high on the list. He renounced a presidency which he might have claimed. The wise old soldier knew that a schoolteacher and not a warrior should be the first president of the nation that was being born, for progress lies along the path of peace and culture and away from strife and hatred.

At the elections held soon thereafter, Tomas 

Estrada Palma was the almost unanimous choice for president. It was with a feeling of reluctance that he turned his back upon Central Valley. 

For long years he had cherished its people as friends and neighbors and they in turn honored and revered him. But the call of his homeland could not go unheeded. When the time came for his departure, a fast steamer of the United Fruit Company was placed at his disposal. Three days later he arrived at Gibara, the town from which, thirty years before, he had been taken to exile in chains.

2. At the Gates of Life 

The three supreme events in a human life are birth, marriage and death. The first and third are inexorable; we have all been born and we all will die. The second seems to be a matter of choice, but it comes from an inner urge as compelling as others of nature's ways.

Organized religion has exploited these events, sometimes helpfully, too often shamefully. Since professional practitioners-merchants, florists, morticians, to say nothing of tax-gatherers stand at these gates of life to take toll, it would seem that the church, instead of being a fellow conspirator, should by wise counselings and reforms in its practices direct its efforts to the end that there should be a reduction in the high cost of being born, of getting married and of dying.

Sometimes I have thought I might do something about it. A good many years ago I made an attempt. My wife and I had just been married on a salary of forty dollars a month and the high cost of anything would have been a casus belli in our fight to balance the budget.

At that time in Cuba 28 per cent of the children were born out of wedlock. True, economics could not be blamed for all of it. As between divorce and extra-marital relations, Cuba, under the tutelage of organized religion, had traditionally chosen the second of the two evils. However, the

3. Caught in an Insurrection

The Cuban revolution of February 1917 under the leadership of General José Miguel Gomez was probably the costliest, most destructive uprising in Cuba since its struggle for independence in 1895-98. Someone remarked that the situation preceding the abortive rebellion made one feel as if he was living on a volcano. 

It was at this time of unrest that, under the aegis of the Mission Boards of the United States, a conference of Protestants was called to meet in Havana. As a continuing organization, a Committee of Conference was established under the presidency of the Rt. Rev. Hiram Richard Hulse,

Episcopal Bishop of Havana. I was asked by the committee to take the position of executive secretary, giving full time to the work of co-ordinating the religious activities of the entire island, at least so far as those not related to the Catholic Church were concerned. I never succeeded in establishing any satisfying degree of fraternal cooperation with the Catholic priesthood. During my seventeen years of residence in Gibara only one of the parochial priests was in any way friendly. This was a young man lately come from 

Spain. We exchanged experiences, much to our mutual benefit.

It was during the curacy of this priest, in April 1915, that the disaster of the sinking of the Titanic

occurred. A citizen of Gibara was one of the sixteen hundred who perished. Being a practicing 

Catholic, his widow arranged for a mass to be celebrated one month after the date of the disaster. Whether the story was apocryphal or not I do not know, but it was reported at the time that as the Titanic went down the band played "Nearer, My God, to Thee." Being aware of this report, the priest asked me for a Spanish translation of the hymn and a copy of the music, which I was glad to furnish. This memorial mass was the occasion of great interest in the town, owing in part, at least, to the use of this music in the service. 

It proved to be the last mass to be celebrated by the young priest in the village church. His ecclesiastical superior called him to account for having used a Protestant hymn in a Catholic church. Just what happened to him I do not know, but he never returned to his parish. 

A comparable lack of understanding on the part of the hierarchy was evidenced in Havana at the time of the death of Theodore Roosevelt. 

Cubans remembered that Roosevelt had led the

Rough Riders who fought in Cuba, and that later, as president of the United States, he had withdrawn the American army from the island so that the independent republic might be established. 

Hence they gave him public acclaim as a great hero, and arranged a memorial service in his honor at the National Theater in Havana. The priest who served Americans of the Catholic faith in the city was invited to participate in the

service. He could not accept, because his bishop forbade him to have anything to do with the memorial of a man who had made such sharp criticism of the church as had Theodore Roosevelt. 

To return to the events of February 1917: My acceptance of the secretaryship of the Committee on Conference had to await the approval of the American Friends Board of Missions and, even more important, that of my family.

But to return to my experiences: When I awoke next morning it was to find myself on the vast estate of one of the largest sugar mills of the island, the Manati Sugar Company. War prices for sugar were bringing prosperity to Cuba. Everyone, poor and rich alike, was "dead against"

the rebellion. It was sending up in smoke the riches of the country, they declared. The progress of the republic was being set back a decade. The 

United States should send troops and put a stop to the whole thing. Bread was being taken from the mouths of their children and their sons were being conscripted into the army. So they argued, sometimes calmly, more often vehemently, but always with a background of violence that spawned bitterness and hate. Dragon's teeth were sown in that rebellion which still corrupt the soil of Cuba.

Although I was in the town of Manati, I was still twenty miles from the sea, which was the only way out for me. Inquiry at the office of the company brought the information that there was a tramp steamer unloading which would be ready to steam for the next port in a few hours. The train for the port left at eight o'clock with me on board. At twelve o'clock I was at the captain's mess table spinning yarns while we rode the high seas toward Puerto Padre, one of our mission stations within forty miles of home. 

The captain was from my own home state, Kansas. That gave us something to talk about. I told the story of the old truck gardener of Holguin, who delivered his vegetables from door to door, using a ramshackle wagon drawn by a single horse which was only a pile of bones with a horse's skin charitably thrown over it. I had never met this vegetable vender, but he had been described to me and I had been told that his name

was Mr. Montgomery and that he was from Kansas. One day I happened to be in Holguin and saw, coming down the street, what was undoubtedly Mr. Montgomery's outfit. I approached him cordially. "Good morning, Mr. Montgomery." His greeting while respectful was none too cordial.

Being from Kansas myself, I thought to make conversation by referring to a common interest. "They tell me you are from Kansas," I said. "Yes," was the unexpectedly quick reply, "and I've been from it for forty years. May the Lord help me to get further from it. Kansas hasn't produced nothing but John P. St. Johns, Mary Leases, Carrie

Nations and Sockless Simpsons. When I left it forty years ago I was so mad that as soon as I got across the state line I stopped and threw mud back at it. My blood boiled so that when I picked up the mud it baked on my hands and they ain't been clean since."

The captain had his story to tell also. It was how the corn grows in the Kaw Valley. When he was a boy his father sent him one day to get some green corn for dinner. The corn stalks were so large that he had to take the ax to chop them down. But this was useless. The stalk grew so fast that before he could chop twice in the same place it had shot up again. He then tried to shin up the stalk, but before he knew it a morning glory vine grew around him and bound him fast to the corn stalk. His situation seemed hopeless, when along came a swarm of grasshoppers and ate every green thing including the corn stalk

and let him gently to the ground. He didn't mind, he said. Green corn wasn't good without butter and their cows had gone dry already. 

So we whiled away the time. No sooner had the freighter dropped anchor in Puerto Padre

Bay than a white motorboat bedecked with shining bronze came alongside the steamer, and two military officers demanded to be taken on board even before the customs officers had cleared the ship's papers. The men were the commander and lieutenant from the United States destroyer that had come to protect the sugar plantations in the vicinity. They were interested in finding out what was happening on the island and what were my impressions of the situation. Having learned what they could, they returned to their warship.

At the first opportunity I went on shore, only to find that there was neither a train to Gibara nor any way of communicating with my family that day. I stayed at the Mission Home that night and the next morning at six o'clock took the train for home. The train moved so slowly that 

I wanted to get out and push. My family had sprung surprises on me on various occasions. This was once that I surprised them by walking in unexpected and unannounced. For the moment I had reached my journey's end. The war went on its tragic way, burning, killing and destroying.

4. A Missionary Visit Twenty Years After 

Most folks are missionaries of some sort. For the moment, however, it is quite the style to be allergic to the word "missionary." Hence, if this narrative of a missionary service is to mean anything—indeed, if it is to have a hearing at all—I must set limitations and fix definitions which like highway markers tell which way we are taking and to what point it leads.

Missionary work, in its religious connotationand it is with that we are now concerned-by its very nature and at its highest must assume the divine spark in every man. Missionary work is a normal outreach for men with religious motivation. Such men possess a message so precious and want to share a service so important that they are impelled to cross group boundaries and national frontiers to place their lives beside those who will be richer if these things are shared and poorer if they are withheld.

Missionaries are human, very human. They have the virtues and failings of other folks. Sometimes enforced isolation distorts the judgment, but just as often, I think, it gives a perspective that adds clarity to the vision. During many years of residence in Cuba, I was intimately associated, not only with missionaries of my own society, but with those of all denominations, from the Bishop of Havana to the humblest day school

teacher in a remote village. 

I never knew one who merited contempt. One and all, they were not only ready to give but were actually giving the last full measure of devotion to the ideal of which they had laid hold. 

After my many years of missionary service, followed by more than ten years of business association in Chicago, the American Friends Board of Missions opened the way for my wife and me to make a religious visit to Cuba, where we had spent so many happy years and where with others we had had the opportunity of inaugurating the work which had developed into a full-fledged, though not a full-grown, Yearly Meeting of Friends, carrying on its work through the medium of the 

Spanish language.

In pursuance of this concern we were absent from Chicago nineteen days, seven hours and five minutes. Of this time we spent one night, going and returning, in Miami, Florida, one night in 

Camaguey, Cuba, one night in Santa Clara, and four days and nights in Havana, leaving a little over eleven days in the field of the Friends in Oriente province. Five of these days were spent at the Young Friends Conference held at Gibara.

A short time was spent at each of the other centers. With three exceptions my wife accompanied me on all these visits. In all I attended thirty-one meetings for worship, Sunday schools, conferences and other group gatherings. Not being interested in putting on an endurance test, I suc-

ceeded in reducing the number of my personal calls to thirty-three. My wife made many more than that. I commend her to marathon promoters.

I would be deluding myself and would serve no good end if I pretended that it is possible to make an objective study of and report on the field and work of Friends in Oriente, Cuba, in eleven crowded days when almost every public meeting was the occasion for a demonstration of affection. Even the impromptu gatherings at crossroads Friends centers were turned into spontaneous ovations. Human nature can't stand such things. They take your mind off your work. A big lump comes up in your throat, and you want to hide where nobody can see you wipe your eyes. 

The explanation of the ovations is not hard to come at. It is twenty-one years since we left 

Oriente province. Meanwhile a whole new generation has been born and has grown to manhood and womanhood. Twenty-one years is a long time and even the older generation has forgotten our bungling mistakes. Such generous praise affects me. I admit it. I am sure that my wise companion saw inflation coming on and as a precaution she bought two alligator leather belts to use in case the swelling up should proceed to the bursting point. How useful they were may be inferred from the fact that she did not put them on the customs manifest at Miami. They were thought of as used wearing apparel. On the ride home on the airlines, deflation took place to such

an extent that the alligator belts were given to our sons.

These limitations being confessed, it may still be that an account of my impressions, which were vivid and colorful, will help you to understand conditions better. 

Forty years ago in October my wife and I left 

Iowa for the long journey to Cuba. Exactly nineteen days later we landed at our destination in 

Gibara. Today a member of Western Yearly 

Meeting could attend his meeting on Sunday morning, take a plane that evening at Indianapolis or Chicago, and land the next morning at the very edge of the field of Friends in Cuba. If the weather were good he could spend a day at each of our four leading centers and by paying automobile hire he could see many of the rural centers, and yet be back home in time to tell the story to his meeting on the following Sunday.

More than forty years ago, measured from the publication of this book, when I attended the first Sunday meeting held by Friends in Holguin, it was necessary for me to take the train on Friday morning from my home in Gibara, and the first train I could get for the return trip was on the following Tuesday. Now on those same tracks five gasoline-driven cars are running each way every day, besides I don't know how many buses on a parallel highway.

The central highway has brought about a profound change in the life of the Cuban peoplethe greatest change in forty years. This concrete

road stretches over the entire length of the island from Pinar del Rio to Santiago and beyond. It carries a tremendous traffic. I think I have not seen anywhere in the United States an equal length of road with as heavy a traffic of buses and trucks. Private cars are not so numerous. 

Thirty-five years ago we thought it a great achievement when the then new central railway made it possible to travel in comfort to Havana and return for $50, the price of one way on the steamship. The other day I purchased a one-way bus ticket from Holguin to Havana for $3.50, with the privilege of a stopover anywhere we wanted. 

A round trip would have been proportionately less. The furious speed of the buses accords with the spirit of the time. Before boarding the bus we were told to commend our souls to God and trust to luck. Lady Luck has many devotees. 

The leading cities along the highway are building suburban additions which rival those of Havana in beauty and comfort. Lovely homes and gardens are appearing here and there in rural areas along the highway route. The general aspect of the working people's homes in the same area shows decided improvement. However, in some other parts we saw evidence of deterioration in the living conditions of the submerged group.

Let me now submit a bill of particulars as regards our own Friends people, their field and work.

HOLGUIN 

The property at Holguin-the school building, the church and two mission homes-seems to be kept in quite good repair. Ramon Morrell, director of the school, in personal conversation with me urged the building of a second floor over the grade rooms. This would increase the classroom space, permitting a larger number of students. 

The new story would also on occasion serve as an auditorium. He estimates the cost of such an economically constructed improvement as $6,000. 

He envisions an increase in the income of the school if it is made.

The Sunday school was well organized. I attended the men's class, made up of about ten substantial citizens. It was taught by the pastor although there were at least four in the class who were as well equipped to teach as he is-this without reflection on his ability. The uniform lessons were used. At a Sunday school held in a chapel some distance from the center of town, the same pastor taught the children, using some special lessons very skillfully. The evening meeting was the conventional church service, the innovation being that they patiently bore with a sermon by me.

One evening we attended a gathering of the married folks' club. About fifteen couples were present-an interesting group. In nothing can 

Friends exercise so helpful an influence as in the home.

Beginning at ten o'clock in the morning we had a two-hour session with the high school teachers. 

On my part it was mostly giving a sympathetic ear to their concerns. It was encouraging that they had concerns. The teachers had not yet been paid their June salaries. The Holguin Institute, supported by public funds, has affected attendance at our school. The group was optimistic. All the teachers, at least all who were present, had signed contracts for next year, some at considerable financial sacrifice. 

Holguin has always been an important city of Oriente province. I find it now an important city of Cuba. Henry D. Cox and wife have given many years of service in this city. Friends have an open door.

GIBARA 

Gibara is in the economic doldrums. It has good farming country around it and in recent years has acquired considerable popularity as a summer resort. It needs industries to give wage earners a living. The mission buildings-church, school and home-are kept well painted and in repair. But some improvements and repairs are urgently needed in connection with the home and to make proper sanitary provisions for the annual

Young People's Conference. The pastor, Juan 

Sierra, is a very capable and consecrated young man. He has completed the first year in the engineering course at the university. It was not possible to see the local work under normal conditions, as the Young People's Convention held the stage all the time we were there. Some very devoted people are members here. 

Government license has been secured for the opening of a day school. The plan is for the pastor to direct the school, with capable young people from the church as assistants. The low economic level of the community makes it difficult to finance the school. The problem, however, is partly met by the fact that the pastor is to direct the school. Since his salary is given by the church, supplemented somewhat by the Board of Missions, he can direct the school without being a substantial charge upon its budget. 

Our arrival at Gibara was a real home-coming to us, and, it appeared, to the church and town also. Greetings and embraces awaited us from everyone. One man, a stranger to me, boasted that he still had the rocking chairs we sold when we left Gibara. Hardly had we stepped out of the taxi when a messenger from the son of our old washerwoman appeared with greetings. His mother had died two years ago, at the ripe age of 103. For many years she had done our family wash. (Tell me, is soapsuds conducive to longevity?) I went to my old barber to get a haircutnot that I needed it. The old man had long since

gone to his rest. His sons whom I knew were carrying on, and one of them trimmed my hair. 

I asked what the charge was. "I don't think I ought to charge you anything," was the reply. I insisted that he charge me the fair price. "Well,

Mr. Jones, if you put it that way," he said in all seriousness, "since you are bald-headed I will charge you only fifteen cents." At once I resolved to get enough haircuts to last a year. They charge me seventy-five cents in Chicago.

A touching incident was our visit to the graveyard where our little girl had been laid to rest thirty-four years before. I had thought that I did not want to go there; it seemed futile. But finally 

I did go, and found a token of the love of the present generation which made that little grave sacred ground and gave it significance. The concrete posts and railing around the grave were in perfect condition, and a luxuriant croton of variegated colors made the spot radiant with cheer. 

Through all these years the sexton had cared scrupulously for the grave, not because he was paid for it, but because it was the resting place of one of the family of the missionary who first came to Gibara.

While the holding of the Young Friends Conference at Gibara prevented our viewing the work there under normal conditions, it did offer the opportunity of sharing with the young Friends of Cuba the concern of one who had spent his youth and manhood in companionship with those who had preceded them in Friendly service. The fol-

lowing expresses the gist of my concern for them:

"To the Young Friends of Cuba: 

"This is a word of goodwill and encouragement. I will try to speak to you from the standpoint of a generation already past the meridian of life. Whatever others may think about it, to us you are the most important people in the world. "The torch of truth which we try to pass on to you may sometimes seem to you only the ashes of dying embers encased in earthen urns. We do not wish it to be so. If the light grows dim in us, we confidently expect it to burst into a living flame in you. It is the enthusiasm, the faith, the eagerness, the hope, the adventuring of youth that is yours. It gives us confidence to face the future. Jesus was scarcely more than a youth when he made the great adventure of world redemption. "We humbly acknowledge the failure of our own generation in much. It has failed to give you a warless world. It has failed to abolish poverty and economic oppression. We bow our heads in shame as we think of the thousands of worse than homeless children in a none too friendly world, and they with less than half a chance because their parents, men and women of our generation, held their own selfish satisfaction in higher esteem than the plighted love of marriage and home. There has been failure to provide justice and equality of opportunity to those of all races and conditions. The slavery of appetite, the greed of gold has made monsters of men before our eyes

and we have been too careless and too hopeless about it. "The material achievements of our generation clamor insistently for first place in your recognition. They roar at you from the skies. They honk at you from the highways. They blare at you over the wave-lengths of the air. They smite your eyes alike at roadhouse and at skyscraper.

All the while the achievements in the things of the spirit are being smothered in an avalanche of cynicism. "We hope that you will rightly value the heritage of the generation that has preceded you. If they sometimes appear to you to have failed in much, we hope you will charitably remember the limitations under which they labored. Even 

Jesus met with apparent failure. However far short our generation has fallen in accomplishment, we have bequeathed the spirit of martyrs, of men and women who have risked and suffered much for their convictions. Perhaps our apparent failures will prove through the greater effectiveness of your generation to have been the foundations upon which you will rear the superstructure of a world more in harmony with the mind of Christ. "Therefore let our failures be your tasks and our shortcomings your opportunities. Starting with the heritage you have received, we want you to go on and search out the path of human redemption through Jesus Christ and, finding it, to dedicate your lives to helping men be Christlike.

"We covet the comradeship of service with you at our side. In that comradeship we would not limit you to the ways we have wrought and thought. We would that in you there might be the mind that was in Christ Jesus, that he might lead you into all truth. "Do not fear to face frankly the problems of life. Be loyal, but let your loyalty be to Jesus

Christ, to the will of God, to humankind. Let your loyalty be of the heart and mind rather than of forms and words. "We would with you open our hearts to the inflow of the Spirit. We would be baptized together in love. We would join you in your search for truth. We wish that our fellowship together may be of such a nature as will encourage you to give expression with freedom to such concerns as may be laid upon your hearts. 'Quench not the Spirit.' "Do not fear to walk in new paths of service.

Do not forbear doing things that are new and different. Always seek earnestly and prayerfully to meet human needs as Jesus sought to meet those needs, not as self-serving but in humbleness of mind."

PUERTO PADRE 

The buildings at Puerto Padre are in good condition and seem to be entirely adequate for the present. Mrs. Martinez has been so long at this place that one observes the impress of her per-

sonality. Although the name "Colegio Wilmington" is displayed prominently above the school entrance and although the official name of the church is the Spanish equivalent of Friends Church, nevertheless the entire plant is known throughout the area as Mrs. Martinez' School.

The impress of her personality was further evidenced in the groups with which we met. The reception on Saturday night was all that such a reception should be. The attendance was large enough and not too large for the seating and other space facilities. The introductions were just right. There was just enough program to keep things interesting and lively. The refreshments were abundant and satisfying. The folks, after effusive though brief valedictories, left at the proper hour. The Sunday school the next morning, within the limits of time and resources available, was all that a Sunday school should be. 

Especially pleasing was the young woman who taught the adult class. She has her bachelor's degree and withal has a keen insight into the problems about which people should be thinking. 

The evening meeting, which was a preaching meeting, was not up to the standard of the other gatherings. It would be easy for me to lay the blame for this on the sermon, which was my responsibility, but the sermon does not explain all. 

There was an evident absence of the guidance perfected through the years in the technique of such meetings, but which was there, albeit unobtrusively, in the reception and in the Sunday

school. To put it negatively, Mrs. Martinez' long suit is not a preaching meeting.

BANES 

Economically the Friends of Banes are by far the strongest of the four Friends centers, yet they have the poorest physical equipment. The appearance of the buildings is probably as good as that of thirty-five year old, economically constructed frame buildings can be. These buildings consist of church, home and school, and another building, leased from the United Fruit Company at the nominal rental of one dollar per year, which helps to meet the needs of the expanding school. Members at Banes made it plain that they want new buildings.

As was the case everywhere, the Banes Friends gave us an enthusiastic welcome. Manuel Manesses, clerk of the Monthly Meeting, had sent us a telegram at Gibara urging a visit to Banes. Dr. 

Tamayo and his wife met us at Dumois. This entailed a journey at dawn of twenty or twenty-five miles. We were lodged in the spacious and comfortable American Club. The Tamayos and we had lunch with Mr. and Mrs. E. S. Walker. He is general manager of the Banes department of the United Fruit Company. 

The big meeting was to be on Tuesday night.

It so happened that before, during and immediately after the meeting hour an eight months' drought was broken by a three and one-half inch

rainfall. The rejoicing was tempered by disappointment as to the crowd. However, families to the number of about 125 souls gathered in spite of rain. Miguel Ayuso, a delegate to the World 

Conference of Friends, was from Banes. A most pleasing surprise awaited me. At the hour for meeting to begin, Juan Guzman, the pastor, quietly took his place at one side of the raised platform. The meeting at once settled into silence. 

For some minutes no sound was heard except the patter of raindrops on the zinc roof and occasionally the muffled cry of a babe in its mother's arms. It was truly impressive. There was a covering from the unseen world, the flowing of the Spirit as from vessel to vessel. Both of us spoke.

We visited the so-called Co-operative Bakery run by the Monthly Meeting. The profits are devoted to mission work in rural communities. So far it has been successful financially and has given an opportunity for service in a wholesome way to a number of people.

RURAL WORK 

I ex- We visited briefly nine rural centers. pand the meaning of the word "rural" to include such substantial towns as Velasco and Chaparra. 

Of the nine centers, eight have buildings. Some are quite new and, with the exception of those at 

Bocas, all are in good repair. They are collecting a fund in Bocas to make needed repairs.

Friends at Gibara hired an auto from two o'clock in the afternoon until eight o'clock at night to take me and the committee over almost impassable roads to visit the rural work there. 

Banes did the same for both of us. In all but one place groups gathered to welcome us and exchange words of sympathy and love and spend a time in worship together. It was all very heartening. Five of these rural chapels have been built by the communities themselves without outside help. In most cases they did the labor with their own hands.

Fusil, twelve miles out from Banes, is selected as representative of what is being done in this rural work. The building, just completed, is to serve for both meeting and school. The studding is of rough-hewn native hardwood, the siding of shiplap and the roof of French tile. The flooring is of hardwood. At the back are a lean-to, a sanitary service and a concrete tank for water. The approach at the front is a concrete walk and steps. 

The material cost about $400, all of which was raised in the community except $40 donated by the Banes Monthly Meeting. Not a dollar of paid labor went into the building. In all, twenty-five different men donated their labor for a longer or shorter time. It was a labor of love. The neighbors take a commendable pride in this simple building which means a better community in which to establish their families and rear their children.

The inauguration took place the last night be-

fore we left for Havana on our return. The meeting opened with silence. Most of the men were necessarily standing and the women and the children were uncomfortably crowded in the seats. 

The silence seemed to move among us like a living thing, like a breathing from heaven. At the close of my sermon I used an illustration from my experience in Spain; I showed an aluminum cup I had brought from there and told the story of its use in feeding the refugee children. No sooner had I sat down than a leading member and prominent man in the community came down the aisle and deposited some coins in the cup to help feed the children in Spain. Others followed his example. The pastor mildly suggested that they defer this offering until the close of the meeting, but no, they wanted to do something about it and do it now. Their attitude blended beautifully with the spirit of worship. I know of no surer mark of true religion than the desire to do something helpful for somebody. 

One matter of deep concern is the economic life of the people. The standard of living must be raised if they are to support in any adequate way institutional organizations for religious, educational and social development. Forty-five years ago the rural Cuban squatted on the piece of land which produced most of what he needed for food and shelter with perhaps something extra which he could exchange for rice and cloth. The coming of the sugar and other industries on a large scale has completely changed his world. He now finds

himself a part of a great industrial enterprise from which he receives his wages and which furnishes him a house. It has placed him in the stream of modern industrial progress. But he has no part in directing this industrial giant; he has no voice in its management. Yet to it he must look for education, recreation and bread. He has, willy-nilly, exchanged a simple life, unlettered but virtuous, for vassalage to a foreign colossus. His future is not his own. It is determined for him in a directors' room in New York.

A QUAKER IN AN ANARCHIST MEETING 

Barcelona, January 3, 1937.—I went to church

today. I usually go, but I don't expect any credit for it this time in the Good Book because I didn't intend to go. In fact, I didn't think I was attending a religious service until I came away and started thinking about it. Now I am sure I attended one.

Let's have the whole story. Yesterday in conversation with the Spanish consul general in Geneva, Switzerland, Sr. Rivas y Cherif, he suggested that I should by all means see Sra. Federica Montseny, minister of public welfare in the Valencia government under President Azaña. The consul told me that she was an ardent anarchist, but an able administrator and a loyal member of the

Popular Front government. 

On arrival this morning I missed seeing Alfred

Jacob and he left word for me to come to a certain pension for dinner at 1:30 P. M. Since it is Sunday all offices are closed and contacts are impossible to get. I set out on foot to see the city. Two or three things impressed me. One of the first things I saw was a communist parade. It was two blocks long, men marching two deep, singing the "Internationale," or whatever it is that communists sing, with clenched fist held above the head. The marchers seemed to get a lot of satisfaction out of their performance. But after three years (since Christmas 1933) of such parading they could scarcely get a second look from the crowds that thronged the wide paseo. This was partly due, no doubt, to the competition of other political groups. In almost every city block along

the boulevards there are flashing signs announcing the headquarters of some political faith, such as Republican, Marxist Communist, Communist of Moscow, Anarchist, Socialist, Collectivist, Lib ertarian, Co-ops, and so on ad infinitum. Always the headquarters are ablaze with posters. All public and many private buildings are plastered over with them. They make the posters of the most grimly fought political campaigns in Chicago look like announcements of a Sunday-school picnic.

In my walk this morning I passed the Cine 

Coliseum, where Federica Montseny was speaking on the subject, "The Doctrine of Anarchism Faces Reality in Spain." Not only was the theater crowded to the doors, but the crowds overflowed into the wide boulevard, leaving only passage for one-way traffic on the far side, loudspeakers bringing her voice to the overflow. The meeting was announced to begin at eleven o'clock. By a quarter of one I had managed to get in far enough so I could see Sra. Montseny. She is in middle life, plain as any Quaker preacher. Her simple logic, coupled with a mastery of the Castilian tongue, seemed almost magical in its effect. She said that the great objective is human freedom and with it must go human responsibility. Enlightenment of all the people must pave the way. 

She believes in working with the United Front temporarily because it is the only way Fascism can be defeated, but she holds that power must be taken away from government rather than added

to it.

While communism in Russia had done many beneficial things, yet it will inevitably lead to tyranny. She paid her respects to Roosevelt by saying that he was going in the wrong direction. Whatever may be his benevolent intentions, his policy will lead only to the enslavement of the people.

She closed with a call to the youth of Spain to consecrate themselves in sacrifice and service. It reminded me of John R. Mott and Robert E. Speer in the heyday of the Student Volunteer Movement. As she closed they sang whatever it is that anarchists sing. If it had been their technique,

I could easily imagine the crowd hitting the sawdust trail. A few raised the clenched fist. As the throng quietly dispersed it seemed to be in the spirit of an inward high resolve rather than an external show.

A QUAKER HOBNOBS WITH FASCISTS 

Burgos, January 22.-Today I entered Spain on the Nationalist side. The chauffeur, another passenger and myself were able to satisfy both the French and the Spanish authorities and were on our way, in a Chrysler car, something after ten o'clock. On our way is right; how we did go! The streets through the small towns are notoriously crooked and crowded with people and animals. 

In the first town we passed after Irun, I looked at the speedometer. It registered 85 kilometers, and the driver quietly smoked a cigarette and con-

versed with the man seated next to him. A normal Spaniard cannot converse without gesticulating with at least one hand! How he got through the town was a work of legerdemain that I couldn't understand. Once, later, on a straightaway road, I looked at the speedometer and it registered 135 kilometers. I began trying to think of some good deed that I had done in my lifetime.

This kept my mind occupied and I looked at the speedometer no more. We covered the more than 

300 kilometers to Burgos in time for their dinner hour there, 2 P. M.

At five o'clock the Count de Vallellano, president of the Spanish Red Cross, was supposed to be in his office. I called at that hour. I had a letter for him. After I had waited an hour, one of the men came to me and said that the count would not be in; he was distributing presents at the hospitals in honor of King Alfonso's birthday, which is tomorrow. Disappointed at not having the opportunity to meet a count, I consented to meet the secretary of the National Assembly of the Red Cross. While I was not given his name 

I consoled myself by thinking that anyway he will know more about conditions as they are than the count. He greeted me cordially from behind a desk piled high with work. When I told him who 

I was and why I had come he was enthusiastic. 

I hastened to caution him that we were not a wealthy people and that donations, if any, might be quite small. He replied, as others have, that the important thing is that a group of people in

22 

far-off North America have thought enough of Spain to send someone to inquire with a view to lending a hand.

In reply to my question as to the needs he said that food they had in sufficient amount for the present. The thirty thousand war orphans were being cared for mostly by the religious orders. 

He did not know much of their needs. In the 

Red Cross work there was desperate need of medicines and hospital equipment. 

He was much interested in knowing how the refugees were being cared for on "the other side." 

I was glad to be able to tell him. He was very sympathetic and was one of the number, all too few, who spoke kindly of the "others."

As I rose to go I asked him for his name so that I would know with whom I had been talking. 

Imagine my feelings when he handed me a card which said, "The Count de Torrellano, Marquis de Buniel, General Secretary of the Supreme As sembly of the Spanish Red Cross." You could have bowled me over with a feather. I was glad, though, that I had known him just for what he is, a real man. He walked arm in arm with me to the outer entrance, where he asked the chauffeur to take me to my hotel.

While I was waiting for the above interview I talked with a mother and her little daughter. She told me that her other child, a boy, was in a Loyalist refugee colony near Santander. She herself was living quietly in her home in what was not now in the war zone. She had come to the Red

Cross to see if she could get her boy home, where she was prepared to care for him. While we talked an official of the Red Cross came to her with the information that it was found impossible to get her boy through the lines, so the family would have to continue separated. Not to quote the words, but to give the meaning-this: A dividing line of hate has been drawn between Santander and Burgos across which not even a child can pass in safety. But whether the child does cross the line or whether it remains in the colony, it will be reared in an atmosphere of hatred of its fellow men that will transform that home or that colony from a lovely garden into a desert. 

A young lady in the office of the Red Cross was introduced to me but without her knowing whom I was representing. I asked her the usual question as to what was the greatest need in Spain. 

Instantly she replied, "Someone to come to us and show us a better way, a way that will not mean killing our brothers in cold blood. This civil war is an atrocity." 

What has caused this terrible mess of human suffering which no Spaniard wants but from which he can see no way out except that which leads through a crescendo of horrors? It takes a book to tell this story which I must tell in two 

1. Through the long years of hopeless struggle by the underlings in Spain the wealthy landowners, the aristocracy, the privileged classes have resolutely refused to share their opportunity

with the underprivileged and now the explosion has come. 

2. The poor of the Spanish nation have had no stake in the country's wealth; hence they look to violent revolution as the way to make life livable, and they are finding it the way of death. 

A QUAKER MEDITATES 

On Sunday morning, January 3, 1937, I dropped out of the sky into Barcelona just as the sun rose out of the Mediterranean Sea. On Saturday afternoon, three weeks later, in a Chrysler car, I rolled through the blackened wrecks of homes and business blocks in the Basque city of Irun and crossed the bridge into France just as the sun was setting in the Bay of Biscay. It was a strenuous three weeks with a thrill every minute.

It seemed impossible to realize the nervous tension to which I had been subjected. Two days in bed and time out for a Rugby football game at 

Hendaye, France, seemed to make me as good as new. 

Sometimes I wonder if it isn't all a dream. It doesn't seem real, this rendezvous with death.

Fighting men expect death, but here it is women and innocent children who die. On three different occasions while I was with the Loyalists, neighboring towns on their side were shelled by the rebels and homes with families were completely destroyed. I also saw the Loyalists pulling down the walls of convents and churches and carting

the debris away while the walls against which the priests and nuns had been lined up and executed still stood. 

It seems incredible. It is as if they had reached a ghastly impasse, with death as the only way out. It is a kind of insanity. All wars lead to a mental unbalance and worst of all is this civil war in which the passions are further inflamed by class hatred. Spain is stretched upon a cross.

My rendezvous was also with life-life that was left to struggle on after war had done its grim work. In the rebel territory are many thousands-how many I do not know-of war orphans.

On the Loyalist side hundreds of thousands have fled the war-menaced towns and cities and trekked eastward toward the Mediterranean Sea into the already crowded cities of Barcelona, Castellon, Valencia and Alicante. 

Shall we send food to these undernourished orphans and refugee children and mothers who stretch out their hands in hunger to us? Shall we mediate a love that will reach those who suffer in the dark shadows of this modern crucifixion?

CHAPTER IV Too Old for the Draft: A Diary When the Selective Service and Training Act of 1940 was passed by Congress, it was a matter of deep concern for me how I could best bear testimony to the faith that was in me that impelled me, not to acts of war, but to the practice of good will toward all. I found this same to be a matter of deep concern for many young men of good will. Of course I was too old for the draft to include me. Nevertheless, I felt constrained to lend a hand somehow to those who were caught in the net. Besides we were all involved in this horrible mess and just to sit idly by seemed unworthy.

The opportunity to do something about it came unexpectedly through the Civilian Public Service. The pages that follow give a day-to-day account of this development as I was related to it during the spring, summer and autumn of 1941.

May 23.-A whipped dog came into my office this afternoon. There now, I shouldn't have said that! It wasn't a dog at all. It was a man who reminded me of a whipped dog. It was a warm day and the door next the street was wide open.

Mildred happened to be out at the moment and as he edged in at the door I was at my desk talking on the phone. He hesitated, looked furtively about. I nodded to him and he actually slunk into a corner. His first words showed me that the

scourging he was receiving was coming from within himself. "You know Walker L. I am his pastor." "Yes, I know Walker. He is a conscientious objector." He pulled out a sodden handkerchief and mopped the top of his forehead (which reached to the back of his neck). "Walker said you wanted to see me." "Yes," I replied; "he is a member of your church, I believe." "He is, but I don't think he should have asked for conscientious objector status. It embarrasses our folks." "He asked you to invite me to talk to your people some time and help them to understand his position," I said.

The minister moved uneasily, shifting his weight on the chair from one side to the other. 

The lashings of his inner self were as plainly visible as if leather thongs had been laid upon his bare back. "I-I couldn't do that. You see, some of our folks wouldn't like it. In better times I used to preach and teach the way of peace. Walker always did take things seriously. I didn't think it would come to this. Our church is poor, we can't help pay his way in camp." "Your teaching has brought Walker to choose the way of non-violence. He wants to go to the 

Friends Civilian Public Service camps, instead of the military service. Did you know that Walker does not have to go to a Civilian Public Service camp if you refuse to support him? He told me himself that he would go to prison rather than go into the military system. A young man willing

to stake all for conscience' sake is the most precious possession your church could have. You don't need me to talk to your people. They are seeing what religion can do for a man in the person of Walker. What he does is more effective than a thousand sermons. Why be afraid of some who may not like it when you have brave men like Walker on whom you can depend? He is making salvation visible. His is a clear light in a foggy, muddled world. He can even teach you a lesson of valor."

The minister was visibly moved. He grasped my hand, then turned and walked out, a conqueror. The inner voice had won its plea.

David Miller Stoffregen, a member of Cincinnati Meeting, came to my home after office hours.

He had driven from Cincinnati to consult personally about Civilian Public Service and his relation to it as a conscientious objector. He is a graduate of Earlham, class of '38. As to his finances, he said on Form 101, "My only asset is my wage from week to week." He appeared to have scruples, or perhaps I should say a natural hesitancy, about accepting help from others with his finances. I tried to point out to him two considerations: First, we are all in this thing, and while some, as in his case, will contribute a year's work in camp, the only opportunity others of us will have to make a contribution is by helping maintain someone else in a Civilian Public Service camp. Second, unless I am mistaken, the Selective Service Draft will be with us for a long time. 

After the current year's draftee has completed his service and is out in the economic world making a

living, there will be other young men who will need financial help and he will undoubtedly then have the opportunity of contributing to their maintenance just as others are now contributing to his. His mind seemed easier.

June 2.-The last of our preliminary contingent for the Quaker Hill camp arrived this afternoon.

I took the latest arrival to Quaker Hill in a downpour of rain. The men were all gathered in the shop. The new tools were in the process of being marked. Those with iron or steel surface were given quick, sharp blows of a hammer and punch, which left dotted markings like this: C PS 

(Civilian Public Service). The tools with wooden handles or frames had the same initials seared upon their surface after the manner of burnt wood work.

The first camper to arrive came a week ago— 

Howard W. Alexander, an old friend from last summer's Interracial Work Camp in Chicago, of which he was a member. He has his Ph.D. degree from Princeton and has just finished a year of teaching at Fenn College, Cleveland, Ohio. Howard is a Canadian by birth. As such he would not have been subject to the draft, so this experience is not a matter of compulsion with him. He carefully thought the matter through and decided that he could best make his testimony effective by being a member of a Civilian Public Service camp as a conscientious objector. As a conscientious believer in the non-violent way of life he had not the remotest chance of becoming an American citizen. But the filing of a "Statement of Intentions" did put him on what proved to be the road to the Civilian Public Service camp at Quaker

Hill. He had saved up about $600 and he counted the $420 needed for a year's camp fee as the best investment he could make. His experience in the 

Student Work Camp convinced him of the value of the Work and Worship method of creating a better world. As a "creative pioneer" he staked his all. No, he was not compelled to give $420 camp fee plus a year of hard work. He gave it freely as the choice of his own free spirit. The only compulsion came from within. He liked to work with his hands. He craved the companionship of fellow artisans.

Norman Walter Zumbrunen was the second "creative pioneer" to come for volunteer work be-

fore the formal opening. Zumbrunen ("Toward the Fountain") was not reared in the atmosphere of pacifism. He learned it the hard way. Quiet and unself-assertive, he is not the type you would think of as battling the current alone, yet he is one of twelve conscientious objectors to the way of violence in his entire denomination. A "creative pioneer" with few associates in the vast, unexplored fields of a great religious group, he has not lost his way. He moves with a certainty of direction and a clarity of vision that, combined with the sweat of honest toil, have in them the essentials of a new world. 

Robert G. Starbuck came to the camp under his own power. In the course of earning his own living he uses his hands as an electrician. Bob made himself a trailer "just for the experience," but experience needs to be hitched up to a dynamic just as a trailer is hitched to an auto. 

Working with the hands may be drudgery or it may be a sacrament. Bob is going to see new meaning in the things he does.

From Muncie this morning came an auto bringing Bob Cooper and his father and mother. They arrived two hours before they were due. The old folks were as excited about the adventure as was the son. One thing about this camp business is that it affects so many people. So many are dreaming about it. Bob Cooper is an aviation fan. 

He has his license as a civilian pilot. What a pull military aviation must have had for Bob! It took something more than wishful thinking to go

against that pull. It took a sensitive conscience. 

The opportunity to do hard work side by side with others helps a lot. 

Eugene Fisher and Marlowe Rector are factory hands. The former is the son of a minister who also works in a factory on week-days and on Sunday preaches to a small church group. Gene and 

Marlowe are already skilled in the use of their hands. They have the discipline of work. They have that priceless possession, the spirit of youth. 

Can those things be amalgamated? I think they can. Civilian Public Service has its most delicate and at the same time its most important work right there.

Allen Osborn makes his living as a grocery clerk. His religious life has been carefully nurtured in his home. An idealist, but an idealist who is not satisfied with words as the expression of ideals. He is one of those who find the makings of a better world in work and worship, in service and meditation, in the hands and in the heart. 

The last camp member to arrive was the Muslim. I confess I had a curiosity to see what he would look like. He phoned me from the bus station this afternoon. I was not expecting him until Thursday. Moreover, although he is a Mohammedan, he has the very Quaker name of 

Whitson-Holman Whitson. As I entered the station my eyes fell on a man apparently of draft age whose swarthy features seemed fitting for one of the Muslim faith. No, he said, his name was not Whitson. A voice behind me spoke up. I

turned and was face to face with a coal-black but kindly faced Negro. This was my Muslim religious objector. I had thought of Mohammedanism as a warlike religion, but the roots of war are in racial discrimination and Muslims put us to shame in the recognition of racial equality. Holman 

Whitson will have something to show us in building a better world.

Dear Friend:

From the address of Tom Jones at Yearly Meeting we learn the following: 

1. The administration of the Civilian Public Service camps by Friends and the other peace churches is still on trial. 

2. If Friends are unable to meet their share of the expenses of maintaining the conscientious objectors in camp the government will take over control. 

3. The value of the camps as training centers would be lost. Many of our young men who cannot conscientiously submit to a service without religious ideals would choose prison rather than a camp where coercion prevailed. 

4. In order to maintain the camps now in charge of

Friends, $30,000 for the next four months, over and above support now assured for men in camp, will be needed. We must make good with the government. 

Iowa Yearly Meeting approved the attempt to raise a fund amounting to $1 per active resident member over and above the support necessary for boys in the Civilian Public Service camps. Let there be no diversion of funds from United Budget for which responsibility has been accepted. 

5. An emergency need for funds is due to the following: (a) Increased prices for food. (b) Unexpected costs in preparing CCC and other camps for occupancy. (c) 

The lag in support from churches other than the historic peace churches. We are assured that the larger religious denominations will come forward with support if given four months in which to get organized. (d) The camps are not now functioning on maximum efficiency because they are not operating at capacity and consequently cost more per man per month than they will a few months hence when men are sent to us and there is capacity operation. 

6. Immediate transmittance of funds through regular channels is urged.

Our young men are giving their all. Will you share with them the sacrificial giving?

CHAPTER V The Unseen World About Me 

Thus far I have taken you with me in a world of action, my world. This world of action is not a world of might. Force is incidental and subordinate. Violence is altogether unlovely. In retrospect, it is borne in upon me that the intangibles, the unseen, the things of the spirit are the realities of eternal and ever-growing value. If they are absent from me, I am a clod. Whatever of energy or perchance of life there is in a clod is unreleased. It is imprisoned while I am free. As a clod, I may be measured, analyzed, catalogued, my past history read and my future closely predicted. As a person possessing spiritual attributes, may be labeled but the label is outgrown; I may be classified but I do not stay put; I may be studied, observed, dwelt upon, and the mystery only deepens while reality persists.

Just now there is a struggle going on that should not be. Reality is essentially spiritual, whereas the machine appears to dominate. The vision of men becomes astigmatized in the glare of their achievements. The onslaught of the machine seems overwhelming. Offhand I would have no difficulty in naming a dozen that were unknown or little known in my childhood, each of which has

been revolutionary in its action at least to the extent that it has caused a regrouping of the problems of living. A dozen revolutions! Political revolutions have nothing on this. A dozen did I say? Almost I could name a dozen in one field alone, that of electricity; witness the telephone, radio, internal combustion engines, electric lighting, household gadgets and power-driven machinery ad infinitum. Could we be otherwise than machine-minded?

The results on human outlook are so impressive that even thinking people have sometimes been carried away by its illusions. They see the universe as a machine, and only as a machine.

Others, larger in numbers, have not thought deeply and have become machine-intoxicated. To them the machine is nothing less than a demigod. If they do not fall down and worship, it is because they express their subjection to it in some other way. Shall man be enslaved or liberated by his own inventions?

Now I apprehend that this is not the first time in human history that the invention, introduction and use of machinery has dislocated organized living. A view of years long gone by may help interpret the present. In the remote past our ancestors were emerging into a new epoch of achievement. Somebody or some group invented the wheel and combined two wheels to make a cart. That primitive beginning of the machine caused as radical a regrouping of the problems of living in that remote day as have the complicated

machines for us in our day. It brought man out of the cave. It enabled him more readily to erect mounds of earth, and transport materials and build shelters for himself in suitable locations. 

It extended his contacts and increased his knowledge. Revolutionary as was its effect upon his living, he nevertheless mastered his two-wheeled machine. He made progress because he mastered it. Today as yesterday man can keep his poise. It is he and not the machine that has the mastery. 

As I look at my world, what do I see in the way of significant social adjustments? Freedom is very precious to men. Radical social experimentation is being made in the search for freedom. 

The shattering of the foundations upon which was built the pursuit of wealth, together with the fear of economic insecurity, has caused the major interest to center about economic independence. 

We face a world groping in distress. We see homeless, wandering men; pinch-faced, timid women; shivering, supperless children. It moves us tremendously if we allow ourselves to look at it, as we seldom do. Terrible as it is, it is only part of the picture. Economic misery must first be relieved and ultimately prevented. It is important to live comfortably. But it is more important to live for a great ideal. A free spirit is more to be desired than a full stomach. Dedication to a noble purpose is better than economic security without a noble purpose. I do not mean to set one over against the other, but they should be

seen in proper perspective. Economic stress should not distort our sense of values. 

Some of you will remember that first scene from the play Green Pastures. The old Sunday school teacher is reciting to the pickaninnies the sacred history from the first chapters of Genesis. "And 

Methuselah lived a hundred and eighty and seven years, and he begat Lamech: and Methuselah lived after he begat Lamech seven hundred and eighty and two years, and he begat sons and daughters: and all the days of Methuselah were nine hundred and sixty and nine years and he died." Without quibbling over the question of literalism here, let us take the story as it is. Methuselah must have been economically independent and he had plenty of leisure. A little figuring will show that his wife washed the dishes for him more than a million times and made up his bed more than 300,000 times. Yet the most that could be said for him was that he lived 969 years and he died. One wonders if it was all worth while.

Another man lived. The span of his life was a short thirty-three years. In the land where he lived property values were so separated from human values that it was said, "The foxes have holes and the birds of the air have nests, but the human being has not where to lay his head." After a crowded life of helpfulness, spiritual and material, he was put to death at an early age because the leaders of the land esteemed economic security more than the freedom of the spirit. Who would say that the life of Jesus was not worth while?

Before me today are two paths, each inviting me to freedom. One stresses economic freedom by way of compulsory social action. The other emphasizes individual initiative as the pathway to the largest life. The extreme of one is collectivism. The extreme of the other is anarchism. 

These characterizations are inadequate, and while it would be manifestly unfair to ascribe to either one or the other an exclusive contribution to human welfare, I must in fairness say that I am more inclined to look for helpful progress by stressing individualism than I am by compulsory collectivism.

Socialization under compulsion makes a strong appeal today to many who are deeply concerned to find the way out of a situation that seems intolerable. But the escape proposed is only partial and the loss involved is too great. As a philosophy, collectivism has produced a number of striking slogans: "Production for use and not for profit"; "Planned production"; "From each according to his ability, and to each according to his need"; "Peace and security"; "Collectivism or chaos"; "Effective leadership"; etc. These sound well. They may have real value. But I confess to a growing distrust of slogans as signboards to point the way to the reconstruction of society. I confess further to a skepticism of compulsory social action prescribed for us from above. 

Of course free men will co-operate, of course they will do together those things which can be better done together than separately. But it will

be the co-operation of free personalities. It will spring from the grass roots and work itself out through a society so ordered that individual responsibility, a thing highly desirable, shall find its expression in co-operative effort in building a world where each one may be at his best. 

There are two further words you will expect me to say and I think I ought to say them. In two areas of human contacts-or let us say human frictions we find the vehicle of progress frequently stalled with a hotbox. These are the areas of international affairs and interracial relations. 

As I look at my world it seems to me that civilization has advanced only as it has been able to escape from the scourges of war and economic and racial inequality.

The arbitrary exercise of force retards more than it accelerates progress. By arbitrary force I mean that which is exercised in disregard of the worth of personality. We frequently hear the plea that a peace pact must be implemented, that a union of democracies must be implemented, that there must be a "League to Enforce Peace."

The most that can be said for such an anomalous procedure is that it would put the wrongdoing in better hands. It would probably result in more efficient wrongdoing, making it not only anomalous but more effectively destructive.

Ruthless disregard of personality in the imposition of racial discrimination is equally undesirable. Whatever may have been the conditions in the past, it is true today that all peoples move

forward together. Differences of complexion, physical features, customs and habits give picturesqueness and color to the march. It becomes increasingly clear that anything which places limitations on the free functioning of any group retards the progress of all. It is equally certain that to erect barriers between classes is to block the traffic on the road of human development. 

This is not to ignore the dangers involved in interracial contacts. But it is a dangerous thing to live. Stout hearts weaken not because of dangers but because of vain and futile aims. We must live dangerously if we would achieve.

Mankind is on the road. The cry of "Westwardho!" has for its goal a new heaven and a new earth wherein dwelleth righteousness. Possession of "free lands" today is accomplished by spiritual discoveries. They are lost only as the locusts of hate devour the heritage. Much of the baggage of the past will be cast aside. There will be a widening of the horizons of friendliness, a broadening of human fellowship as we resolutely follow the gleam. The injunction, "Thus far shalt thou go and no farther," has no meaning to the free spirit. 

That there is an unseen world beyond I cannot doubt. We seem to touch the fringes of this unknown realm through the intangible realities which press upon us in this life, some of which I have tried to describe. It is a wise Providence that veils from our eyes that unexplored country, lest we become careless of our responsibilities dur-

ing the span of this life when we must function in a world of material things. That other world may be a world of material things, too, but it is another world outside our vision. To me my life at its best-all human life at its best-is infinitely beautiful and incomprehensibly precious and immeasurable in its outreach.

Some day for me, as for others, the sands in the hourglass of life will run out. I will cease to function in what is now my visible world. Will that invisible world of myself, which is the only basis of certainty or reality, cease to be? Beyond the veil of darkness is there only chaos, or is that world just hidden in shadow which with the turn will break into the splendor of the dawn?

In my home we have a radio. On a quiet evening I adjust the dial and turn the switch that releases the sensitive filaments to gather from all out-of-doors only the sounds that are fitted for just that instrument. At times I get static and am disturbed by its raucous screech. More often my ears are smitten by jazz or harangues or just clatter. But sometimes there comes a symphony, a lovely song, a heartening discourse. The instrument itself seems almost humanly tender. 

May I use this as a parable? Human life is so mysterious, so marvelously tender and beautiful, it seems imperative that it should project itself beyond its material limitations. Call it poetic imagination if you wish, or emotional reaction if you prefer. To me it is a most important reality.

Somewhere-it may be far in the unfathomable,

illimitable bosom of God's creation, or it may be very, very near-there will be that which answers to my own heart-throb, that which responds to the wave-lengths of my own life, that which will be home to me when death and dissolution come. It is life made new.
