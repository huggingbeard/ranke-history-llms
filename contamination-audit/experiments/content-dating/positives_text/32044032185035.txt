President Carter told the Congress and the Nation in his State of the Union Message of January 19, 1978 that he would propose drug law reforms. 

He said: "We will propose legislation to reform regulation of the drug industry, which will protect the consumer and make regulations fairer and less burdensome." The President also announced that he would continue to pursue vigorously his Administration's commitment to devising ways further "to reduce the burden of outdated, ineffective and nitpicking regulations."

The "Drug Regulation Reform Act of 1978" is the fulfilment of President Carter's plan for human drugs. This Section-by-Section Analysis is a detailed explanation of that comprehensive proposal. The provisions of the bill were developed from the views and experiences of those who administer and enforce the current law and consultations with and suggestions and advice from representatives of the Congress, other executive departments and agencies, affected industry groups and individual drug firms, professional and health care providers and organizations, and consumer and public interest groups. 

There is a clear consensus that the current law, enacted 40 years ago, is inadequate. The country needs a drug law that is capable of dealing with problems and issues that the current one is obviously unable to face, or can face only with a new set of tailor-made regulations. 

Essentially, the current law is unable to respond to basic scientific advances promptly or efficiently and this failure has transformed the law from a mechanism to facilitate safe and effective drug therapy to one that increasingly hinders it. 

The dynamic developments in the science of drug therapy clearly have outstripped a relatively static law. The new law will redress that imbalance.

DRUG REGULATION REFORM ACT OF 1978 

S. 2755 and H.R. 11611

SECTION-BY-SECTION ANALYSIS TO ACCOMPANY THE PROPOSED LEGISLATION 

MARCH 24, 1978

The Drug Regulation Reform Act of 1978 contains two separate titles. 

Title I revises and reforms Federal laws applicable to the regulation of drugs for human use that are being marketed or tested in the United States or being imported into or exported from this country. Title II 

amends the Public Health Service Act to create a National Center for 

Clinical Pharmacology in the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare.

Section 1 of the bill contains its short title ("Drug Regulation Reform 

Act of 1978") and a table of contents of the sections in the bill.

TITLE I 

DRUG REGULATION REFORM Title I represents a revision and restatement of all of the current provisions of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act ("FD&C Act") and the Public Health Service Act regarding drugs for human use. The Drug 

Regulation Reform Act does not affect the laws governing veterinary drugs. Title I also contains reforms of the authorities of the Secretary under the FD&C Act to assure the safety, effectiveness, and quality of all drugs for use in humans and to enforce the new law. Title I is divided into 6 parts as follows:

Part A 

Part B 

Findings and Declarations; Definitions. 

Approval of Drug Entities and Drug Products. 

Part C - Drug Information, Labeling, Promotion, and Education. 

Part D - Prohibited Acts and Penalties. 

Part E- Administrative Provisions.

Conforming, Transitional, Effective Date, and Other General Provisions.

§ 101 

§ 102 PART A 

FINDINGS AND DECLARATIONS; DEFINITIONS Section 101. Findings and Declarations. This section states the principal objectives sought by the bill and the justification for making the regulatory controls of the Drug Regulation Reform 

Act applicable to all drugs regardless of whether they or their components have ever been outside the State in which they are found.

Section 102. Definitions. This section sets out definitions for terms that have particular significance to the structure and implementation of the Drug Regulation Reform Act. Some of these terms have definitions in current Federal law which, although appropriate in the context of other statutes, may be confusing or inappropriate in this law. 

Paragraphs (1), (6), and (7) define three distinct elements of the term "drug" as now defined in section 201 (g)(1) of the FD&C Act. These elements are the drug itself, the product containing the drug, and a component of the drug or the product. Section 102 recognizes the differences among these elements under the regulatory scheme created by part B of Title I. 

For example, no unique requirements are imposed on the manufacture and distribution of non-drug substances (or so-called "inactive ingredients") before they are used in the making of a drug or drug product containing the drug; consequently, these items are defined in section 102(1), and are regulated under the bill, as "components." The definition also clearly establishes that the term includes (1) substances that are used in the manufacturing process but do not remain in the finished material

$ 4 (e.g., liquids used for rinsing and then removed by drying), and (2) substances that are contained in the finished material, even if they were not intended to be present (e.g., undesired contaminants). This breadth assures that anything which might adulterate the finished drug product is subject to appropriate controls (e.g., under section 116).

In order to focus on the substance that actually provides the therapeutic benefit (as distinct from the product that is used to deliver this substance into the patient's body), section 102(6) defines "drug entity". The definition is patterned after section 201(g)(1)(B) and (C) of the FD&C Act, and includes substances intended to be used in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, or treatment of a disease, injury, or other condition affecting the health of a human, or in the prevention on a disease or health condition, or otherwise to affect or evaluate the structure or any function of the human body. One of the changes from current law is the express recognition of injury or other health conditions as targets for drug therapy. Because the existing statute refers only to "disease," it has occasionally been interpreted to exclude conditions which are considered "natural," but which are attended by medical needs often fulfilled by use of drugs. These conditions include puberty, pregnancy, and menopause. Drugs used in conjunction with such conditions should not be excluded from the definition of, and the regulatory control over, drug entities. Likewise, a substance used in a human to diagnose or evaluate normal bodily functions (such as metabolism, blood flow, or glucose tolerance) should be clearly included in the definition of a drug entity, even though it may not fit within the current statutory definition of a drug as "affecting" the body or its functioning.

National Formulary, or the Homeopathic Pharmacopeia. Compilers of the United States Pharmacopeia and the National Formulary recommended the deletion. The treatment of homeopathic drugs in subpart 8 of part B of Title I clearly subjects an article in the 

Homeopathic Pharmacopeia to this title. One Federal Circuit 

Court of Appeals has held that the definitional standard in section 201(g) (1) (A) of the FD&C Act cannot be accepted as the sole test of whether something is a "drug". National Nutritional Foods Assn. v. 

FDA, 491 F 2d. 1141(2nd Cir. 1974). Continuing to rely upon that definition would be of doubtful legality, and would certainly serve no regulatory purpose.

Section 102(6) (B) follows the current law in excluding foods, cosmetics, and medical devices from the definition of "drug entity", because these are regulated under distinct statutory schemes. The new definition would, however, preserve the priniciple, found in sections 411(a)(2) and 509 of the FD&C Act, that if a food or cosmetic is represented for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitgation, or treatment of a disease or other health condition in a human, or in the prevention of a disease or other condition affecting the health of a human, the food or cosmetic becomes subject to regulation as a drug entity as well as a food or cosmetic.

Under the Drug Regulation Reform Act, drug entities and drug products are frequently subject to different regulatory requirements in accordance with the differences between them.

Section 107. Drug Monographs. This section summarizes the system of drug monographs established by subpart 2. 

Subsection (a) states the general contents of a monograph: (1) identification of the drug entity, (2) statement of the indications for which use of the products to be licensed under the monograph has been determined to be safe and effective, (3) description of the types of products that may be licensed under the monograph, and (4) prescription of standards and requirements as to such products.

§ 16 Subsection (b) states that provisions as to authority and procedures respecting monographs are contained in sections 109-112.

A monograph, under subpart 2, differs from a differs from a new drug application (NDA) under current law in the following principal respects: (1) a monograph relates to a drug entity, and numerous manufacturers of products containing the entity may be licensed under the same monograph; an NDA relates to a drug product, and each manufacturer of a product containing the same entity must have its own NDA; (2) a monograph is a public document, issued on the basis of publicly available information and after a public procedure; an NDA is a private document, issued on the basis of undisclosed information and after a procedure that, except for advisory committee meetings, is generally not open to public participation or review. A monograph and an NDA are alike, however, in that approval of a monograph and approval of the first NDA for a product containing a newly discovered entity each constitute the Secretary's decision that a drug is safe and effective and may be marketed in the United States.

When a pioneer manufacturer develops a new drug entity, it will seek approval of (1) a monograph applicable to the entity and (2) a license to market a drug product containing the entity. 

In general, matters that relate to the drug entity will be contained in the monograph or in the appendices to the monograph, and matters that relate to a drug product licensed under the monograph will be contained in the license for the product. No trade secret information will be contained in monographs or their appendices; trade secret information will be contained only in licenses. Licenses generally will be private documents, particularly in regard to details on manufacturing processes. Information labeling for drug products will be contained in licenses and also in appendices to monographs.

Section 109. Standards for Issuance, Amendment, Suspension, and Revocation of Monographs Generally. Section 109 establishes the criteria to be used for issuing, amending, suspending, and revoking monographs for drug entities, in all but the exceptional cases covered in section 110.

$ 30 Subsection (a) establishes the general requirement that the Secretary shall issue a monograph for a drug entity under section 109 if he determines that the drug entity (1) is effective, (2) has been assessed for risks, and (3) is safe.

In issuing a monograph, the Secretary is obligated to follow the procedures set forth in section 111.

Subsection (b) provides that, before the Secretary may issue a monograph under section 109, the Secretary shall determine that the petitioner for the monograph has shown, by substantial evidence, that the drug entity described in the monograph is effective. Subsection (b) also provides definitions for two very important terms used in sections 109 and 

110: "effective" and "substantial evidence."

The term "effective" is defined to mean that a drug entity, when incorporated into a drug product which is used in accordance with the conditions of use set forth in the information labeling for that drug product, will have the effect that it is represented in that labeling to have in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, or treatment of a disease or other condition affecting the health of a human, or in the prevention of a disease or other condition affecting the health of a human. 

This definition does not differ from the current FD&C Act standards. An important improvement has been made over the current law, however, in that significance of the effectiveness of the drug is expressly considered for the first time in making decisions regarding approvability of the drug. (This improvement will be discussed under subsection (e) below).

$ 109

The requirement of effectiveness was introduced by the Drug Amendments of 1962. It has made a major contribution to public health by significantly reducing the number of ineffective drugs on the market and by preventing the marketing of new ineffective drugs that may be doubly dangerous--either because of inherent risks or because of the failure to act on a serious disease or injury that may otherwise be treated. The drug industry, the medical profession, consumer groups, and others, such as the HEW Review Panel on New Drug Regulations (Dorsen Panel), strongly support retention of the effectiveness requirement.

Subsection (b) defines the term "substantial evidence" to mean evidence consisting of adequate and well-controlled investigations, including clinical investigations, conducted by experts qualified by scientific training and experience to evaluate the effectiveness of the drug entity involved, on the basis of which it could fairly and responsibly be concluded, by experts qualified by scientific training and experience to evaluate the effectiveness of the drug entity involved, that the drug entity will have the effect represented in information labeling for drug products eligible to be licensed under the monograph. This definition is substantially the same as that set forth in section 505(d) of the FD&C Act.

S 32 This standard reflects the desire of Congress in the Drug Amendments of 1962 that the determination of effectiveness be made by objective scientific studies. The majority of health professionals and citizens who have considered the queston strongly support the current standard. After a 2-year study, the HEW Review Panal on New Drug Regulation concluded in 1977 that the available evidence did not justify "fundamental changes in the rigorous review of new drugs for safety and efficacy which is mandated by the 1962 amendments." (The 

Dorsen Panel also concluded that "the current drug review process can be made considerably more efficient and the entire process can be expedited." Many of the aspects of the Drug 

Regulation Reform Act of 1978 are intended to achieve these goals.) The principle established by Congress in 1962 that adequate and well controlled scientific investigations serve as the standard to evaluate the effectiveness of drugs, a principle which was strongly endorsed by the Supreme Court in 1973 (Weinberger v. Hyson, Westcott, and Dunning, Inc., 412 U.S. 

609), should not be altered, except in the tightly limited and compelling circumstances set forth in section 110. 

Subsection (c) states that the Secretary may issue a monograph only if the Secretary determines that (1) the petitioner has shown that the drug entity has been tested by all methods reasonably applicable and sufficient to detect and evaluate the risks of the drug entity and (2) the results of these tests have been submitted to the Secretary. A requirement for testing "by all methods reasonably applicable" appears in section 505(d)(1) of the FD&C Act and has been part of the law since 1938. The requirement does not mean that every conceivable test must be performed on a drug, but rather that

$ 109 adequate tests are needed to assess the risks of the drug under the circumstances of its intended use. The proposed bill would add the words "and sufficient" to the phrase "all methods resonably applicable" to make this point clear.

Subsection (d) references the various discretionary authorities of the Secretary under section 108 to include additional requirements or standards in a monograph, but only if the requisite determinations are made with respect to imposing a particular standard or requirement. 

Subsection (e) states the fundamental standard for the decision whether to issue a monograph and thereby approve a drug for marketing in the United States. The Secretary may issue a monograph only upon a determination that the petitioner has shown the drug entity to be safe. The term "safe" is defined to mean that the health benefits of the drug entity clearly outweigh the risks presented by the drug entity, considering the standards and requirements applicable to drug products eligible to be licensed under the monograph. Thus the law makes it clear that the decision on whether a drug can be used for medical care in the United States is a risk-benefit determination. The definition of "safety" reflects longstandard practice in evaluating drugs for approval. The definition does not lower the standards applied under current law. Every drug--even aspirin--presents some risks. If "safety" were defined to mean the absence of any risk, then no drug could be approved.

$ 34 This clarification should eliminate or reduce the confusion that presently arises regarding drug decisions under the FD&C Act. Because the term "safe" is not defined in the current law, many people erroneously assume that the term means "harmless" or "innocuous". In permitting such a misinterpretation, the law does a substantial disservice to consumers and patients, who may make a decision to use a particular drug with little awareness of the real risks that the drug may pose. 

In determining whether a drug entity is safe, the Secretary is directed to consider several factors: (1) The health benefits of the drug entity and the significance of these benefits. In this regard, the Secretary is to consider the following: (a) the known, suspected, or potential effects on the health of patients, and the frequency and magnitutde of these effects, that result from the effectiveness of the drug entity; (b) the known effects on the acceptability of drug products eligible to be licensed under the monograph to patients, due to the convenience of the dosage forms, the frequency and severity of adverse effects, or related factors; and (c) the known, suspected, or potential effects on the public health that result from the effectiveness of the drug entity.

The essential health benefit of any drug is, of course, its effectiveness. Nevertheless, the significance of the effectiveness of many drugs is often debatable, particularly in cases where the long-terms effects in reducing morbidity or

S 109 mortality of patients are not clear from physiological effects which the drug is shown to produce in the body. A drug may be clearly effective in producing certain changes in the body (e.g., lowering blood pressure or cholesterol levels in the blood), but the overall therapeutic effects of such changes may not be known or agreed upon by expert medical opinion. Thus, it is important to consider the known or potential significance of the positive effects of the drug in determining whether its benefits outweigh its risks.

In addition to the effectiveness of a drug, other factors relate to the benefits it may provide. For example, the extent to which a drug is acceptable to patients (e.g., due to ease of administration or frequency of administration) may encourage or discourage patient compliance with a selected course of therapy. (2) The various risks presented by the drug entity, and the significance of these risks. Among the risks are (a) the known or suspected adverse effects upon the health of patients and persons handling the drug, including the frequency and severity of these effects, that result from the use of the drug under the conditions set forth in its labeling, (b) the potential adverse effects upon the health of humans implied by animal investigations or other scientific information, including information regarding chemically or pharmacologically related drug entities; (c) the known, suspected, or potential adverse effects upon the health of individuals when the drug is used for conditions not set forth in the labeling, but which are known or can be expected to occur, including inten-

$ 36 tional abuse of the drug; and (d) the known, suspected, or potential adverse impact on the public health when drug products are used under conditions which may or may not be set forth in the labeling.

These four items make it clear that the risks of a drug include the risks from improper use and the societal risks that are presented by the drug. Therefore, a drug that may be acceptable on a risk-benefit basis in a few individuals may be unacceptable when viewed from the standpoint of society as a whole. The listing of these various possible adverse effects does not mean that the risks of each must be tested for and assessed under subsection (e), which only requires tests by methods reasonably applicable. It would be wasteful of scarce scientific resources, and would unnecessarily delay approval of drugs, if every conceivable risk had to be fully explored for purposes of evaluation under subsection (e)(2). (3) The benefits and risks associated with other forms of therapy, including drug therapy, that are available to treat the population of patients for whom the drug is intended. This provision does not require that a drug offer a better riskbenefit ratio than currently available drugs in order to be approved. Few drugs are uniformly effective in all patients, and most carry risks that vary from patient to patient as well. 

It is therefore not generally possible to regulate on the premise that one can demand ever more favorable risk-benefit ratios for new products. Nevertheless, there occasionally occur situations in which one drug presents a substantially better or worse risk-benefit ratio than all of the currently available

$ 109 products. For example, the FDA recently removed from the market a series of older drugs when a newer product was developed which was equally effective in all patients and offered an incidence rate for the most severe side effect that was only 1% of that of the older products. The substantial safety gain presented by the newer product was clearly an appropriate factor to consider in determining the marketability of both groups of products. (4) The adequacy of information labeling for assuring the appropriate use of the drug. In determining whether to approve a drug, the Secretary shall consider the circumstances under which the drug is likely to be used in practice and whether the labeling directed to physicians and patients is adequate to assure that the drug is used properly, both to achieve its effectiveness and to prevent undue risk. (5) Finally, the adequacy of the standards and requirements described in the monograph in assuring the appropriate use of drug products. All of the remaining circumstances regarding the drug products to be licensed under the monograph (e.g., restriction to dispensing on prescription, requirements for surveillance of use, and the conditions for marketing individual products) must promote the proper use of the drug by patients and practitioners.

Section 110. Standards for Provisional Issuance, Amendment, Suspension and Revocation of Drug Monographs in Special Circum stances. This section provides for the accelerated approval of "breakthrough" drugs if certain special requirements are met. A provisional monograph for a drug entity could be issued under this section only if all of the following conditions are met: (1) (A) The drug entity is intended for use in a lifethreatening or severely debilitating or disabling disease or injury, (B) The drug entity offers a major therapeutic advantage to patients with that disease or injury, and (C) The patient population will not be adequately served by distribution of the drug under a registration for a drug treatment investigation under section 129;

$ 110 (2) Delaying approval of the drug entity would present significantly greater risks to the patients than would issuing the monograph; (3) The drug entity has been assessed for risks; (4) There is significant evidence of the effectiveness of the drug entity, and well-controlled studies of its effectiveness are under way, if they are ethically and methodologically possible; and (5) The benefits of provisional approval of the drug entity clearly outweigh the risks from such approval.

$ 42 Sometimes it may become apparent, even before adequate and well-controlled clinical studies are completed, that a new drug constitutes a major therapeutic advance. The purpose of provisional approval is to enable patients to obtain the life saving or other major health benefits of a breakthrough drug earlier than is possible under the current law or under section 109. At the same time, section 110 maintains high standards of evidence regarding risk assessment and effectiveness and assures that, if at all possible, the adequate and well-controlled trials regarding effectiveness will be completed.

A drug entity provisionally approved under this section would be subject to the following restrictions: products containing the entity could be dispensed only on prescription; licensees under the monograph would be subject to recordkeeping and reporting requirements and to surveillance requirements; and well-controlled clinical studies of the drug entity would have to be conducted, if ethically and methodologically possible. The Secretary could impose additional restrictions under section 108.

A provisional monograph would expire after three years, and the drug entity would automatically be withdrawn from the market, unless the monograph were renewed on the basis of new findings that all the requirements for a provisional monograph continued to be met. Upon commencement by the Secretary of proceedings to revoke a provisional monograph, the monograph would also be immediately suspended.

S 110 The requirement that well-controlled clinical studies of effectiveness be completed and evaluated prior to approval of a drug entity has been a bulwark of public health protection since it was enacted in 1962. That standard should be retained for full approval of any drug entity. Section 110 would refine that requirement by authorizing temporary, "provisional" approval for certain drugs without completion of well-controlled studies in very limited and narrowly drawn circumstances. 

Such approval would be termed "provisional" and would be subject to mandatory and discretionary restrictions and limitations not generally applicable to full approval. In particular, a provisional approval could be terminated more readily than could a full approval.

This section does not compromise in any way the principle that only drugs shown to be safe may be marketed. When a petition for provisional approval is presented, the Secretary will have to make the same kind of benefit-risk assessment that is made when a petition for full approval (issuance of a monograph) is presented. The Secretary will have less evidence of effectiveness, but that evidence has to be sufficient to justify a finding that the drug entity offers major therapeutic advantages for patients with a life-threatening or severely debilitating illness or injury. An illness or injury will meet this standard only if it is life-threatening or severely debilitating in a significant percentage of the patients who have the illness or injury. In addition, the Secretary could issue a provisional monograph only on the basis of a finding that the drug entity is safe. "Safety" for the pur-

S 44 poses of section 110 (provisional approval) has the same meaning as for the purposes of section 109 (full approval). 

See sections 109(e) and 110(e).

In place of well-controlled studies of effectiveness, this section requires "significant evidence" of effectiveness, that is: evidence consisting of valid and meaningful scientific investigations, including investigations in animals and well-documented clinical experience and clinical investigations (unless such investigations are not feasible due to an absence of methodology or due to ethical proscriptions on such investigations), conducted by experts qualified by scientific training and experience to evaluate the effectiveness of the drug entity involved, on the basis of which it could fairly and responsibly be concluded by experts, qualified by scientific training and experience to evaluate the effectiveness of the drug entity involved, that the drug entity will have the effect represented in information labeling for drug products eligible to be licensed under the monograph. Under this test, drug entities for which there is little or no scientific evidence of effectiveness could not be approved. 

One of the findings required for provisional approval is that the patient population to be served by the drug entity

S 111 could not be adequately served through a drug treatment investigation under section 129. A drug treatment investigation is substantially the same as a "compassionate IND" under current law and practice. Under a registration for such an investigation, an unapproved drug product could be made available to physicians on individual request. It is plain that such a distribution system can meet the needs of only a very small number of patients, who are being treated by physicians familiar with the drug treatment investigation procedures and willing to assume the burden of invoking and complying with those procedures. In some cases, the appropriate patient population for an unapproved drug product may be served under a drug treatment investigation registration on individual request.

In other cases, however, the appropriate patient population will be too large or to widely dispersed for that system; and, consequently, provisional approval may be appropriate. 

In sum, section 110 has been carefully drafted to meet the specific need for early approval of drug entities that constitute major therapeutic advances, and to meet that need without opening a loophole for provisional approval of unsafe, ineffective, or otherwise unnecessary new drug products.

Subpart 3 sets forth the requirements applicable to all drug products, whether licensed (for manufacture and distribution) under subpart 4, registered (for investigational use) under subpart 5, or permitted (for export) under subpart 6, of part B of Title I. These requirements fall into three categories: (1) registration of establishments in which the drug entities or products are handled (section 113); (2) general standards relating to the quality of drug entities and products (sections 114-118); and (3) rules relating to the importation of drug entities and products (section 119).

Section 113. Requirements for Registration of Establishments in Which Drug Entities and Drug Products are Manufactured and Distributed. Section 113 is intended to provided the Secretary with information on the establishments in which drug entities and drug products are handled, so that inspections can be conducted to assure that the substantive requirements of are fulfilled. 

The provisions in this section are based upon language enacted by Congress in section 302 of the Drug Amendments of 1962 and in the Drug Listing Act of 1972, and codified in section 510 of the FD&C Act.

Subsection (a) requires any person who manufactures imports, exports, or distributes a drug entity or a drug product, in an establishment located inside the United States, to register each establishment in which the activity occurs or is to occur.

Under section 510(b) of the FD&C Act, such registration is required now on an annual basis. In addition, each person at the time of entering into operations requiring registration

§ 58 must register under section 510(c), and each person commencing operations in an additional establishment must add that establishment to the registration under section 510(d). 

As drafted, section 113 (a) carries forward the requirements of the FD&C Act, except that it does not require annual renewal of registration. Implementation of section 510(b) has shown that annual renewals are not a cost-effective way of maintaining information regarding persons and establishments subject to regulatory oversight. In place of annual registration, subsection (c) (2) of section 113 authorizes the Secretary to require any person who has registered an establishment under the section to amend the information required for registration from time to time in order to maintain current records relating to registration. This revised authority would allow the 

Secretary to engage in systematic updating of particular categories of information as the need for such updating arises. 

Section 113 (a) also extends the registration requirement under current law to distributors of drug entities and drug products. Many of these distribute drug products under their own commercial names (although the products are manufactured by other firms), and most hold drug products under circumstances which, if not properly managed, can affect the products adversely. Registration will provide the Secretary with essential information about distributors. The burden on the individual distributors should be minimal; many are cur rently registered on an annual basis with the Drug Enforcement Administration of the Department of Justice under the Controlled Substances Act. The registration requirement does not

S 113 apply to pharmacists and practitioners who only dispense drug products to patients; under section 102(5), distribution does not include dispensing (as defined in section 102(4)). 

Subsection (b) requires persons who are manufacturing drugs outside the United States for importation into the United States to register the establishments at which the activities are occurring. This is a change from the current law. Section 510(i) of the FD&C Act permits, but does not require, a foreign establishment to be registered. Experience under 510(i) has shown that registration provides a valuable mechanism for the Secretary and for the Customs Services to monitor drug entities and drug products that are offered for import into the United States, to reduce the administrative costs of reviewing such entities and products on a shipmentby-shipment basis, and to reduce the time the goods are held before they are released for use in the United States. 

Without registration, incoming shipments must be sampled and examined to determine whether they comply with applicable regulatory requirements in accordance with section 801 (a) of the FD&C Act. The cost of such testing is borne by the government, unless the goods are rejected (in which case, under section 801(c), the government's expenses must be reimbursed by the importer of the commodity). Thus, this expense is generally passed on to the taxpayers. In addition, to the extent that delaying release of approvable commodities increases manufacturing costs, consumers are indirectly affected by this practice through higher prices. It is therefore proposed that foreign establishments be required to register and that the voluntary system under current law be eliminated.

S 60 Subsection (b) also contains a provision in current section 510(i) of the FD&C Act that a foreign establishment not be registered unless the Secretary finds adequate and effective means are available, either by agreement by the applicant or by arrangement of the government of the country in which the establishment is located, to enable the Secretary to determine whether drug entities and drug products offered for import into the United States should be refused admission under the new bill. Routine sampling of imported products is not, by itself, an adequate substitute for inspection of manufacturing and related facilities. 

Currently the FDA has bilateral rrangements with the governments of Canada, Sweden and Switzerland under which each country's health authority will accept the results of inspections of manufacturers conducted by the health authority of the other country, in lieu of making inspections. The inspections serve as a basis for permitting imports into the respective countries. These arrangements have been very satisfactory, and the Secretary will seek opportunities to expand their use.

Where reciprocal bilateral arrangements are not possible, the FDA has entered into arrangements with individual firms that permit FDA to inspect those firms at the overseas site on a routine basis. Such an arrangement would serve as a satisfactory basis for allowing registration under section 113(b).

S 113 Subsection (c) lists the information necessary for registration: the name of the person seeking to register the establishments; the address of the principal place of business of such person; the address of each establishment to be registered, including a brief description of the activity occurring at the establishment (e.g., manufacturing, packaging or repacking, labeling or relabeling, or distributing); and a list of the drug entities being manufactured, imported, exported, or distributed. This list is patterned after requirements in current section 510(j) of the FD&C Act and implementing regulations, but eliminates unneccessary items of information which would no longer be necessary under the Drug Regulation Reform Act. In particular, because of the elimination of the various subcategories of drugs that exist under the FD&C Act, information clarifying which category a registrant claims a particular drug to be in is no longer necessary. Likewise, because of the product license, registration, and permit provisions, no separate list of drug products would be necessary under section 113(c).

Paragraph (2) of subsection (c) allows the Secretary to require a registrant to update the registration information from time to time in order to keep the Department's files current. This procedure, it is believed, will reduce the burdens both on the regulated industry and on the FDA under the current annual registration system.

Registration under Section 113 is automatic upon submission of the required information; and, in the case of establish-

Requirements Regarding Manufacturing and Distri- 

Section 114 relates to the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, manufacturing, holding, and distributing a drug entity or a drug product. It establishes the requirements which these activities must meet in order to assure drug product quality.

Subsection (a) empowers the Secretary to prescribe regulations requiring that the methods used in, and the facilities and controls used for, manufacturing, holding, and distributing a drug entity or a drug product conform to current good manufacturing practice, as determined by the Secretary, in order to assure that a drug entity or drug product will be safe, will have the identity, strength, and bioavailability, and will meet the quality, purity, and stability characteristics, required under the new statute. 

This subsection is based on section 501(a)(2)(B) of the FD&C Act, added by the Drug Amendments of 1962, which requires that the processes and facilities used in manufacturing and holding drugs conform to "current good manufacturing practice" in the industry. The statutory language has been changed in four respects: First, the word "distributing" has been added to assure that the scope of the regulations includes distribution operations as well as manufacturing and holding operations. Second, it is made clear that the good practices are determined by the Secretary. Third, the Secretary is expressly authorized to set forth the required regulation. 

Fourth, the Secretary is directed to consider the competitive effects of good manufacturing practice regulations. For example, the Secretary should not specify one machine be

S 64 used when several, provided by different suppliers, are equally acceptable to achieve the desired objective. 

Generally, the Secretary determines what are current good manufacturing practices through public rule-making procedures; and in some cases, applicability of the requirements in a particular situation may necessitate expert opinion to supplement the regulation. Although the legislative history of the Drug Amendments of 1962 is quite clear that Congress intended the Secretary to issue regulations implementing the current good manufacturing practice standard of the FD&C Act (in accordance with the Secretary's rulemaking authority under section 701(a) of that Act), and despite several comprehensive reviews and restatements of good manufacturing practice regulations in the last 16 years, objections to the Secretary's legal authority and to the legal status of these regulations continue to be raised and should be resolved with finality. 

Subsection (b) provides for variances from current good practice to be permitted by the Secretary under certain circumstances. Section 501 (a) (2) (B) of the FD&C Act does not provide for such variances, although in practice they have been made through approvals of new drug applications, biological product licenses, and other product review procedures of the FDA. The Medical Device Admendments of 1976, however, authorized variances and exemptions from the good manufacturing practices requirements for medical devices under section 520(f) of the FD&C Act. Subsection (b) of section 114 was patterned after the Medical Device Amendments, with certain modifications.

S 114 First, there are no exemptions from good manufacturing practice requirements. The wide range of differences that exist among various types of medical devices does not exist among various groups of drug products and, unlike the case of medical devices, there are few, if any, drug products for which compliance with good manufacturing practices is not appropriate to assure that the drug product is safe and would otherwise meet the requirements relating to product quality.

Second, under the Medical Device Amendments, a variance is allowed when the Secretary determines that the alternative methods and facilities "are sufficient" to assure that the device will be safe, effective, and otherwise in compliance with the FD&C Act. Again, the wide variations among medical devices makes this test more useful for their regulation than for regulation of drugs, where the general good manufacturing practice regulations have been and continue to be applicable to all drug products.

Therefore, subsection (b) of section 114 provides that a variance would be permitted only in two cases: (1) where the alternative methods, facilities, or controls proposed by the person seeking the variance are at least equal to those required under the regulations, or (2) where compliance with the regulations would result in the drug product or drug entity being adversely affected, degraded, or rendered unstable and where alternative methods, facilities, or controls would be adequate to assure that the drug entity or drug product will be

$ 66 safe and will meet the requirements of the law. Experience over the last 16 years indicates that these two exceptions are sufficient to assure that the technology of the industry is not frozen by a rigid set of regulations but can continue to evolve to develop new and superior methods, controls, and facilities for producing drug products, and to avoid those rare situations where compliance with the regulations would result in an inferior drug product.

Subsection (c) provides that no drug entity or drug product shall be manufactured, held, or distributed under insanitary conditions whereby it may be contaminated with filth or rendered injurious to health. This is a restatement of section 501 (a) (2) (A) of the FD&C Act, and is intended to assure that, whether or not regulations have been issued under section 114 (a), no drug entity or drug product may be subject to conditions which may render it dangerous to patients or consumers.

Section 115. Requirements Regarding Compendial Standards of 

Identity, Strength, Quality, Purity, and Stability. Section 115 establishes requirements that drug products meet standards to assure that they perform in patients with consistency. 

The key terms are "identity", "strength", "quality", "purity", and "stability". "Identity" refers to whether the drug entity is the chemical which it purports to be. "Strength" means the quantity or concentra tion of drug entity, or the potency or level of activity of the drug entity, in the dosage form. "Quality" and "purity" signify the absence of contaminants or other materials which may increase the risk of adverse effects from, or dilute the effectiveness of, the

S 115 1 drug entity or drug product. "Stability" refers to the ability of a drug entity or drug product to meet its standards of identity, strength, quality and purity over a period of time. 

Subsection (a) continues the requirement under section 501 (d) of the FD&C Act that a drug entity or drug product which is recognized in the United States Pharmacopeia have the identity described in, and not differ in strength from, nor fail in terms of quality, purity, or stability to meet, the standards set forth in that Pharmacopeia. 

Subsection (a) also provides that the determination of compliance will be made by the compendial tests or methods of assay, except as provided in subsection (b). The United 

States Pharmacopeia has recently acquired responsiblity for the National Formulary, a second official compendium under the FD&C Act. This merger, together with steps taken in cooperation with the FDA, should substantially improve the quality of the compendium and increase its utility as a regulatory tool.

Subsection (b) is also based upon current law, and authorizes the Secretary to prescribe alternative or additional standards, tests or methods of assay, if the Secretary determines that the compendial standards are inadequate, incomplete or inappropriate to achieve their objectives. Subsection (b) does go beyond section 501 (b) of the FD&C Act in that it expressly includes compendial standards as well as compendial tests or methods of assay in the matters to be evaluated by the Secretary. This extension is necessary to authorize the

§ 

68 Secretary to supplement the United States Pharmacopeia with additional requirements.

Subsection (b) further provides for alternative standards, tests, or methods of assay to be accepted by the Secretary in approving applications for drug product licenses under subpart 4 of Part B of Title I. Experience has indicated that variations in individual drug products, all of which contain the same drug entity in the same quantity, can lead to problems in utilizing the compendial standards, tests, or methods of assay for all of the various drug products, because of variations in inactive ingredients or methods of manufacture. 

For example, the substitution of one color additive for another can result in the inability of a product to be tested by a certain procedure. It is therefore important that the Secretary be permitted to accept the use of standards, tests, or methods of assay that differ from, but are adequate and appropriate as alternatives to, and have a sensitivity equal to or greater than, those prescribed by the compendium as the basis for determining compliance with the compendial standards.

Requirements Regarding Packaging and Containers of Drug Entities and Drug Products. Section 117 is intended to assure that packaging and containers of drug entities and drug products prevent possible risks to public health or economic deception. 

Subsection (a) requires that packaging that packaging and containers of drug entities and drug products comply with any applicable standards and specifications set forth in the United States Pharmacopeia. This provision carries forward section 502(g) of the FD&C Act and is analogous to section 115(a). 

Subsection (b) contains a provision, similiar to section 115(b), authorizing the Secretary to impose additional or alternative packaging standards and specifications if the 

Secretary finds that the compendial standards or specifications are inadequate, incomplete, or inappropriate to assure safe handling of the drug entity or drug product, to minimize deterioration, to prevent deception of purchasers or contamination of other drug entities or drug products, or to

S 70 otherwise protect the public health.

This authority is a broadening of authority currently existing in sections 502 (h) and (i)(1) of the FD&C Act. 

Among the circumstances contemplated that might fall within the authority of this subsection would be: (a) to assure the safe handling of live vaccines, medical gases, and other drug products which, if improperly handled or insecurely packaged, could create risks to medical personnel and others involved in the distribution and dispensing of these products; (b) to reduce the potential for rapid deterioration of drugs which are inherently unstable, where proper packaging can preserve the identity, strength, quality and purity of the product for an additional period of time; (c) to prevent deception of purchasers where the packaging is so made, formed or filled as to be misleading as to the quantity or quality or contents; and (d) to prevent contamination of other drug entities or drug products, for example, from penicillin-containing products which may be dispensed with the same equipment used to dispense non-penicillin products. 

One mechanism which could be used to carry out some or all of these functions is the so-called "unit-of-use" packaging by which a particular regimen of a drug (e.g., a ten- or thirty-day supply) is prepackaged in a container for dispensing to patients, thus enabling the pharmacist to carry out dispensing functions without having to come into physical contact with the drug product. Oral contraceptives have been manufactured and distributed in unit-of-use packaging for a number of years, and the practice is common with the majority of drug products

S 71 $ 119 dispensed in Europe. Pharmaceutical manufacturers, wholesalers, and retail pharmacies in the United States have been considering unit-of-use packaging for some time, and are now evaluating its potential impact on capital and operating expenses, physician prescribing practices, and pharmacy services. 

Unit-of-use packaging in certain cases would be appropriate to protect the public health, but whether widespread use of unit-of-use packaging would be feasible or in the consumer's best interest needs further study and experience. 

Subsection (c) prohibits the use of any container for packaging a drug entity or drug product, which container either may render its contents injurious to health or which is composed of any poisonous or deleterious substance which may render the contents dangerous. This requirement carries forward section 501 (a)(3) of the FD&C Act.

A period of five years of exclusive use of that information provides a pioneer firm with reasonable protection of its investment. 

This exclusive use period is in addition to, and independent of, any protection the pioneer firm may derive from a patent,

The period of exclusive use runs concurrently with the patent period and does not extend it. If the patent has more that 5 years to run, the exclusive use period will expire before the patent does. The 5 year period does not begin to run until the monograph becomes effective. Thereafter, normal patent rights, if any, are applicable. 

Plausible arguments can be made that the period of exclusive use should be longer or shorter than 5 years. There are no data that point to a particular length of exclusive use as the appropriate period. However, 5 years appears to strike a reasonable balance between the public interest in encouraging investment in development of new drug entities and the public interest in promoting competition among drug products. This policy will also avoid duplicative testing of drug products containing the same drug entity, the unnecessary exposure of human beings to the risks of drug testing, and the waste of scarce scientific resources involved in duplicative testing.

Subpart 5 - Investigational Use of Drug Products 

Subpart 5 reflects another significant reform in the regulation of drugs. Under the current FD&C Act, the subject of clinical investigations involving drugs is discussed in only four sentences in section 505(i) and repeated in section 507 (b). These authorize the Secretary to promulgate regulations regarding the investigational use of drugs and to impose conditions upon such use, including requirements for information submitted to the Secretary, controlling the distribution and use of the investigational drug, and obtaining of informed consent from research subjects. Experience in administering these authorities has shown that a comprehensive restatement of principles and policies in this area is needed in order to guide the agency, the regulated industry, clinical investigators, and the public.

One of the major changes in subpart 5 from the current law is the recognition of three distinct types or categories of clinical investigations involving unapproved drugs. First is the "drug innovation investigation," defined in section 127(a) as an investigation involving a small number of human participants (in normal health or with a disease, injury, or condition under investigation) which is intended to examine the clinical pharmacology of an approved drug entity or an unlicensed drug product or to assess preliminarily its risks and effectiveness. 

This is truly the experimental phase of the drug research and development The process, the first time that a drug entity is introduced into man. objective is generally to determine how the entity functions in the human body and to assess whether there is any value in proceeding into large scale clinical trials. It is estimated that about 90 percent of all drug entities tested at this point do not proceed to the later development phase, because of a lack of pharmacological activity in humans, undesir-

able side effects, or other factors which make it undesirable or unnecessary to invest further resources into research on the entity. Also in this category are investigations designed to study, with the use of drug products, biological mechanisms in humans. Many of these are conducted by individual physician-scientists with no immediate intention of developing a commercial drug product. 

The second category of clinical investigations involving unapproved drugs is the "drug development investigation," which is defined in section 128(a) as being for the purpose of evaluating the effectiveness of a drug product and assessing its risks. This is the portion of the drug research and development process which is intended to provide the evidence to prove the effectiveness perceived for a drug entity in the drug innovation investigation (or during clinical trials conducted on a drug outside the United States). It is during this phase also that the short-term and relatively common adverse effects of the drug are identified and the evidence for assessing the risks of the drug can be gathered. 

Virtually every drug that enters a drug development investigation will be offered for commercial marketing in the United States, unless its risks turn out to be greater than originally anticipated, its effectiveness less than originally perceived, or commercial interest in the drug product declines (e.g., because a competitor markets a better product or the cost of development and production will not be justified by foreseeable revenues). 

The third category of clinical investigation of unapproved drugs involves "drug treatment investigations" as defined in section 129(a). 

These investigations provide a drug product to small numbers of human participants with a serious disease, injury, or condition who are not satisfactorily treated by alternative forms of therapy. The use of the

unapproved drug is primarily intended to provide treatment to the participants rather than to assess its risks or effectiveness, although information relevant to such an assessment may be derived during the investigation. These investigations have frequently been characterized as "compassionate" or "therapeutic" investigations because they involve the use of an investigational drug under circumstances in which benefit to the patient is far more important than obtaining scientific knowledge. 

Two situations characterize the most common drug treatment investigations. (1) A drug product which is being worked up for commercial marketing under a drug development investigation is found to have sufficient evidence of effectiveness that patients with the condition for which the drug product is intended, who cannot be treated by any other available therapy, have a reasonable possibility of being successfully treated with the drug product. At this point, however, adequate and well-controlled studies have not been completed to justify a decision that the drug can be approved for marketing under a monograph. Often, scientists and physicians become aware of the existence of the drug product, and the manufacturer or the FDA will receive inquiries from individual physicians seeking to gain access to the drug in order to attempt to treat individual patients. The drug treatment investigation allows these patients to receive the investigational drug prior to official approval, subject to the normal requirements for a clinical investigation. Included in these requirements is that informed consent be obtained for each patient and that any information relevant to the risks or effectiveness of the drug be reported so that it can be considered in reaching a decision on approval of a monograph for the drug. (2) A drug entity has been denied approval for commercial marketing, or has been withdrawn from the marketplace, because of a conclusion that its risks outweigh its benefits. Nevertheless, there may remain a very small

group of patients to whom these risks may be acceptable because of unique benefits provided to them by the drug. Under the Drug Regulation 

Reform Act, this situation could be handled in either of two ways: (a) under the special requirements for distribution and dispensing of drug products set forth in section 108(e), or (b) as a drug treatment investigation. Either system would permit the limited number of patients to receive the drug while minimizing the risks to the rest of society. 

The decision on which mechanism to use would depend on the facts of each particular situation.

It should be clear that the regulatory objectives, and the controls to attain these objectives, are different for the types of investigation. 

A major defect in the current law is that all of these investigations are treated under the same set of rules. The Drug Regulation Reform Act cures this defect and provides the Secretary with appropriate tools to attain the regulatory goals sought with respect to each type of investigation.

Another major reform in the law pertaining to the investigational use of drugs is the introduction of informal and formal mechanisms to review regulatory decisions made by the Secretary in this area. A frequent criticism of the present system is that sponsors of clinical investigations and other scientists are not able to obtain a fair and impartial evaluation of differences that may exist between them and the individual Department employees assigned to review and monitor their investigations. In recent years the FDA has taken several steps to provide opportunities for reconsideration, and it is by no means clear how valid the criticisms are today. Nevertheless, it is true that the 

FD&C Act provides no legal recourse for resolution of such conficts, and a United States Court of Appeals has held in effect that regulatory

$ 125 decisions regarding clinical investigations are not subject to judicial review. Turkel v. Food and Drug Administration, 334 F.2d 884 (6th Cir. 

1964). The Secretary believes that this rule should be changed.

Section 125. Objectives in Regulating the Investigational Use of Drug Products. This section sets forth the objectives of regulation of clinical investigations involving drugs. It recognizes the conflicts in policies that our society seeks to pursue, and provides both guidance and mandates to the Secretary in resolving these conflicts.

Subsection (a) states that, in all investigations, two objectives are to be sought. First and foremost, the Secretary is to protect the rights and health of humans who participate in clincial investigations. Second, to the extent consistent with the first objective, the Secretary is to avoid inter ference with the discovery and development of new drug products intended to benefit and promote public health and, to that end, the Secretary is to facilitate opportunities for scientific investigators to pursue such discoveries and development. language recognizes the tension existing between protecting research subjects and providing freedom for investigators. 

Both of these goals are in the public interest and, should be pursued simultaneously where possible. Where, however, there is conflict between these two objectives, subsection (a) makes it clear that protection of the rights and health of human subjects of research is paramount.

Recently, inspections by the FDA and hearings conducted before the Subcommittee on Health and Scientific Research of the Senate Committee on Human Resources have disclosed problems in the quality, reliability, and validity of scientific data submitted to the FDA and to other Federal regulatory agencies in support of applications for permission to market regulated products. Although fraud is an occasional concern, more serious is the discovery of simple negligence on the part of a number of toxicology testing laboratories, clinical investigators, sponsors of clinical investigations, institutional review boards, and others participating in the process of drug development. The Congress recently approved a substantial increase

Consequently, it is appropriate to recognize in section 125 (b)(1) that the quality, reliability, and validity of scientific data are of major significance in carrying out the objectives of regulation regarding drug development investigations.

The objective set forth in section 125(b) (2) also reflects reflects a certain tension between two aspects of the Secretary's responsibilities under the law. First, the Secretary must be able to evaluate--critically, fairly, and objectively-scientific investigations that are submitted in order to obtain permission to market drug products. The Secretary must be free to reject studies that are inadequate, unreliable, or fail to demonstrate the effectiveness, or to assess the risks, of a drug product. At the same time, review of proposed studies before they are commenced may prevent mistakes, may assure the adequacy of the study, and may otherwise increase the likelihood that completion of the study will generate the kind of data which the Secretary needs to make a final determination regarding the drug. This prior review can preclude unnecessary exposure of human participants to risks in investigations which will ultimately have no scientific or regulatory value; it can also reduce the time required to obtain the valid scientific evidence to make a decision that a drug is or is not able to be approved. Thus, prior review of investigations can have significant benefits to the public health, as well as to the protection of rights and safety of research subjects. 

Nevertheless, such prior review does create the potential for

Subsection (b) (2) recognizes this conflict and states that, to the extent that the Secretary's objectivity can be maintained while engaging in prior review, such review is appropriate; but where a conflict arises, objectivity must take precedence.

Section 126. General Requirements. Section 126 establishes the general principles regarding the use of a drug product which is not licensed under subpart 4 for purposes of conducting a clinical investigation involving humans.

Paragraph (1) of subsection (a) provides that any person who intends to conduct a clinical investigation in which an unlicensed drug product will be dispensed to a human shall obtain a registration from the Secretary before permitting the drug product to be so dispensed. 

Paragraph (2) of the same subsection requires any person who intends to manufacture, distribute, or import into the United States an unlicensed drug product for purposes of clinical investigation shall either obtain a registration or assure that the person to whom the drug product will be distributed has obtained such a registration, before commencing such a distribution of the drug product. 

Paragraph (3) provides that any person who intends to dispense such a drug product shall either obtain a registration or be included as an investigator under a registration obtained

§ 126 by another person before commencing such dispensing. 

Thus, the distribution and dispensing of nonlicensed drug products may not lawfully commence until either the manufacturer or distributor of the drug product, or the investigator actually conducting the study, or some third person (e.g., a research foundation), has obtained a registration under subpart 5. This system is designed so that the authority to obtain a registration is not vested exclusively in any one person. 

While the vast majority of drug products which under current law are being researched for ultimate marketing purposes are under the sponsorship of pharmaceutical manufacturers, approximately one half of all drug investigations are under the sponsorship of independent clinical investigators.

Subsection (b) excludes from the requirements of registration the manufacture, import, export, or distribution of substances which the handler neither intends to be, nor has any reason to know will be, used in a human or dispensed to a human. In the area of preclinical investigations, the Secretary, through the FDA, has recently undertaken a program to assure the quality, reliability, and validity of toxicology and other non-clinical laboratory studies submitted to the FDA in support of a request to test or market drugs or other regulated products. Section 127. Registration Requirements for Drug Innovation Investigations. Section 127 contains regulatory requirements and procedures pertaining to drug innovation investigations. Because these investigations involve small numbers of human participants and because their purpose is limited to examining clininal pharmacology, to assessing preliminarily the risks or effectiveness of drugs, or to studying biological mechanisms in humans, the objectives of regulation may also be limited. As stated under section 125(a), these objectives are (1) to protect the rights and health of humans who participate in such an investigation and (2) to the extent consistent with that purpose, to avoid interfering with the discovery and development of new drug products intended to benefit and promote the public health, and to that end to facilitate the opportunities for scientific investigators in their pursuit of that purpose. To achieve these objectives, section 127 narrows the focus of the Secretary's regulatory review to those aspects of the investigation which may adversely affect the health or the rights of the participants.

S 94

This narrowing of focus reflects the desirability of removing regulatory impediments to scientific creativity that may currently exist due to the failure of the current FD&C Act to distinguish among the various categories of clinical investigations. Under present practice drug innovation investigations are reviewed not only for protection of subjects, but also for the adequacy of scientific design of the studies, so that the results of the investigations will be of maximum benefit to the Secretary in reaching decisions on the approvability of drugs. Because perhaps 90% of all drug innovation investigations will not lead to petitions for drug monographs, the investment of resources, both by the Department and by the research community, in assuring the best possible scientific design in these investigations is not justified. Furthermore, delays and other costs that may be imposed upon sponsors and investigators in drug innovation investigations are perceived by many to discourage such investigations.

Consequently, section 127 has been prepared with the objective of reducing regulation over technical aspects of the science of drug innovation investigations, without compromising the protection of the rights and safety of human subjects. To the extent that this goal is accomplished, clinical research in the United States may become less expensive, more attractive to creative scientific minds, and ultimately more productive of knowledge about diseases and other conditions affecting the human body and of drugs that may aid in preventing or treating these diseases.

Subpart 8 Homeopathic Drug Entities and Drug Products 

This subpart provides a special set of requirements for the unique type of drugs used in homeopathic medicine. The theory of drug therapy may be divided into two schools of thought: allopathic and homeopathic. 

The first teaches that drugs provide their benefits through pharmacological action in the body; that is, that the drug produces a biochemical change that ultimately manifests itself in the therapeutic, preventive, or curative effect sought. Homeopathic medicine, on the other hand, is premised on the theory that drugs also operate at dosages well below those needed to produce a pharmacological effect. The mere presence of the drug is sufficient to disturb the natural balance (or homeostasis) of the body, which will respond on its own to restore the balance. 

Homeopathy is analogous to (but in fact quite distinct from) the theory of immunization.

Although homeopathy was a very important element of medical care in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, it declined in popularity and practice following the pharmaceutical developments of the last 35 years. It is estimated that only 2-3% of all practicing physicians in the U.S. today apply homeopathic principles on a regular basis. In other

S 144 1 nations, however, homeopathy continues to be widely practiced. 

Homeopathic drugs present a variety of special questions regarding regulatory objectives and controls. The doses that are administered are extraordinarily minute, measured in amounts smaller than micrograms. 

The compounding is done by homeopathic pharmacists according to individual prescriptions and the Homeopathic Pharmacopeia (the homeopathic counterpart of the United States Pharmacopeia). The number of individual "drug entities" and "drug products" is exceptionally large, easily exceeding the total number for allopathic medicine. Products are rarely labeled as to their intended purposes; instead, the the homeopathic effects are described in extensive homeopathic literature. 

The consequence of these facts is that homeopathic drugs have never been fully subjected to the requirements of the current FD&C Act, and should be distinguished from allopathic drugs under the Drug Regulatory Reform Act. Any other approach would probably not be costeffective in protecting the public health, given the present role of homeopathic medicine in the United States.

Section 144. General Requirements. Under subsection (a), any person who intends to manufacture or import a homeopathic drug must comply with the requirements of subpart 3 of part B 

("General Requirements for Drug Entities and Drug Products") and subpart 8. 

Subsection (b) exempts homeopathic drugs that comply with the requirements in subsection (a) from the monograph and product license, registration, and export provisions of part B, and the information labeling provisions of part C, of

S 124 

the Drug Regulation Reform Act. 

Subsection (c) authorizes the Secretary to revoke an exemption under subsection (b) for any homeopathic drug if the Secretary determines that the homeopathic drug presents a risk of illness or injury to persons using such drug entity or drug product, either from adverse effects or as a result of a lack of effectiveness. In such a case, the monograph requirements and standards would apply. Thus, no quack medicine could be promoted under the homeopathic drug provisions.

Section 162. Criminal Penalties. This section establishes the criminal penalties for the commission of acts prohibited by section 161.

Subsection (a) carries forward the misdemeanor provisions of section 303(a) of current law, with three exceptions. First, subsection (a) substitutes a standard of negligence for the standard of strict liability applicable under current law. Thus, with respect to violations concerning human drugs, this subsection would overrule the principle of strict criminal liability set forth in United States v. Park, 421 U.S. 658 (1975). Second, the subsection would increase the maximum fine for a misdemeanor from $1,000 for both individuals and business entities to $25,000 for individuals and $50,000 for business entities. These higher maximum penalties are appropriate in view of the higher standard of criminal liability, the risks to the public from violations of the statute (and the consequent seriousness of violations), and inflation. Third, the subsection exempts from misdemeanor penalties various non-fraudulent violations committed by practitioners, scientific investigators, and phar-

$ 150 macists. The exempted violations could be the basis for imposition of a civil penalty under section 163 and/or an administrative disqualification under section 166. Practitioners, scientific investigators and pharmacists would be liable criminally for submission of false statements (section 161(4)) and for disclosure of confidential information obtained under the statute (section 161(6)).

Subsection (b) carries forward the felony provisions of section 303(b) of current law, with two exceptions. The exemption for nonfraudulent violations by practitioners, scientific investigators, and pharmacists applies to the felony provisions as well as to the misdemeanor provisions. 

However, practitioners, scientific investigators and pharmacists would commit a felony if they knowingly or willfully submitted a false statement to the Secretary or disclosed confidential information obtained under the statute. Second, the maximum fine is raised from $10,000 for both individuals and business entities to $50,000 for individuals and $1,000,000 for business entities. These higher maximum penalties are appropriate in view of the fact that they would be imposed only on an individual or firm that is a repeat offender or a knowing and willfull violator, that such violations may present serious risks to the public, and that the deterrent value of a maximum fine has been eroded over time by inflation. Under subsection (b), it is a felony either to violate the statute knowingly or willfully, or to commit a prohibited act as a result of negligence after a previous conviction for a negligent violation of the law (or after a conviction under the current FD&C Act).

This section authorizes the Secretary to issue subpenas for the testimony of witnesses and the production of documents. Many other federal agencies have such authority-. including the NLRB, the SEC, the ICC, the Federal Energy Regulatory

Commission (formerly the FPC), the Small Business Administration, the Department of Housing and Urban Development, the Department of Commerce, and the Department of Labor. In particular, the other health regulatory agencies have 

EPA, CPSC, and OSHA subpena power to enforce statutes which they administer (e.g., the Toxic Substances Control Act; the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act; the Noise Control Act of 1972; The

Consumer Product Safety Act; and The Occupational Safety and 

Health Act). In the area of drug regulation, both the Drug Enforce ment Administration and the Federal Trade Commission have subpena power. The lack of subpena authority for the Secretary to enforce the drug statutes for which the Department is responsible is anomalous.

The provisions of section 174 are modeled after those in other federal statutes authorizing administrative subpenas. An adminis trative subpena may be issued by the Secretary, but may be enforce only by a court. Therefore, a party who wishes to challenge the appropriateness or scope of a subpena has an adequate and timel judicial remedy.

The Secretary needs subpena authority in order to conduc adequate investigations of possible violations of the law. Becaus most violations of the Drug Regulation Reform Act are misdemeano rather than felonies, the Secretary generally does not have the a of a grand jury in conducting an investigation. Some types

S 175 violations do not carry any criminal penalties. Possible violations may be investigated by FDA inspectors, but the scope of their investigations is generally limited by the scope of the Secretary's inspection authority. In some cases the Secretary is unable to pursue an investigation to a successful conclusion due to lack of authority to compel witnesses to testify and to subpena documents. 

The need for subpena power is greatly increased by the change in the standard of misdemeanor liability under section 162(a). For a misdemeanor case to succeed, the Secretary will have to develop admissible evidence not only that a violation occurred, but also that the defendant caused the violation through negligence. To meet that higher standard of proof, the Secretary will need subpena authority.

Subsection (j) authorizes the issuance and execution of inspection warrants. Such warrants first gained attention as a result of the Supreme Court's decisions in Camara v. 

Municipal Court, 387 U.S. 53 (1967) and See v. Seattle, 387

U.S. 541 (1967). The first case involved a criminal prosecution for violating a municipal housing code by refusing to permit inspection, without warrant, of a dwelling unit in a building. The second case involved a conviction for refusing to permit a city fire department inspector to enter and inspect, without a warrant, a locked commercial warehouse. The 

Court held that in both instances a warrant was constitutionally required, but that probable cause for a warrant should be determined in the light of a "flexible standard of reasonableness that takes into account the public need for effective enforcement of the particular regulation involved." The 

Supreme Court later limited the requirement for such warrants in United States v. Biswell, 406 U.S. 311 (1972), in a case upholding a warrantless inspection of a gun dealer by Federal firearms inspector under a regulatory statute which made it a criminal offense to refuse to permit an inspection of a regulated premises. The Supreme Court said that "where, as here, regulatory inspections further urgent federal interest, and the possibilities of abuse and threat of privacy are

$ 175 not of impressive dimensions, the inspection may proceed without a warrant where specifically authorized by statute.' 

As sections 161 and 175 are now drafted, the inspection of a regulated premises without a warrant is specifically authorized. Therefore, under existing Supreme Court decisions, subsection (j) is not necessary, and paragraph (1) is intended to make this clear. Nevertheless, two factors lead to its inclusion in the Drug Regulation Reform Act. First, the 

Department frequently obtains inspection warrants now, in lieu of bringing criminal charges against persons skeptical of the Department's authority to conduct warrantless inspections under the FD&C Act. Experience has shown, however, that U.S. magistrates and Federal judges are occasionally reluctant to issue inspection warrants without an explicit statutory authorization. Secondly, the issue of warrantless administrative searches is currently pending before the Supreme Court in Marshall v. Barlow's, Inc., No. 76-1143. It is, of course, possible that the Biswell standards will be modified by the Court's decision in Barlow's. Consequently, the provision of section 175(j)(1)(c) is written to accomodate future interpre tations of the Fourth Amendment of the Constitution. 

Paragraph (1) also makes it clear that an inspection warrant may be sought and obtained by the Secretary even thought not legally required, and that the execution of an inspection warrant will not preclude administrative or judicial proceedings for a refusal to permit a lawful warrantless inspection.

S S 177 172 Paragraph (2) authorizes issuance of such such warrants by

Federal judges and U.S. magistrates. These warrants may be issued only within the territorial jurisidction of these officials and upon a sworn application showing probable cause and only for the purpose of conducting inspections of regulated premises as authorized by the statute. The paragraph defines "probable cause" as "a valid public interest in the enforcement of this title, or regulations thereunder, sufficient to justify inspection in the circumstances specified in the application." This language accords with the Supreme Court decisions cited above. Paragraphs (3), (4), and (5) establish procedures for the issuance, execution, and return of an inspection warrant.

The Drug Amendments of 1962 directed the Secretary to review all drug products covered by new drug applications

$ 194 previously approved under the FD&C Act (i.e., between 1938 and 1962) and to withdraw approval for any which were not shown to meet the effectiveness requirements added in 1962. The 

Secretary contracted with the National Academy of Sciences and National Research Council (NAS/NRC) to conduct the initial review. Manufacturers of those products for which the NAS/NRC did not find substantial evidence of effectiveness were given an opportunity to gather and submit additional evidence of effectiveness to the Secretary. These drugs, with a very few exceptions (described in section 192(b) (2)) have been rereviewed on the basis of the new submissions by FDA staff. 

Those which were still lacking substantial evidence of effectiveness were provided a notice of opportunity for a hearing on proposals to withdraw approval of the new drug applications covering them. Of the many requests for hearings that were submitted, approximately 200 are still pending. Even the most optimistic attorneys doubt that these requests can be processed and hearings held (or legally sufficient denials of hearings prepared) in less than four years.

Thus, without a significant change in the resources to complete this project or in the legal procedures applicable to the project, it is unlikely that the Drug Amendments of 1962 will be fully implemented by 1982. It should be emphasized that none of these hearings now appears to involve a drug product that poses a serious risk of illness or injury to patients. But each drug has been twice evaluated for effectiveness and twice found wanting. Section 195. 

Continuation of Regulations. This section provides for the continuation of existing administrative regulations which are in effect on the day preceding enactment of the Drug Regulation Reform Act, unless modified, modified, superseded, or repealed by the Secretary.

Section 196. 

Savings Provisions. This section provides that nothing in the Drug Regulation Reform Act, shall in any way affect (1) the laws regulating drugs for use in animals other than humans, (2) the laws relating to controlled substances, or (3) the antitrust laws. It also preserves the authority of the Secretary of the Treasury under the customs and related laws and the Attorney General under the controlled substances statutes. (A provision affirming the authority of the Attorney General and the Federal Trade Commission under the antitrust statutes is contained in section 185.) 

Section 197. Application of State Law. This section governs regulation of the labeling and packaging of drugs for human use by 

States and localities. Because the Drug Regulation Reform Act extends Federal jurisdiction to drugs moving solely in intrastate commerce, and because interstate commerce would be unduly burdened by a substantial number of differing State and local requirements

S 201 applicable to the packaging or labeling of drug products, the relation of State and local regulation to Federal regulation should be clarified.

Subsection (a) provides that a) provides that no State or no State or locality may establish or continue in effect any requirement with respect to the packaging or labeling of a drug product which is different from, or in addition to, any requirement made applicable to such drug product under the proposed legislation. 

Because there are some situations in which regulation of the packaging or labeling of drug products by States and localities would constitute a useful supplement to Federal regulation, subsection (b) authorizes a State or locality to petition the Secretary for exemptions from the general prohibition on non-Federal regulation under subsection (a). 

Under this provision, the Secretary may authorize imposition of a State requirement on a drug if the Secretary finds (1) that the requirement is more stringent than a requirement under the Federal law, or (2) that it is required by compelling local conditions and compliance with the requirement would not cause the drug to be in violation of a requirement under the new statute. This provision is based upon section 521 of the FD&C Act regarding medical devices.

Section 1801 establishes the Center in the Department of

Section 1802 (a) authorizes the Center to conduct and support research in clinical pharmacology and clinical pharmacy, including investigations (1) of the safety and effectiveness of existing and new uses of drug products, (2) for the development of drug products or diseases and other conditions of low incidence, (3) of drug products of special significance or with respect to which there is substantial controversy as to safety and effectiveness and for which there have been either no or minimal animal investigations, and (4) otherwise to facilitate breakthroughs in research on drug products.

Section 1802 (b) directs the Center to conduct an ongoing review and analysis of the use of drug products in the United 

States and to prepare an annual Drug Experience Assessment Report for the Secretary.

Section 1802 (c) directs the Center to facilitate, and authorizes it to support, analysis of the consequences of State and Federal regulation of drugs, public and private research activities and practices regarding drug products, and practices of the pharmaceutical industry and health professionals respecting drug products.

This section directs the Secretary to arrange, by contract, for an independent evalu ation of Title I of the Drug Regulation Reform Act of 1978.
