CHAPTER I THE NEED FOR FOR MEASUREMENT 

Educational and Vocational Guidance has been attacked and criticized by many public spirited men and women as being just another fad. The hard-boiled business man with a practical turn of mind -and many a school and social agency executive may be included in the class of hard-boiled ind practical wants to be shown conclusive proofs that guidance is worth while before he is ready to support it. 

He wants to see concreto results before he invests any sum of money in it. Guidance must prove its own worth before its proponents can expect public support. 

Is there any justification for calling guidance a fad? Are there any proofs that vocational guidance scientifically carried out can be made to pay large dividends? Can the professional vocational guidance men prove to industry and to society as a whole that their program is practical, effective, and above all that it brings results? 

Can they prove to the business man that vocational guidance reduces labor turnover, makes for happier and more contented workers, increases efficiency, and reduces cost of production? In short, can a vocational guidance director prove that his work is bearing worthy results?

There have been many times when even those very men and women engaged in the practice of guidance have questioned the value and ultimate results of thoir efforts. Many who are truly honest with themsolves have at some time or other questioned the wholo guidance movoment. They have been at a loss to prove not only to others, but to themselves, that their work was truly necessary and worth-while. 

A business man can measure the effectiveness of a new employce, a new machine, or a new dovice by the amount of work produced. 

A teacher can perhaps measure the success of hor offorts by examining the pupils at the end of the year in order to find out how much of tho factual matter she had given them they have retained. But how can a vocational guidance counselor measure the results of his labors? 

He is not dealing with quantity production; he is not 2

dealing with machines and dollars and cents; he is not dealing with tangible objects. He is dealing with that evasive, intangible, and immeasurable, though very important something called personality. 

He is dealing with abilities and their possible development, with character, behavior, and social conduct. He is dealing with every side of an individual, with the enfolding of the whole human being. 

He is dealing with shaping the future of boys and girls, and with the destiny and happiness of men and women. 

How, then, can the vocational guidance man measure the success of his work? The task seems almost impossible. The difficulties are innumerable. In spite of all the obstacles that seem to be in the way of measurements, the vocational counselor should, and eventually must, submit his work to the measuring rod, and thus determine whether his program is producing enough results to warrant continuance and expansion.

The need of actual measurements in the field of guidance is obvious to all concerned. As we have stated before, the difficulty has been and still is, and probably will remain to be for many years to come, that guidance is not dealing with tangible objects. 

It is very difficult indeed to measure the effect of certain influences on human beings. It is not an easy task to measure the influence that an individual, a group, or the community as a whole has on a growing boy or girl, or on a man or woman. How can one tell whether it was the advice of the vocational counselor that finally led a boy to make certain choices in life, to plan certain things, and finally to achieve a certain goal? how can one measure human behavior with any degree of accuracy? Human beings are not stable objects. They do not always react as they are expected to. Their conduct at times is unexplainable. Their moods, emotions, and feelings, often change without any apparent reason. Humans are complex and complicated organisms not easily subjected to microscopic examination. What, then, can be done to evaluate a guidance program?

In spite of all these seemingly baffling difficulties, an attempt must be made at measuring and evaluation. Even though it may be impossible to put vocational guidance through a microscopic analysis, yet there are certain things that can be done. For example, it is possible to get a general impression of the effect of a guidance program by noting the success or failure of those individuals who have partaken of such a program. The status of those individuals who have been put through the guidance process can be recorded both before and after guidance has been in operation, and any differences noted. 

It is even possible to compare these "guided" individuals with others of identical abilities, characteristics, and other circumstances both before and after they have been guided, and note if there are any marked differences in their later years. In other words, group control studies can be conducted, and undoubtedly results of some kind can be obtained.

Those of us who are either directly or indirectly engaged in the field of guidance are convinced that such work is indispensable. Yet, how many of us could honestly prove, with dollars and cents, that the money and time spent in this field brings enough returns to justify our convictions? The present writer confesses that he has spent many waking hours pondering the question of whether guidance generally speaking is truly worth while. Of course we all have an occasional outstanding success to which we point with pride, but is our work successful as a whole? 

To quiet these dubious feelings, the writer decided to put our past efforts in this field under the measuring-rod. For the good of our own souls, we wanted to see, if possible, in black and white, whether the time and effort spent in guiding boys had been in vain, or whether in the end something is accomplished by conscientious guidance.

CHAPTER II PLANS FOR THE STUDY 

Though the thought of submitting guidance to the measuringrod had been in the writer's mind for some time, economic and social conditions of the past several years made it imperative for all of us to evaluate or re-evaluate our work. The need for occupational readjustment following the revival and expansion of industrial and commercial enterprises in 1935 and 1936, together with the increased interest manifested everywhere in the field of educational and vocational guidanco, finally convinced us to undertako such a project. 

Accordingly, in the early summor of 1936 a decision was roached to attempt a study that would at least give an indication of the true valuo of the guidance program as carried on in the Worcestor Boys' Club. Those of us who had been close to the Guidance Department could see that the efforts made by the staff were not all in vain. 

But even we could not feel entirely certain of our ground. 

Consequently we set ourselves to work out a plan to check the effectiveness of our program.

At first it was thought advisable to take a random but representative sample of the cases handled by the Department, study these cases intensely and extensively, following them for a period of several years in order to find out what became of the boys thus aided. In analyzing this plan, however, it soon was apparent that the results, whatever they might be, would prove little or nothing. For, supposing that most of the cases followed up and carefully examined proved to be successful, how could one toll whether their success, if they had boon successful, was due to the aid given by the Guidance Department? After a great deal of consideration it was finally agrood to attempt a so-called groupcontrol study. In other words, the progress made by a group of boys who had registered for guidanco and had boon put through the various stops of the guidance program would be compared with the progress made by an identical group of boys who had not boon served by the Guidance Department. 

1. OBJECTIVES 

The plan finally evolved was to examine the guidance 5

program in detail, to test it in the light of results obtained, to determine whether or not it was effective, and to find out what phases of it needed revision and roorganization. Tho study was to be an attempt at self-evaluation for our own satisfaction and for our own self-direction. It was hopod that even if the study should show that our program was falling far short of its aim, at least wo would have some indication as to whore the faults lay, and thus enable us to cradicate them. In short, we wanted to know, for our own peace of mind, whether or not we were doing our job right, and if not how we could improve the program. 2.

12. ECONOMIC STATUS OF FAMILIES 

A factor which appears to play a very important role in the up-bringing and development of children is the economic status of the family. In a study to measure the effects and results of educational and vocational guidance the economic status of the family is indeed of major importance. Explanations here are unnecessary, for all are acquainted with the implications. In the present study, real efforts were made to determine the economic standing of the family of every one of the subjects. Every home was investigated, public and private charity records examined, data from various sources analyzed, and every home was then rated either "Comfortable", "Marginal", or "Dependent."(1)

Rated according to the above classification the families of the two groups appeared as follows: (1) This classification was adopted, with some modifications, from that used by Glueck and Glueck in "One Thousand Juvenile Delinquents."

A family was rated marginal "if it lived on daily earnings but accumulated little or nothing, being usually on the verge of dependency. Here were included instances in which temporary aid was resorted to once or twice in order to tide over a critical situation. 

Aid may have been given for a few days or even a month, and with this little assistance the family was able to manage its own problems." 

In this group were included those who had been aided at least once previous to 1930, and at most four different times since then.

From the above table it is seen that the economic status of the two groups was relatively the same. The proportion of boys rated as "comfortable" was considerably higher in Group N than in Group A. 

The reverse was true in the "marginal" rating. Both groups had about an equal share of "dependents". Group N, therefore, was no more handicapped economically than Group A. If anything, it was a little better off.

It is thus seen that the boys who had left school prior to December 1st, 1936, were, at the beginning of the experiment, quite comparable in school grades reached, age, I.Q., and economic status. 

Yet the boys from Group A remained in school longer than those from Group N. The median grades completed was almost two years higher in the former group. Since the two groups were comparable in 1931, what caused the boys from Group A to excel those from Group N?

The immediate assumption would be that the advice and guidance given to the boys from Group A may have spurred them on; it may have given them a higher goal toward which to strive. It may be that the boys in Group N lacked the spark to kindle their enthusiasm and ambition. It may be that they failed to envision their future in the proper perspective. It may be that they met with problems that seemed to baffle them, and which they could not solve without outside assistance. They may have had the wrong attitude toward life, work, and education. Whatever may have happoned, the fact remains that they did not attain as high a degree of formal education as the boys from Group A. It is promature, however, to draw any conclusions at this point. e. Boys who left school in the middle of a term. C. BEHAVIOR AND DELINQUENCY 

As an incidental but nevertheless important part of our investigation, we checked the records of the boys in the two groups to ascertain if any of them had ever had any kind of court record. 

The problem of juvenile delinquency to-day seems to be uppermost in the minds of many people in the field of social work. 

A number of studies have been made to determine the causes, and to find remedies for it. The writer has, for some time, been interested in this aspect of juvenile behavior, and therefore it was natural that he should touch on this matter in this study. Most people would probably agree that it is not going too far afield to consider the behavior of youngsters in any study attempting to evaluate a guidance program. For, in the final analysis, should not guidance be interested in the whole individual, in the sum total of his personality, rather than in any single phase of it? Guidance to be really effective must consider the entire make-up of the individual as well as his environment, and all the problems confronting him. 

The procedure followed in this part of the study was entirely different from that followed in the other phases of it. In the first place, whereas in the other parts of the investigations the boy himself played a large role, in this part he did not even know that his record of conduct was being examined. Then also, the only thing considered in this part was the court record. 

The procedure adopted was as follows. The juvenile and adult files of the court were examined to see if any of these boys had committed any unsocial acts during the period from September 1st, 1931, to December 1st, 1936. (1) (Both the juvenile and adult files had to be (1) It is well to remember that no boy from either group had had any delinquency record of any sort prior to September 1st, 1931.

examined because some of the boys were no longer juveniles in 1936.) 

These records disclosed whether a boy had been before the court or the probation officers, and what offense, if any, was charged against his name.

This investigation showed that one boy from Group A, and 11 from Group N had been officially brought before a juvenile court judge for unsocial acts.

A further examination of those records revealed the fact that the one boy from Group A had had only one offense charged to him in the summer of 1933, and apparently since then had been conforming with the rules of society. Of the 11 boys from Group N, on the other hand, 6 had been recidivists. One of these six had been before court at seven different times, and on December 1st, 1936, was confined in the State Reformatory. Two boys had been before court four times, one three times, and two twice.

In drawing conclusions one should always guard against generalizations. It is dangerous to draw general conclusions from data pertaining to human beings, their behavior, and their development. 

One cannot be always certain which factors played the most important part in shaping the destiny and unfolding the lives of men and women. 

The task of evaluating the importance of forces played upon the development of character, personality, and success of young people becomes still more difficult. The same can bo said concerning the moc.surement of programs in the field of guidance. How can one determine for a certainty just what was the main influence that diverted a boy's interest and attention from one subject to another, from one school to another, from one occupation to another? How does one know for sure what it was that awoke a boy's desires in certain things, and fired his ambition toward some unpremeditated goal? What are the inner forces that drive one youngster on, and yet leave another of pparently identical montal and physical powers behind both in school and in work? Some psychologists prefer to call these forces "drives".

Many of the boys who were accepted for guidance in the fall of 1931

were taken from a list resulting from the answers to the question: "Do you wish some help in planning your futuro?" This question was answered by all the boys who took part in any educational activity. 

Those that answered yes were later invited to a private conference whence the guidance process began, and the others were left alone. 

In 1931 the Guidance Department was so new that very few boys knew anything about it or even that it existed, and fewer understood what it meant. They had never heard of such a thing before; consequently, they could not have chosen to undertake and follow a guidance program deliberately. A number of boys answered the question, "Do you wish some help in planning your future", "Yes" or "No" without fully understanding the meaning of the question. Consequently, it is safe to assume that the boys who were selected as Group A were really no more interested in themselves and their future than the boys chosen as Group N. 

Still another point might be raised. It might be argued that for a scientifically carried out group-control study, groups should be selected before the experiment is begun. In this particular case, therefore, the two groups should have been selected in 1931, and then one group should have been made to take guidance while the other was left to shift for itself. The writer admits the soundness of this argument. Perhaps it would have been better had our two groups been selected in 1931. But at that time we had no idea of undertaking such a project. An individual will not and cannot be forced to take some one else's advice. Guidance cannot be successful until and unless it is voluntary. It must be sought willingly. All that we can do is to make our program attractive so that people, boys and girls, men and women, will want to partake of it.

It might be well to say a few words here concerning this matter of delinquency and behavior. It should be kept in mind that the Guidance Department of the Boys' Club is primarily concerned with the educational and vocational problems of the boys. As has been said before, however, these problems cannot be divorced from others arising out of thwarted personalities. But as a rule, very little attention is given to the question of behavior. We go on the assumption that a boy whose heart and soul are wrapped around a certain ideal, a certain ambition or goal, has no time to think of mischievous acts. His mental and physical energies are employed toward the fulfilment of his ambition, rather than toward destructive ends. 

Perhaps it is going a little too far afield to say that a program of educational and vocational guidance reduces delinquency. 

Perhaps the results of our investigations have but very little value because of the samll number of cases involved. Perhaps it was merely chance that more of the non-advised boys fell prey to temptations and became delinquents. However it might be, the delinquency percentages of the two groups at least indicate that something can be done along the line of prevention. For, after all, very few will quarrel with the idea of setting before a boy a worthy and attainable goal, and then leading him to reach for it. If this is done properly, there need be few worries concerning that boy's behavior or social conduct.

On the whole, then, the boys from Group A seem to be at least a little ahead of the boys from Group N. Both groups were on the same level in 1981. Both groups had the same opportunities offered to them during the interval covered by this study. The only difference seems to be that the boys from Group A availed themselves of the services of the Guidance Department, and, consequently, had the benefit of continuous counsel and advice. It would appear, therefore, that this guidance had some effect on them.

At the very beginning of this study we asked ourselves several questions: Are there any proofs that guidance can be made to pay dividends? Can vocational guidance men prove that their program is practical, effective, and that it brings results? Can thoy prove that it reduces labor turnover, makes for happier and more contended workers, increases efficiency and reduces cost of production? In short can a vocational guidance program show results? We are now ready to answer these questions; and even though our proofs are rather meager, yet we at least have certain evidences which lead us to make our answers affirm ative.

How have we shown that the guidance program as carried on in the Worcester Boys' Club pays dividends? In the first place the boys who had taken advantage of the counsel offered were earning more money than those who had not. They were earning more for themselves, and probably more for their employers. The dividends, consequently, were larger for society as a whole. But if we could measure in dollars and cents the increased happiness of these young workers, and the value of worthy citizens against delinquents and criminals, would not these dividends grow still larger?

That the guidance program is practical and effective there seems to be no doubt. Did not those "advised" boys show better preparation for choosing their work? Did they not make greater use of their potential abilities, or aptitudes, both in school and outside? 

Guidance, furthermore, does and did reduce labor turnover, and, consequently, cost of production. Did not a much larger percentage of "advised" boys remain in one job longer than the "non-advised"?

Did not they make fewer changes in employment than the non-advised boys? And, what is more, a large number of them were and are still planning to remain in the same field for the rest of their working lives, because they like their jobs, and they see possibilities where they can use their aptitudes and abilities. 

There is no question but that our guidance program does show results, even though these results are yet infinitesimal. But the results are not yet complete. It is yet too early for the guidance given to show its full effect on the boys. Of one thing we are certain, however, it does show promise. Its probable future success can be seen and begun to be measured in dollars and cents. Above all, are not happiness and joy and personal satisfaction derived from achievements in school and at work more valuable than money itself? 

We shall never be able to measure these things, but they will always be treasured by human beings.

It is the author's belief that the difference in achievement between the two groups, even though quite perceivable to-day, will become still clearer and more distinct as the years roll on. It seems reasonable to assume that a boy with his eyes fixed on a worthy and attainable goal will do his utmost to reach that goal. If the desire to achieve that goal has been strongly imbedded in him, he will waste very little time and energy on uscless and undesirable activities. 

After all, was it not just such an objective that led the boys from 

Group A to strive forward? And might it not be possible that had those 11 boys from Group N had similar objectives before their eyes, they would have not fallen into delinquency? 

If guidance can spur a boy on; if systematic and organized guidance can help a boy recognize his potentialities and make use of them to the fullest extent possible; if guidance can aid in extending the length of time that a boy can profitably remain in school; if it can aid youths in choosing and preparing for more suitable occupations;

if it can reduce labor turnover in industry and give the employers more stable and possibly more satisfactory workers; if it can aid in the prevention of delinquents and lead boys toward desirable and worthy citizenship; if guidance can do these things -- and apparently it can -- then it more than pays for itself. It brings large returns to the individual and to society. It is more than worthwhile and desirable. 

It is a necessity.
