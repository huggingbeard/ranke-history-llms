THE LOVE AFFAIRS OF ABRAHAM LINCOLN 

When Abraham Lincoln ventured forth with his own law firm, he chose William H. Herndon as his partner. In doing so, he unwittingly selected a man who was to become his personal historian, an amateurish but energetic biographer destined to wield a powerful influence on the voluminous literature which was to grow up about his name. Other contemporaries also recorded their impressions of the remarkable career which had unfolded in their midst but their conclusions, as well as those of the next two generations of Lincoln writers, carry the unmistakable Herndon imprint. 

In assuming the role of biographer, Mr. Herndon, who was ten years Lincoln's junior, was awarded authoritative standing on the strength of his years of close relationship with the many opportunities they afforded for observation. Claiming for himself a perfect understanding of the mind and motives of his famous associate, he declared, "I know Lincoln better than he knows himself . . . I had advantages over all other men in knowing the facts of Mr. Lincoln's life." 

Surviving his partner by more than a quarter of a century, Mr. 

Herndon set himself to the immense task of assembling what he considered pertinent evidence concerning Lincoln and to an interpretation of his every act. So numerous were the papers which he gathered that he boasted of them as "the most perfect of any living or dead man." Samuel Johnson's biography by Boswell alone might be an exception, he said. He came to bristle with impatience when the accuracy of his documents was questioned and was frequently in open rebellion against those who challenged his opinions. 

A part of Mr. Herndon's data is not now considered dependable; some of it may be dismissed as prejudicial and several contradictions are to be found among the statements he represented as being true. His legal training did not always prevent him from confusing fact and fiction. But he was correct in one particular. "Every important. fact of Mr. Lincoln shall be known, come what will," he predicted; "the reading world wants to know all events, facts, instances -the girls he 'hugged' and the like." The world has indeed shown an astonishing interest in the lonely figure who occupies one of the first ten places in history, and not the least of this curiosity concerns his relations with women. 

Of no part of his ambitious biographical project did Mr. Herndon assume so much authority, and in regard to no other did he make so many tragic mistakes, as he did when he attempted to record the sentimental side of his subject's life. 1

KATIE, CAROLINE AND POLLY 

Although Lincoln's boyhood days in Indiana have been diligently investigated, little of romantic interest has been uncovered. When 

Tom Lincoln brought his new wife to the motherless home there, Abraham gained two stepsisters, Elizabeth and Matilda Johnson, one a year older, the other three years younger than himself. There has never been a serious suggestion that he thought of either excepting as sisters. Both married young, Elizabeth at fourteen and Matilda at fifteen. It was probably fortunate that the boy who was in training to save the American Union did not become entangled in an early marriage. His observant stepmother reported, "He was not very fond of girls, as he seemed to me."

Certain feminine names of these Indiana years, however, have been connected sentimentally with his. The importance of these affairs has no doubt been exaggerated by the imaginative genius with which the subject has been pursued. The first girl, Ann Roby, better known as Katie, attended a short term of school with Abraham when both were about fifteen. Although their supposed sentimental affair has been embellished with many details, it is a matter of history that Ann, at eighteen, became the wife of Lincoln's chum, Allen Gentry.

Long afterward, Ann Gentry graphically described her tall, crudelooking schoolmate; "His skin was shriveled and yellow," she said; "he wore baggy, too short buckskin breeches, a linsey woolsey shirt. 

His shin-bone was sharp, blue and narrow." She mentioned other lasting impressions-he seemed to have unusual mental gifts, was an excellent speller and a writer of good essays. The conversational theme he chose on one of the few times they were alone together had not escaped her memory, it was of astronomy that he talked, not of love.

Caroline Meeker was next. This young lady was the niece and dependent of a Justice of the Peace whose Kentucky jurisdiction was opposite the Indiana shore where young Lincoln ran a ferry. When he was brought into the Squire's court charged with operating without a license, Caroline witnessed the skillful manner in which he managed his own acquittal. She spoke to him privately about the excellence of his argument and their brief romance was born. We are informed that he returned several times to the Squire's home, ostensibly to read the Statutes of Kentucky, which pleased the Squire, but also to see Caroline, which did not please him.

Tradition, for such it mostly is, further affirms that Lincoln c.-re took advantage of the authority granted to the finder of a red ear at a husking bee by kissing this reputedly pretty, brown-eyed girl w') 2

was of about his own age. But for him she was unattainable, even had he been matrimonially inclined, which he almost certainly was not. A more eligible suitor had a priority on the family approval. 

Although suggesting a legal career to the Indiana backwoodsman, the Squire made it clear that he wanted no lovemaking with Caroline. 

She apparently did not agree with her uncle, and it is believed that when her ferryman friend discontinued his visits, she waited expectantly and was even hopeful of word from him after his removal to Illinois many months later. If so, she waited in vain. 

There is a sorrowful sequel to this tale of the Kentucky Squire and his ward. She reluctantly married the family choice and died in childbirth on Christmas Day, 1835, when in her twenty-sixth year.

The first Illinois girl mentioned romantically with Abraham was Polly Warnick, christened Mary, daughter of Major William Warnick, sheriff of the county in which the Lincolns first settled. Here the 

Caroline Meeker circumstances occurred all over again. The railsplitter from Indiana had arrived too late with too little. Major Warnick was a man of means and influence. He hired the lanky newcomer to split a thousand rails but frowned upon sentimental notions which included Polly. She was soon married to a young man thought more desirable, especially from a pecuniary point of view. 

Abraham spent a month the following winter at the Warnick home where he studied the Major's worn copy of the Statutes of Illinois, and the Warnicks, it was said, came to have a favorable opinion of their guest. Local purveyors of folklore claimed later that Polly was present when the future statesman made his first political speech, that she followed his public career with great interest and announced her belief, if we may believe their account, that he would make his mark in the world. At any rate, she lived to see his martyrdom. 

THE ANN RUTLEDGE MYTH 

Although called one of the world's most famous romances, the popular account of the courtship of Abraham Lincoln and Ann Rutledge must be rejected by those who desire biographical truth. Mr. 

Herndon joyfully promoted this pleasing piece of fiction, calling it one of the "best episodes in Lincoln's life." He is largely responsible for the pathetic details with which the world has become familiar. "Mr. Lincoln loved Ann Rutledge to his death, no mistake," he wrote.

He presented this unsupported story as a true chapter in the inner life of his partner and friend.

Notwithstanding the successful exploitation of this romance and the spectacular efforts of the poets and fictionists, little is actually 3

known about such an affair. The myth was launched by Mr. Herndon in a lecture during the year after Lincoln's death. Lincoln probably never talked to him about Ann during their years of association and he ´had no firsthand knowledge of the events which he so glibly related. 

Accepting at face value a sentimental story he had picked up on one of his many excursions in search of biographical material, he eagerly supplemented it with a choice selection of gossip and folklore, and the heart-rending tale of passionate, everlasting love was evolved. 

There is good reason to believe that the resulting fable, to which Herndon gave the fullest publicity, gratified his wish to embarrass the widow of the man he had pledged to "set right before the world." 

The proven facts are few and simple. Ann Rutledge, pretty and unsophisticated, was eighteen when Lincoln, then twenty-two, wandered into her home village of New Salem, Illinois, from Major Warnick's bailiwick. Titian-haired Ann was to all reports an attractive maiden and masculine admiration was not lacking. She gave encouragement to an enterprising storekeeper, John McNamar, and when he left to visit his family in the East, she was committed to become his wife. Several months without a letter in reply to the many she had written is believed to have modified her resolution not to accept the attentions of other men. 

Lincoln stepped into this situation, but not, it is now known, as a contender for her hand. They had similar intellectual qualities and aspirations a fertile field for friendship. No admissable evidence exists that marriage was even considered. The tales of their reputed engagement originated thirty years later when aged witnesses obligingly supplied Mr. Herndon with useful details from faded memories, a process greatly facilitated by their natural desire to bask in the fame of a great name. Lawyer Herndon conveniently suspended the rules which govern the validity of evidence. 

Given in support of the romantic tale is the statement that Lincoln lived at the Rutledge Tavern when it was operated by Ann's parents, thereby having the benefit of close proximity to favor his love affair. The fact is that he never actually lived in the tavern and as the town covered only a few acres and had but a hundred inhabitants, close proximity was assured in any event. Another claim is that this romance began when Ann came to Postmaster Lincoln seeking letters from her absent lover. Lincoln did not, however, becom village postmaster until after she and her family moved to a farm awned by Mr. McNamar a few miles distant. 

Various accounts highlight Lincoln's constant attention to Ann when she became sick during the malarial epidemic which beset central

Illinois in the summer of 1835. Authentic information on this point is that he saw her infrequently during her illness, the last time two weeks before she died. He was engaged in surveying some miles away but had there been a betrothal, or even a close relationship, we may be sure that this would not have kept him from her bedside. Undoubtedly he was upset by her death, which occurred August 25th, much more so than he was by the passing of other friends who had succumbed to the common malady. Mr. Herndon, however, stoutly insisted that his mental sufferings were so great as to involve the question of his recovery. One biographer, copying these exaggerations, wrote that "he went out into the world to be forever after, in his heart, a broken, melancholy man."

This alleged romance is perhaps the greatest of the several Lincoln legends which continue to be publicized by literary sentimentalists who have not chosen to investigate its background. Intriguing versions have appeared from time to time in national magazines. Less than twenty years ago, the conservative Atlantic Monthly featured a series of letters supposedly written by New Salem residents of a century before, including a number from Lincoln to Ann. 

Although warned of the forgery, the letters were published, to the later embarrassment of the editor. 

Had Ann lived, she would in all likelihood have married John McNamar, who returned to New Salem shortly after her death. This prosperous young man had put the destitute Rutledge family under financial obligation to him and demure Ann was probably a part of the bargain. By her marriage to him, she would have redeemed the family from a pressing creditor and spent the rest of her life with a husband who came to be regarded as something of a skinflint. 

True or false, the American people seem unwilling to disbelieve the hauntingly beautiful account of their favorite president's "lost" love. Most of those who visit the rebuilt New Salem where he is honored as no other man has ever been, go to nearby Petersburg where Ann's pitiful remains, consisting of a few shreds of cloth and a button or two, were reinterred for commercial purposes fifty years after her death. There they read a touching bit of sentiment: I am 

Ann Rutledge who sleeps beneath these weeds, Beloved of Abraham Lincoln,

Wedded to him, not through union, But through separation. 

Bloom forever, O Republic, From the dust of my bosom."

Edgar Lee Masters wrote these mournful lines but they were prompted by William H. Herndon, who was the creator of Ann's commanding place in the Lincoln story.

"I'M GOING TO CATCH, TIE AND MARRY HER" 

The best answer to the claim that the death of Ann Rutledge had a permanent effect on Lincoln is that in little more than a year thereafter, he was giving earnest thought to taking a wife. Four carefully preserved letters make it possible to follow this little-known but fully verified romance from original sources. Lincoln wrote all of them, the first three to Mary Owens, his prospective bride, the fourth to the wife of a fellow Illinois politician, Orville H. Browning.

Miss Owens was the daughter of a well-to-do Kentucky slaveholder. Lincoln first met her in 1833 when she visited a married sister living in the vicinity of New Salem. He was favorably impressed and later, in an untroubled moment, declared to Mrs. Bennett Abell, the sister, "If that young woman ever returns from Kentucky, I'm going to catch, tie and marry her." Her return was arranged and the heedless promiser presently found himself obligated to carry out his part of the bargain.

Miss Owens arrived in style on election day of 1836, when her intended husband was being re-elected to a second term in the State Legislature. He was better dressed and it is said less shy than when she had met him before. Four months of indifferent courting followed. 

Early in December, his love affair somewhat stalemated, Lincoln resumed his legislative duties. The first of his three letters to Mary was written soon after he reached the state capital. "I have been sick," he wrote, "it is but little difference, however, as I have very little even yet to write." Then he reviewed the political news, ending his brief note with "Write soon and say something that will please me." There was no hint, however, that he wanted to be "pleased" by a suggestion that they get down to business on the subject which had induced Mary to leave her father's comfortable home. 

Lincoln returned to New Salem about three months after writing this letter. Soon thereafter he removed to Springfield, twenty miles distant, to practice law. A few weeks later, he again wrote Miss Owens. 

In this second letter, he was plainly hedging: I am often thinking of what we said about your coming to live at Springfield. I am afraid you would not be satisfied. There is a great deal of flourishing about in carriages here, which it would be your doom to see without sharing it. You would have to be poor without the means of hiding your poverty." Then he alluded to matrimony but in words which betrayed a waning desire to share that holy state with her,." . . . I much wis!. you would think seriously before you decide."

Three months later Miss Owens received the third and fit... letter from her hesitant and perplexed suitor. In this one he let the cat completely out of the bag, practically disavowing any romantic inte:-

est. "Friend Mary," he began, "You will no doubt think it rather strange that I should write you a letter on the same day on which we parted." He then made a clumsy attempt to rationalize concerning their faltering love affair. If further acquaintance "would contribute nothing to your happiness, I am sure it would not to mine," he wrote. "If it suits you best not to answer this, farewell. A long life and a merry one attend you."

These letters are thoroughly Lincolnesque but not more so than the one addressed to Mrs. Browning, a composition of some length which deserves much wider recognition than it has received as a piece of superior and delightful English prose. In this most revealing of all his correspondence, Lincoln announced his desire to give Mrs. 

Browning a "full and intelligent account of the things I have done and suffered since I saw you." These "doings and sufferings" turn out to be a jocular, some have thought ungentlemanly, recital of his love affair with Miss Owens, although her name is not disclosed. "In the autumn of 1836" he had promised a lady that he would become "her brother-in-law with all convenient despatch" if she would cause her sister to come to New Salem. The arrangement was made and he was "confoundedly well pleased." Having seen the sister before, he thought her "intelligent and agreeable and saw no good objection to plodding life through hand in hand with her." 

The prompt return of the lady, sister in tow, "stomached" him, he said, for she seemed too anxious, a demerit which, though disillusioning, he would "consent to waive." Her appearance, which had substantially altered, multiplied his bewilderment. "Although I had seen her before," he said, "she did not look as my imagination had pictured her . . . she now appeared a fair match for Falstaff." He did not think that anything "could have commenced at the size of infancy, and reached her present bulk in less than thirty-five or forty years.' 

But inasmuch as he had agreed to take her for his wife, he meant to do so even though "fairly convinced that no other man on earth would have her." 

Determined to "consider her my wife" and to put "all my powers of discovery to work in search of perfections which might be fairly set off against her defects," he conceded her several good points. 

There was her "fine face," and her mind "was not inferior, as I could discover, to any with whom I had been acquainted." 

He now found himself "repenting the rashness" which had gotten him into such a fix. "Through life," he lamented, "I have been in no bondage, either real or imaginary, from the thralldom of which I so much desired to be free." Much time had been spent in thinking how best he might procrastinate the evil day for a time, which I really dreaded as much, perhaps more, than an Irishman does the halter."

But a happy denouement had relieved him of his embarrassment.

He was "out of the 'scrape' 

out, clear, in every sense of the term, no violation of word, honor or conscience." Belatedly pressing his marriage proposal, he had been rejected. Surmising that the lady might have refused "through an affectation of modesty" he repeated it with a slight dash of fervor but without success. Confessing his mortification at this turn of events, he admits that his "vanity was deeply wounded by the reflection that I had been too stupid to discover her intentions, and at the same time never doubting that I understood them perfectly." Then he had experienced a totally unexpected feeling. 

He was a little in love with her." "But let it all go," he continued, "I'll try and outlive it. . . I most emphatically, in this instance, made a fool of myself." The Brownings thought this letter, which was dated April I, 1838, a hilarious piece of fiction, further confirmation of their already high estimate of the author's genius and imagination. Twenty-five years later they asked President Lincoln about it. He replied that it recounted an actual happening in his New Salem period but he did not disclose the name of the sweetheart who had inspired his waggish communication. Shortly after his death she was identified as Mrs. 

Jesse Vineyard of Weston, Missouri. All four letters were produced, creating something of a sensation. Mr. Herndon assisted in this publicity but ignored the obvious fact that the letters discredited his 

Lincoln-Rutledge story. 

When questioned about her romantic experience with Lincoln, Mrs. Vineyard said she rejected his marriage proposal because their union would have lacked congeniality, because his training had been different from hers, and because he was "deficient in those little links which make up the chain of a woman's happiness."

Lincoln by this time was generally regarded as a lawyer and politician of good possibilities. Miss Owens had not overlooked this fact but she was too sensible to risk either his well-being or her own. 

Facing spinsterhood, she thought marriage desirable but not essential and she would not assume a relationship almost certain to prove unsatisfactory. While acknowledging his splendid qualities, she could not disregard his lack of others which she believed were more important to their mutual happiness. Nor did she ever regret that she had refused to marry the man who authored the Gettysburg Address. Her attitude toward the embarrassing publicity caused by the release of the Browning letter was restrained and in good taste. 

Miss Owens had moved to Missouri not long after her parting with Lincoln, marrying Mr. Vineyard there on March 27, 1841. She died, a widow, on July 4, 1877. One of her sons became a prominent 

St. Louis attorney. Lincoln's three letters are cherished by her descendants who have not yet yielded to the tempting offers made for thein. 

When asked about Ann Rutledge, the former Mary Owens said that she did not recall ever hearing Lincoln mention her name.

Mrs. Vineyard told of his last message to her, delivered by Mrs. 

Abell; "Tell your sister that I think she was a great fool because she did not stay here, and marry me."

THE DYNAMIC MISS TODD 

There was included in Lincoln's letter to Mrs. Browning the terse statement, "I will never be satisfied with anyone who would be blockhead enough to have me." A year and a half after his naive narrowing of his marital opportunities, Mary Todd came into his life, a dazzling young woman who would not only have him but who eventually got him. Whether he was ever satisfied with her is an unresolved issue which has been bandied about by all grades of historians for nearly a century. 

Both Miss Todd and Miss Owens were Kentuckians who had been reared in plenty; both were intelligent and well educated. Each was inclined to stoutness, if not obesity. Both made a trip to Illinois prior to their romance with Lincoln. But there were sharp differences. 

Miss Owens was ten years older than Miss Todd. She had a pleasant disposition, was thoughtful of others, sedate and matter of fact. Mary 

Todd, on the other hand, possessed a ready and sometimes violent temper and had an earned reputation for making people uncomfortable. Mary Owens could have married Lincoln but refused him; her more spirited successor battled her imperial way through a tempestuous courtship, outlasting his fears and doubts. We may be almost sure that he would not have reached the presidency had he married Miss Owens and equally certain that he would have missed his great chance had he not married the forceful Miss Todd. 

Mary Ann Todd, the fourth child in a family of fourteen, made her first trip to Springfield when nineteen, visiting her sister, Elizabeth, who had married Ninian Edwards, the son of Illinois' first governor. She returned two years later and lost no time in reconnoitering the masculine field, quite a promising one as Springfield was the state capital and many young and able men congregated there during legislative sessions. Several were headed for national distinction. 

Among them was Stephen A. Douglas, who would oppose Lincoln for the presidency twenty years later. As for A. Lincoln, legislator and attorney-at-law, Mary's first impression was that he was cast in the mold of her friend and former neighbor, Kentucky's great statesman, Henry Clay.

Miss Todd was very active in Springfield's social affairs during the next few months. She was not considered strictly beautiful, but she was thought of as charming and vivacious. Her conversation sparkled; she was quick and clever at repartee, as those who incurred her displeasure usually discovered. Bystanders at this interesting and historical season in the capital thought that Mary Todd and Abraham

Lincoln were worlds apart in most matters, certainly in those having to do with matrimony.

Although undeniably attracted to this dynamic personality, Lincoln agreed with the common judgment. He was badly off financially and in no position to accept further obligations. Another and greater anxiety was his belief that his humble background permanently unfitted him for marriage with a woman of high social standing. Thus, while his affair with aristocratic Mary intrigued him, it also appalled him. 

Those of her people who had helped the affair at the start discouraged her when it appeared to be taking a serious turn. They did not think that she would ever be satisfied with a husband of his type or with the standard of living he could provide.

Neither did Lincoln permit the glamour of his romance to blind him to its practical side. Very conceivably a marriage to Miss Todd would be construed by his voting constituents as a clear break with the common people, not a political asset. But notwithstanding all his hesitancy and doubts, he continued his courting. The belief is widely held that their wedding was set for New Year's Day, 1841, but that when the time for the big event arrived, Lincoln did not appear. 

This story of the nuptial arrangements and the absconder, largely sponsored by Mr. Herndon, was generally accepted until extensive research proved the fallacious nature of the testimony given in support of the story. In order to build confidence for his missing bridegroom version, he furnished many details of the wedding that was to have been solemnized but overlooked such an important matter as the need of a license, if there was to be a wedding. Lincoln did not apply for a license and the story is utterly without foundation. There may have been talk of a marriage but a lover's quarrel was the extent of the happenings on that "fatal" first of January. The account of his flight from the altar is but another of those legends not now accepted by informed students of his life. 

Mr. Herndon undertook to define the cause of Lincoln's extreme gloom at this time, his opinion being that it was the result of his love for Ann Rutledge "coming in contact with new relations about to be assumed," flatly declaring, "His fidelity to it was sublime.” A scientific answer in total disagreement with his interpretation has recently been made. This explanation is that the weather during the last week in 1840 was such as to violently depress persons of Lincoln's singular physical and mental characteristics. This authority also holds that Miss Todd's inclination to irritability reached a high point during this same week and for the same reason. Both parties were simuitaneously in their very worst mental state, a situation which magnified their doubts and basic differences, bringing about a sudden end to their romance. A similar learned and persuasive diagnosis has also 10

been made in explanation of Lincoln's behavior when Ann Rutledge died.

Many stories have been related about Lincoln's supposed attention to other women during the months he thought his love affair with Miss Todd was irretrievably broken. The names of Matilda Edwards and Sarah Rickard, the latter but sixteen years old, appear in many biographies, chiefly because Mr. Herndon gave them undue prominence. Miss Edwards later repudiated most of the statements made about her. " . . . He never even stooped to pay me a compliment," she said. There is some evidence that the Rickard girl received a half-hearted marriage proposal, one which neither she nor her family took seriously.

A lot of publicity has been given to a watch which Lincoln is reported to have given to a Miss Mary Curtis of Louisville, Kentucky, during that January of 1841. The alleged history of this watch, which is engraved as though intended for a gift to Miss Todd from Lincoln, has been ingeniously promoted by fictionized accounts in the New 

York Tribune, The New York Times, and recently in Collier's. Lincoln's financial resources and his buying habits during these early days place this story on the ridiculous side by a wide margin. It should be classified as another of the deceptions which the American people are expected to believe about Lincoln. His entire income for the year 1840 probably did not exceed $300.00, the value of a watch of its kind at that time.

In the extended period following their separation, Miss Todd pretended an unconvincing indifference. She plunged heavier than ever into social activities and did some visiting at distant points. But she had lost her heart to the tall, almost penniless lawyer and the day came when her pride was to surrender to a greater force. Many months after the romance had gone on the rocks, a Springfield woman decided that the future of both Lincoln and Miss Todd would be advanced by a reconciliation, thereby gaining for herself a distinctive but as yet unappreciated place in history. The first meeting of the estranged couple in her home was in secret, but it led to an open renewal of the courtship, and within a few weeks marriage plans were being considered.

With the altar imminent, Lincoln's misgivings increased. He now demonstrated a deep-seated fear of marriage itself, a fear which lost none of its strength in contemplating a union with the accomplished and fiery Miss Todd. The uncertainty of his mind at this time and his caution are well illustrated by the confidential letter he wrote to an intimate friend who had been recently married; "I want to ask you a close question. Are you, in feeling as well as in judgment, glad that you are married, as you are? From anybody but me this would be an impudent question, not to be tolerated; but I know you will 11

pardon it in me. Please answer it quickly, as I am impatient to know.”

The prevailing understanding of this second romance was that Mary desired an early wedding in order to prevent another parting. 

Before many weeks the rehabilitated courtship, despite Lincoln's fears, was making progress. Giving only a few hours notice, they were married in the Edwards' Springfield home on November 4, 1842, most of the witnesses of the ceremony being more skeptical than hopeful.

NOT A DOMESTIC MAN 

Lincoln was irresistably drawn to the political arena where he was fully aware of the competitive strength of his great native abilities. He would not willingly have traded his ambitions for any degree of happiness, domestic or otherwise. He wanted to count for something in public affairs and most of his aptitudes were inclined that way. Herndon's unfortunate determination to make something of the 

Ann Rutledge affair led his judgment badly astray. Although insisting that his partner never loved after Ann's death, his contradictory statement was much closer to the truth; "Lincoln was abstracted, cool and never loved and could not from his very nature." 

His history of fear, hesitancy and doubt in matters of the heart would seem to manifest that Lincoln was unsuited for a romantic relationship with any woman, perhaps for matrimony itself. Marriage to him could only be an incidental part of his scheme of life. Mary 

Owens knew, among other things, that she could not successfully compete for his time and affections with the forces within him which drove him in another direction. She understood that his characteristics did not spell domesticity. Unlike Mary Todd, she refused a marriage certain to be faced with the hazards of such a disillusioning factor. 

Mary Todd Lincoln could not and did not change her bachelor inclined mate in these matters-but she was actually able to contribute vitally to the fulfillment of his ambitions, although indirectly. 

If matrimony was to be his experience, a wife of her type was preferable to one whose efforts would have been aimed at making him over into another pattern. If she was difficult to live with, the same may be said of him for he was a somewhat detached husband and not a model one in many ways. His indulgence with his children was notorious, amounting almost to an abdication of family responsibility, further evidence of his want of domestic qualities. 

Mrs. Lincoln has been accused of having tricked her husband into a marriage which it is charged he regretted as long as he lived. Some have asserted that her interest centered in him because she foresaw his future. That view is not a logical one. His record up to the time of his marriage indicated that he had unusual abilities but Mary Todd was taking the same chances that every woman does who marries. 

Mr. Herndon believed that it was in the year 1840 that Lincoln first 12

dreamed that he might have a large part in the affairs of his country, but his future was so well concealed that even such an astute person as Miss Todd could only guess that the days ahead included something = more for him than being just a successful lawyer with a decided flair for politics. "Lincoln's marriage was a policy marriage," partner Herndon was to say many times, adding "he paid the penalty of it, and the payment of that penalty wrote its receipt on his face." A dispassionate analysis of all the facts shows that this opinion, although a popular one, is terribly unjust to Mary Todd Lincoln. She played a difficult part very well from a long run point of view. There was no interruption in the growth of his mental and moral stature during his married years. If his wife did not promote this magnificent development, her association with him did not prevent it. And whatever her faults, she is entitled to a great deal of praise for having truly loved her husband. 

DON'T VARNISH ME" 

"If Mr. Lincoln could speak to me this day," biographer Herndon said of his martyred partner, "he would say, "Tell the truth and don't varnish me.' In this he rightly credited this wise Illinois lawyer with a strong desire to avoid hypocrisy in any form. Lincoln was suspicious of all biographers; he thought they did too much eulogizing, often ignoring the imperfections of their heroes. 

Mr. Herndon firmly resolved not to make the mistake of suppressing facts. But in his professed anxiety not to do so, he became the retailer of much ordinary gossip. A small part of this gossip took. the form of stories alleging that Lincoln had unconventional relations with nameless women. Although giving publicity to such reports, Mr. 

Herndon's papers overwhelming support the chastity of his associate. 

To those who sought an answer on this delicate point, he wrote, "Lincoln was true as steel to his wife during his whole married life." He also went on record that Lincoln "lived a pure and virtuous life. His idea was that a woman has as much right to violate the marriage vow as the man . . ." Of the President's pre-marital years, Herndon also bore witness, "I can truthfully say I never knew him to do a wrong thing... He was always noble."

A statement made by Judge David Davis, Lincoln's close friend of many years, may be considered strong evidence; "Lincoln's honor saved many a woman-I have seen women make advances, and I have seen Lincoln reject or refuse them. Lincoln had terribly strong passions for women .. and yet he had honor and a strong will, and these enabled him to put out the fires of his terrible passion." 

Other contemporary opinions are pertinent. One witness said, "In his relations to womankind, he was spotless, . . . no marble saint niched in cathedral aisle' was more secure from harm from him." 

Another expressed the opinion that "Lincoln's morals were chaste 13

and unobjectionable," and that "the weak were safe with him—the mark of a princely nature."

Whether Abraham Lincoln always lived on the pinnacle where he has been placed by a worshipful world may remain a moot question for some. Those who conclude that he was ruled by "terribly strong passions," that he fell short of his high reputation in this respect. must do so on the basis of such unsatisfactory evidence as the secondhand tales which Mr. Herndon thought should constitute a part of the record. This belief discards much reliable testimony, including that of Lincoln's own word.

In his last letter to Mary Owens her awkward Illinois suitor wrote, "I want in all cases to do right and most particularly so in all cases with women." Would he have regarded promiscuity as doing right? The weight of his clear, lifelong conception of right and wrong is a formidable one. There is also the subtle implication which may be read into his note to a friend one week after his marriage, "... nothing new here, except my marrying, which to me is a matter of profound wonder.”
