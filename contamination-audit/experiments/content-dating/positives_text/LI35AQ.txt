Introduction 

Six months ago a new three-man, full-time Authority assumed direction of the New York City public housing program. When, on last May 1, the three newly appointed Members stepped into the leadership of the Authority which had become the country's largest residential real estate operation, they faced a complex task. 

During 1957, the New York City Housing Authority was subjected to criticisms from the press and from private organizations concerned with public housing. These criticisms were directed at certain deficiencies in carrying out the public housing program. They fell into two broad categories: the inability of the Authority, as then organized, to cope with the administrative problems presented by changing social and economic conditions; and poor maintenance in a few public housing developments. 

As a consequence, the City Administrator made a careful and searching analysis of the policies, administrative structure, and managerial methods of the Housing Authority. He issued a report in September, 1957, containing recommendations for changes in policy and administration for the purpose of modernizing this organization so that it could deal effectively with the new problems facing public housing in recent years. 

Like any large-scale organization, public or private, attempting to meet the changing demands of a swiftly moving society, the New York City Housing Authority had to change with the times. It needed to reassess its operating methods, alter them where it was shown that they no longer served their purpose with maximum efficiency, and devise new programs to meet the needs of the world's largest city. 

This is the enormous problem that confronted the Authority half a year ago. Since that time, the country's largest landlord has been engaged in the operation of inspecting, assessing, and reorganizing its operations and procedures. 

This period has been marked not only by self-inspection, but also by innovation. While the size and complexity of the Authority's operations require a careful assessment of consequences and costs before any change can be put into effect, it has moved forward as swiftly as is compatible with its responsibility for so many lives and so much property. 

The activities of the Authority have a substantial effect on New York City as a whole. 

At the same time, the population and property that make up the totality of the Authority are sufficiently large to form by themselves one of the larger cities in this country. 

The New York City Housing Authority now provides apartments for 101,864 families living in 87 developments. These developments cover a total area of 1674 acres. When the 

37,870 apartments now in construction or planning are completed, this "city" will be even larger.

With its present population of approximately 400,000, the Authority could qualify as the 25th largest city in the country. By itself, it would be the third largest city in the State.

Large as it has grown, the Authority's program still cannot meet the demand for public housing. Within the past ten years, one million applications for apartments have been received. There is a backlog of over 60,000 approved applications. 

At the same time, the Authority must function under a set of administrative regulations more involved in many ways then those faced by most municipalities. The Authority draws its support from, and is subject to, regulations of the Federal, State, and City governments. 

Its architectural planning, its site selection, its managerial procedures, its financial dealings, its social policies are all subject, in varying degrees, to this tripartite regulation. 

One of the pressing problems that confronted the new Authority was the filling of a number of important executive positions. While these posts had to be filled as swiftly as possible, at the same time they had to be awarded to individuals whose experience and background indicated they could bring to their duties the administrative ability to handle large-scale problems and the creative imagination to bring new techniques to bear on managerial and social problems. 

The Authority not only faced the necessity of recruiting executive personnel, but of creating entirely new administrative units. In some cases, certain operations had been scattered in units located in different departments. In other cases, vitally important functions had simply been added to the responsibilities already carried by Authority executives who, consequently, could not give them the attention they required. Using as a guide the recommendations and suggestions offered by City Administrator Charles F. Preusse, the

Authority acted to fill these posts and reorganize the handling of these responsibilities as quickly as possible.

The personnel appointments, procedural innovations, and policy changes effected during the past six months can be classified into two broad categories. These can be described as changes intended either to advance administrative effectiveness or to institute social planning and thereby keep the Authority's program current with changing social and economic conditions. 

---

Changes to Improve Administration 

Recent Administrative changes have affected each of the Departments of the Housing Authority-Executive, Management, Development, and Personnel. In addition, a new 

Department has been created-General Services. These changes will enable all of the 

Departments to function more effectively and the innovations already introduced will generate further self-improvement within each Department. 

EXECUTIVE 

Appointment of an Acting General Manager In the City Administrator's study of the Housing Authority he had recommended the elimination of the posts of Executive Director and Assistant to the Chairman and the creation of the post of General Manager, who would serve as the Authority's chief administrative executive. In keeping with this suggestion, Mr. Gerald J. Carey, who, at various times, held the posts of Executive Director and Assistant to the Chairman, was appointed Acting General Manager effective May 1.

Formation of New Administrative Units 

Two of the most important administrative innovations introduced by the Authority have been the creation of a new Division within the Executive Department and the unification of two of its former divisions into a separate Department. Both changes are intended to promote efficiency and economy. Neither required the employment of outside personnel; but, rather, resulted in the consolidation of functions that previously had been scattered among several administrative units.

As of October 1, the new Administrative Methods Division went into operation. Its function is to conduct continuing studies of the Authority's operations and make recommendations for improvement. Previously, comparatively fragmentary and decentralized efforts at such research were conducted by personnel attached to different departments. 

Realizing the value of submitting procedures to continuous review by a body of trained and informed staff specialists, the Authority drew together personnel who had already acquired the requisite experience in such research, and familiarity with the Authority's operations, and formed them into a single unit responsible directly to the General Manager.

The Authority has also formed the Department of General Services, which went into operation as of October 1. This reorganization brought together functions that had previously been conducted independently and drew together personnel who had previously worked in separate divisions. 

The Department of General Services is composed of the Purchase and Office Services Divisions, both previously administered as units of the Executive Department. Because of the interrelationship of the functions of these two former divisions, it is expected that their unification will lead to greater efficiency and economy of operation. - 5-

MANAGEMENT Appointment of a Director of Management 

At the time the new Authority was appointed last spring, the position of Director of Management had been vacant since the previous summer. This position is one of the most important in the Authority. The man who holds it is responsible for the operation and maintenance of all completed developments. His responsibility constantly grows as new dwelling units are completed. 

At the time this post was filled, the Chairman outlined the scope of the Director of Management's responsibilities and emphasized their importance by noting that "this huge investment must be protected and maintained for the use of future generations." 

The man selected to fill this post was Albert H. Morgan, who assumed his duties on 

August 1. Mr. Morgan, a graduate of Stevens Institute of Technology, is a Professional

Engineer who has gained a nation-wide reputation in the fields of building management and maintenance for both government and private industry. As a consultant, he has made studies of building and plant operation for both government and industry. 

Maintenance Procedures 

Ineffective maintenance procedures had been one of the principal criticisms leveled against the Authority before its reorganization. Studies are now under way to remedy these defects. These include analyses of what constitute equitable work-loads that should be assigned to maintenance employees and the devising of a program of preventive maintenance, so that severe deterioration will not be allowed to occur. Whenever possible, maintenance operations will be centralized in order to reduce costs. 

A review of the maintenance problems presented by current design and construction standards is now being conducted. As this review progresses, the Management Department will forward to the Development Department suggestions that, when incorporated in future construction, will lead to a reduction in costs. Policing Housing Officer personnel, who function as a Division of the Management Department, have, within recent months, been expanded and equipped with special aids to increase effectiveness. New supervisory positions have been created. In July, seven lieutenants and nineteen sergeants were appointed. The induction of 27 recruits brought the Housing Officers to a new peak strength of 321 men.

A new headquarters for Fort Greene, Farragut, and Marcy Houses, which together have 6,608 tenants, was dedicated at the same time. A communication system linking the three developments, first of its kind in the City, was put into operation. Special patrol cars for the use of Housing sergeants were put into use at approximately the same time to improve efficiency. - 6-

In his address to the Housing Officers at the July induction ceremony, the Chairman said: "Proper policing is not an end in itself. We want better policing in our housing projects because we consider it an important step in keeping the projects desirable places for family living. "The development of an effective police force for the Authority is part of our program to assure that public housing projects are wholesome communities. At a time when crime and delinquency are threatening the peace and safety of large sections of the City, we intend to do everything we can for the protection of our tenants.” 

DEVELOPMENT Retaining of Planning Consultants 

As part of the program of examining and streamlining the operations of every Department, two consultants on planning and design were retained on a one-year contract effective last August 18. These two advisers, George D. Brown, Jr., and Bernard W. Guenther of the architectural firm of Brown and Guenther, are functioning as both supervisors and advisers for the architectural planning program. 

The scope of this program is indicated by the fact that 32 public housing developments are now on the planning boards. When completed, they will provide apartments for 24,803 families.

The consultants have had extensive experience in designing large-scale public and private housing developments. They have also filled important executive positions with government housing agencies.

Their duties include review of the plans submitted by architects, and assistance in dealing with the increasingly complex problem of site selection. They will also devise new procedures in order to cut red tape and shorten the length of time it takes to attain final approval of plans.

Expansion of Playgrounds 

As one aspect of the effort to make public housing facilities better serve the needs of tenant families, the Authority has embarked on a program of playground expansion. 

Public housing developments have an uncommonly large number of children. In addition, many youngsters living in the neighborhoods adjacent to public housing utilize the developments' superior playground facilities. The result is that these playgrounds have become increasingly inadequate despite the forward-looking planning that their inclusion in public housing developments represented. 

The Authority has therefore instituted a program of playground-building that will increase the play space not only in developments now in construction or in planning, but also, wherever possible, in developments that have already been completed. Landscaping plans in developments not yet completed will call for an upward revision of 40% in the amount of play area allocated for each apartment. At the same time, a survey of all completed developments is being conducted to determine where present lawn areas should be converted to play areas. Several such conversions are already under way. -7-

Selecting Housing Sites 

In its selection of locations for new developments, the Authority will seek to utilize substantially smaller sites than has been the case until now. 

Most public housing developments have been built on large tracts of either slums or vacant land. Large blocks of substandard dwellings have been cleared and replaced by housing developments occupying "superblocks" consisting of several City blocks enclosed into one. Where large sections of vacant land have been available, superblock site planning has also been used.

This kind of large-scale building has been the most feasible way of restoring extensive blighted areas where no new housing had been built for decades. Similarly, the erection of developments on large tracts of vacant land was one of the swiftest ways of supplying the housing so sorely needed after World War II, and of providing new homes for many of those displaced by slum clearance developments. 

Because of the progress made by the City's rebuilding program in the years since World War II ended, the number of large sites appropriate for superblock site planning has declined. Suitable vacant land sites are now usually available only in much smaller tracts.

Examining the experience of recent years, the Authority has concluded that valuable benefits would accrue to public housing and the neighborhoods into which it is introduced if smaller sites were utilized. By utilizing small sites, the uprooting and relocation that is an unavoidable consequence of site clearance would be minimized. The use of smaller sites, including those large enough only for a single building, would also allow the use of vacant areas that had heretofore been bypassed as too small. In such cases, the need for relocating families would be entirely eliminated. Also, the institutional appearance that is virtually unavoidable in superblock developments can be more easily eliminated when construction is done on a smaller scale. 

PERSONNEL Withdrawal from Career and Salary Plan 

One of the most immediate and pressing problems confronting the new Authority was posed by the need to provide equitable salary adjustments for certain groups of employees, particularly among those employed in maintenance operations. In their case, fair treatment required wage and hour adjustments difficult to provide because of certain Career and 

Salary Plan regulations. 

Largely, this problem grew out of the fact that the requirements of the public housing program led to the creation of positions that have no exact counterpart in City agencies covered by the Career and Salary Plan.

As a consequence, it was found that the most equitable method of dealing with this problem was to withdraw from the City's Career and Salary Plan. At the same time, the safeguards offered by the Plan were guaranteed to all employees. In this manner, the 

Authority introduced flexibility into its personnel system while it retained the benefits that Civil Service employees are entitled to under an enlightened labor relations program. - 8-

Changes to Advance Social Planning 

The New York City Housing Authority is primarily concerned with people. Its purpose is to have the property it administers serve those who live within its developments. 

In the final sense, therefore, administrative method must be built upon a foundation of sound social policy.

The Authority serves a dual public made up of its own tenants and of the citizens of the entire City. Public housing developments cannot be viewed as self-contained units. 

They affect the lives of those who live within them and the lives of those living in the neighborhoods within which they are located. Therefore, such problems as site selection, income regulations, racial composition, tenant selection, and community relations must be considered as they affect the entire City. 

There is thus a reciprocal relationship between the City and the Authority. The public housing program is an expression of the City's broad social policy, and the social policies followed by the Authority affect the life of the rest of the City. 

Recognizing the inter-related nature of these problems, the Authority has introduced a number of changes and innovations that will enable public housing to serve more effectively the social needs of its own tenants and of the entire City. At the same time, the 

Authority is deeply cognizant that many of its efforts must be considered exploratory. 

The pioneering programs that are being devised to deal with such problems as race relations and social welfare may need alteration or revision as experience is gained following the introduction of various phases of these programs. 

Appointment of Consultant on Race Relations 

On July 17, the Authority announced the appointment of a Consultant on Race Relations, the first position of its kind in any public housing authority in the country. 

The man selected to fill this post was Madison S. Jones, who, as a member of the staff of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, had been in charge of developing and promoting policies for combating discrimination and segregation in housing throughout the country. Mr. Jones also has worked in the field of race relations for the Federal Housing Administration. 

The need to examine the influence of the Authority's operations on interracial and intergroup relationships in the City had become increasingly apparent. A notable record has been made in operating integrated public housing developments, but it has become necessary to re-examine certain aspects of Authority operations which have led to a heavy concentration of minority families in some of its developments. 

At the time the appointment of Mr. Jones was announced, the Chairman noted that his responsibilities will include “an examination of the racial aspects involved in all policies and operations-planning, site selection, tenant relocation, tenant selection, tenant relations, and community relations." — 9 — 

---

Studies of tenant selection policies are now being conducted in order to determine how the public housing program can best contribute to neighborhood integration. Discussions have been held with the major social organizations concerned with this problem throughout the City and, similarly, with neighborhood groups in different areas, so that the problem can be explored on a local community level as well. 

At the same time the office of the consultant has become a clearing house for the provision of guidance and advice when problems bearing on race relations have arisen within the Authority. Requests for guidance and assistance have come from various housing managers and individual tenant requests have also been handled. 

In-service training sessions for personnel whose work brings them into continuous contact with applicants and tenants will be initiated shortly. Race relations policies will be outlined and discussed with executives and subsequent sessions will convey this information to larger groups of Authority employees.

Appointment of Consultant on Social Policy As the Authority's operations expanded across the City during recent years, an increasing number of families were displaced from their sub-standard homes to make way for new public housing. Normally, such families have a high priority for admission to public housing. But, as the number of such displaced families increased, there inevitably was a growing number of them who did not meet the social standards of eligibility for public housing. Similarly, an enormously enlarged tenant body resulted in a growing number of families (actually, very small in proportion to the total number of families) whose behavior made them undesirable as tenants. 

The new Authority recognized the value of utilizing professionally trained social workers to study these "problem families" and diagnose whether or not it was desirable to retain them or admit them, as the case might be, as public housing tenants. In many cases, proper referrals will enable the resources of outside casework agencies to be brought to bear on these families and their problems ameliorated. The result of such efforts would be the salvaging of families who otherwise would be permanently "exiled" to slum dwellings. 

On August 6, therefore, the Authority retained Miss Helen Hall, Director of the Henry Street Settlement and nationally known in the field of social work, to advise it on the establishment of a Social Consultation Unit. Miss Hall, functioning as a part-time consultant, has been developing the program to be followed and advising on the staffing of this new Division. 

At the time this appointment was announced, the Chairman said: "The Housing Authority will not become a case work agency. It will try, however, to help needy families who are considered undesirable as tenants because of their social problems to secure the benefit of aid by outside casework agencies. The Social Consultation 

Unit will also devise ways and means of stimulating tenant leadership, and will work closely with community centers in public housing developments." - 10-

At present the Consultant on Social Policy is conducting a series of intensive studies for the purpose of formulating a program to be followed by the Social Consultation Unit that is being established. Outside social agencies have been generous with their resources and cooperation and have loaned the Authority the services of a number of trained caseworkers to review the histories of tenant families whose behavior has brought them to the point of possible eviction.

These caseworkers are now serving as a temporary advisory unit to the Authority's Tenant Review Board, which is using their diagnoses for guidance in reviewing cases of problem families previously scheduled for eviction. An even more important service rendered by the case workers is the assistance they have given managers. As a result, instances have already occurred where it has been possible to bring outside casework to the aid of problem families.

Development of Tenant Relations and Community Relations Programs 

In addition to the work in tenant and community relations implicit in the programs being developed by Miss Hall and Mr. Jones, other efforts are being made to improve the general quality of tenant relationships within housing developments and of relationships between the Authority and outside organizations. 

Since housing developments so directly affect surrounding areas, the Authority has found it desirable and informative to draw on community leaders for the expression of their viewpoints on social problems. 

One of the early actions of the Authority was to form an advisory committee on tenant relations, whose first meeting was held on May 27. This group is composed of ten community leaders in the social service and allied fields. They have enabled the Authority to gain the benefit of their knowledge and experience and to maintain continuing and close relationships with civic groups who are also concerned with vital social problems. 

On the level of the individual housing development, the Authority has been cooperating with a study conducted by students of the New York School of Social Work in cooperation with Henry Street Settlement. Working in two buildings of LaGuardia Houses on the Lower East Side of Manhattan, the students interviewed families to ascertain their needs and desires for social services and other community assistance and to learn their attitudes and degree of adjustment to their immediate neighbors, the community, and the housing development management staff. 

One of the aims of this effort was to stimulate tenant self-discipline by enlightening them as to their self-interest in contributing to a well-run housing development. The program helped tenants, many of them new to the neighborhood, to handle both individual and group problems. With the interest and approval of the Authority, this program has since been extended to all of LaGuardia Houses. — 11 – 

---

Raising the Maximum Income Limits for Admission and Continued Occupancy 

The Authority considers that one of its most significant acts in altering the social and economic environment that has recently come to be associated with public housing is its increase of the maximum income limits for continued occupancy in Federal, State, and City-sponsored public housing and of the maximum income limits for admission to Citysponsored, no-cash-subsidy public housing. 

At the time the increase in these limits was announced, the Chairman stated: "This is one of the steps necessary to improve the developments and maintain them as normal communities for family living. What we are doing is readjusting our income limits so that they are in keeping with changing economic conditions. The old limits have forced out tenants whose economic circumstances had improved only slightly. As a result, many families with drive and initiative have had to leave public housing. These are families who can be counted upon to exert leadership and set examples of social responsibility to others. 

By raising our income limits, we hope to increase the percentage of families exerting this influence in our developments. "Many of the families who have had to leave public housing developments when their incomes go over the limit still do not earn enough to afford decent housing in the City. 

Many of them have had to return to the slums or seek homes outside the City. The new income limits will reinforce the City's efforts to encourage such families to continue living here."

Another step taken to promote economic diversification in a neighborhood was the Authority's change of two developments in the early stages of architectural planning from the Federal to the City program. On September 18, the Authority announced that Franklin 

D. Roosevelt Houses and Mary K. Simkhovitch Houses, originally scheduled for construction on the Lower East Side under Federal subsidy, would instead be built under the City-sponsored, no-cash-subsidy program. As a result, many of the area's residents whose incomes would have been too high for them to be admitted to the developments already built in this area will be eligible for the new apartments.

Conclusion 

The six months that have elapsed since the reorganization of the Authority have had to be devoted, to a substantial degree, to self-examination and preparation for change. 

No large organization, particularly one that affects so many lives as does the Authority, can alter its operating methods without careful study and painstaking preparation. 

As has been outlined, many of the means for improving operations, and thereby serving the public more effectively, have already been introduced into the Authority. New and highly qualified personnel have been brought in, programs to meet the requirements imposed by changing social conditions are being prepared and tested, and changes in administrative structure have been made in order to utilize the Authority's own personnel resources more effectively.

The Members have made it a policy to acquaint themselves with the actual state of public housing developments throughout the City. They have made repeated field tours in order to view the developments in day-by-day operation and thus acquire personal and intimate knowledge of the conditions that confront both personnel and tenants. 

The Members of the Authority believe that these preparations have laid a solid foundation for the progressively accelerating improvement of operating techniques. They anticipate that the policies they have initiated will contribute toward an improved social and economic environment for the tenants of public housing and that this will, in turn, increase the degree of public acceptance and support for the public housing program. 

It is now almost a quarter-century since this program was initiated in this City. During that time the Authority has responded to the crises brought on by depression and the war-inspired housing shortage. The Authority developed a public housing program that is unprecedented in scope and in the difficulties that have had to be overcome in order to make it operative.

The Members of the New York City Housing Authority are confident that this tradition of successful public service will be continued and strengthened in the years to come. --13-
