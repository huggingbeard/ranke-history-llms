TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS-and longer A Vacation Guide to ALL America 

Edited by FREDRIC E. TYARKS

Copyright 1940, Harian Publications, 270 Lafayette St., New York City

TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS 

-and longer A vacation guide to America (U.S.

, Canada, and Mexico) net 

The purpose of TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS is to guide and to suggest. The plan is to reveal the wide extent of both a continent and a method. Our 48 states are so huge that they are greater than most 

Europe. With Canada and Mexico added, they are the biggest thing known to man. Nothing else that man has tried to carve into something that works for him has been so large and rich, so capable of infinite development.

Hence America as a place in which to vacation is so vast it becomes all things to all men. Therein lies its supremacy over anything else the world has ever known. But therein too lies the reason why no one knows America.

Were the United States a group of 48 separate nations, it would then be better known, for books in profusion and in detail would have been published about the wonders of each state. Yet despite all the work of the state publicity bureaus, the great beauties of, say, New York State remain largely unknown even though here is a genuine empire of scenery, its unknown splendors like the Gorge of the Genessee, Keene Valley, Kaaterskill Clove, and the hundreds of others so largely unheard-of combining to form a giant territory grander than many a European country. And so it goes with Colorado, Florida, California (famous only for Southern California, not for that portion of its coast line which is grander than all Gaspe and as colorfully warm as the Riviera). In terms of the common complaint, Americans know more about Europe than they do about America.

The staleness of the observation only emphasizes its truth. The fact is that Americans know only what they've seen. And they travel the main and crowded highways, with much of the more glorious parts of America hidden even from nearby residents. (What New Yorker knows that within the range of a day's picnic is a sight so famed in 3

Europe that a generation ago, it was Kaaterskill Clove, not New York 

City, that brought foreign visitors to these shores?)

The gay and colorful America, the wide, vivid, turbulent America that shines forth in the confused and songful lines of America's great poet, Walt Whitman, is, to most Americans, an unknown country. 

To many America is a place of 48 states, but what those states offer is a mystery. To so many the Deep South is a place of romance and plantations, but it is still more: huge states of sandhills, great mountain masses, broad beaches, beautiful, colorful jungles of swamps as vast as a duchy, and so on and on. 

If America is an unknown continent, then Americans and their mental horizons, in effect, are limited, and that means, first, that the towns or regions to which they turn for their summer vacations are acutely bounded by the extent of their past peregrinations or the fanciful turns they have given to their imaginations. That means, second, that their means of vacation, the type of vacation to which they have given their weeks of freedom, are shrunk to a repetition of what they have already done, have observed in their wanderings, or have heard their acquaintances tell of. 

The plan of TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS is to give meaning to each section of America-to fill in the blank spaces with terse but adequate descriptions of the scenic and hence the vacation features of each part of the nation. In a sense, this repeats the familiar, for we all know that it rains a lot in Washington and that there's a desert in 

Arizona. But, first, TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS shows what can be done in both places and, second, this vacation guide often proves startling, for Arizona is revealed as a state where the winters in some sections are severe and snow blocks the roads while Washington is revealed as two-thirds a hot, treeless desert.

Specific areas everywhere that promise vacation possibilities are indicated and a town or resort therein discussed briefly. This might be a popular town or resort, or a fashionable one, or the best developed, but from this sample, the man or woman with a vacation ahead can discover the attractions of entire regions hitherto unknown. 

But resorts and resort areas are only a part of this book. The national parks are treated in some detail as they are both famous recreational resorts-among the most popular vacation resorts in the entire country-and also tourist magnets for that ever-growing number of two weeks' nomads who spend their vacations in cars. 

Even more, the entire field of vacations is surveyed so that somewhere within this book, a new form of vacation or a new idea may 4

greet you. Some of the ideas are of course obvious; others exotic, and concerning these latter ones-like dude ranches- you've no doubt heard a lot. The effort here is to show how you can translate a vague desire to learn more about such vacations or a strong desire to see what they're like and how much they cost, into a concrete realization. 

Throughout, TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS is designed to aid the vacationist-not the resort owner or the Chamber of Commerce. 

Hence it differs from the usual state or city publicity by telling the unpleasant aspects of a region as well as reporting the glittering beauties.

This is a book based upon personal investigation. The editor and a staff of researchers have toured America to bring back the facts that will guide you in planning your vacation. The aim has been to present accurate data in the smallest possible space. Hence one page often represents weeks of research. Yet America and American vacations are so huge a subject that TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS is nonetheless a big book, as large in content as many a 200 or 300 page book. Pages are large and the type face is small although quite legible; thus each page holds from two and one half to three and one half times the number of words appearing on the average page of a novel. 

This is an experimental edition. If the present shape of the contents and the nature of subjects covered are to your liking, it will stay as it is. Your comments will be welcomed: compliments if they are deserved; knocks if they are warranted, so that this book in its future editions will become an ever increasingly valuable adjunct to the Am erican vacationist-that means to you. 

-FREDRIC E. TYARKS, Editor LQ

THE NATIONAL PARKS 

What they are

The national parks consist of 23 areas of scenic and recreational value within the continental limits of the United States (as well as one such area in both Alaska and Hawaii). A national park is under the supervision of the Department of

Interior as opposed to the national forests, which are under the supervision of the Department of Agriculture. The national park service also is in charge of national monuments (which are occasionally but erroneously called national parks) as well as national historic parks, national military parks, national battlefield sites, national cemeteries, and the like, all of which are associated with events in American history. In addition there is one national recreational area, Boulder Dam.

The national parks contain some of the most scenic sections of the entire country and are distinctly worth visiting, particularly if a careful route to the parks is followed, as some of the country outside of the parks is greater in scenic value than anything within the parks (this being most true of Yellowstone). 

What to do at the parks 

To the nation as a whole, the parks offer excellent sightseeing. To people nearby, particularly in the western parks, they offer excellent week-end and vacation resorts. Mt. 

Rainier, Rocky Mountain, Yosemite, and Yellowstone are highly popular resorts for people from nearby cities and states.

There are well defined programs for the motorist at the various parks, consisting mainly of motor caravans to scenic points led without charge by a ranger-lecturer. The parks also offer short trail trips, via foot or horse, to outstanding points of interest, many of which cannot be seen via auto. These too are led without charge (except for the rental of a horse) by a rangerlecturer. At night, there are generally campfire talks given by park naturalists as well as open-air entertainment of many sorts, most of which is free: Indian dances at 

Grand Canyon, vaudeville, motion pictures of the park, etc.

For those who spend a good deal of time at the parks, the possibilities include fishing, hiking, mountain climbing, pack trips into the interior, etc. 

What they cost

It is possible to stay at the parks for less than $1 a day and for as much as $20 a day. The first method means staying at your own tent; the second, at swanky hotels. In-between are many other types of accommodations. At most parks in the west, tourists may stay at fine hotels, housekeeping bungalows, nonhousekeeping bungalows, and, at some, in rented tents. Meals are served at cafeterias and restaurants. 

For those who come by auto, stay at the less expensive overnight accommodations, and eat at cafeterias, it is possible to see the parks at a daily cost of $3. $5 a day is generally adequate. Those who do not come via their own car or a rented car will generally average $10 to $15 a day expenses. See the detailed lists of costs that follow later in this book.

Further Information about the parks 

At the parks you will be supplied with booklets describing the scenic, botanic, geologic, and other features as well as an outline of possible trail trips, motor drives, etc. If desired in advance, these booklets may be obtained free from the National 

Park Service at Washington or from the United States Travel Bureau in either New York or San Francisco (which will also answer any question you may have about travel in the United States). The railroads which go to the parks or near the parks issue well illustrated folders (generally, however, without cost data); they are worth securing for the photos and may be obtained upon application to any railroad ticket agent. Travel agents are able to supply information about all expense tours as are bus and railroad ticket agents.

Seasons 

Most of the parks are open all year for those who want to defy the snow that blocks most of the mountain roads. During the winter, if no accommodations are available at the usual summer hotels, they can often be obtained at very reasonable rates at the lodges and restaurants which cater to the park employees (this may not be done, however, in the summer). Conducted tours are available generally from the end of June to September 15. All year parks are 

Acadia, Boulder Dam, Carlsbad Caverns, Crater Lake, General Grant, Grand Canyon South Rim, Great 

Smoky, Hot Springs, Mt. Rainier, Olympic, Platt, Rocky Mt., Sequoia, Shenandoah, Wind Cave, Yosemite, Zion. 

Eastern Parks

Acadia Natl. Park on Mt. Desert 

Island, Maine, adjoining Bar Harbor.

A mountain and ocean park, combining the scenic features of both, the only place along the Atlantic Seaboard where the mountains and the sea meet. The feature of the park is a motor road to the summit of Cadillac Mountain. Park naturalists conduct auto caravans (in which you follow in your own car) to points of interest, and there are campfire lectures, movies of the park, and group singing three nights a week.

Within the park are numerous trails and footpaths as well as carriage roads and bridle paths. The best ways to see the park are from a motor launch which circles the park and by buckboard or tally-ho. 

Great Smoky Mountains Natl. 

Park on the Tennessee-North Carolina border, between Asheville and 

Knoxville. Here are both the greatest mass of mountains east of the western mountains and the tallest peaks in the Appalachians (with the exception of Mt. Mitchell not far from the park in North Carolina), plus deep forests and numerous flowers. Only one road leads across the mountains, ascending a mile into the sky. All motorists are urged to take the side-road at Newfound Gap to 

Clingman's Dome, 6642 feet above sea level; from the tower on the summit, one of the most extensive views in the east is spread before you. There are numerous trails, including the Appalachian Trail, which runs along the crest of the Great 

Smokies. Most hikers climb Mt. Le 

Conte, above the road. Campgrounds have been provided, but virtually no other accommodations are available within the park, except at Elkmont and a few cabins at Gatlinsburg. 

Outside of the park are many tourist cabins, tourist homes, summer hotels, etc. Most people complete the trip through the park within a few hours, but the country outside of the park is particularly striking; it will prove a good plan to tour Nantahala Gorge (US 19), the waterfalls along US 64 in North Carolina, the forest road from Franklin to US 19, and both Chimney Rock and Lake Lure.

Mammoth Cave Natl. Park reach ed from Cave City, Ky. Mammoth 

Cave consists of approximately 175 miles of underground passages, some

of which may be toured. It is the largest cave in Kentucky and has been a tourist mecca for a century. 

The cave can be seen via conducted tours, including boat trips along the underground Echo River. (Rates are given later in the discussion of costs.) Outside of the cave is a good country for hiking, riding, and picnicking.

Shenandoah Natl. Park in western 

Virginia. The chief feature is the 

Skyline Drive along the crest of the Blue Ridge Mts. Although the road runs for 96 miles, most people take it for only a short while as the many curves and the 35 mile speed limit make the journey a slow one. From the road, splendid views of Shenandoah Valley are often revealed. The best way to see the park itself is via footpath or bridle path to some of the falls, canyons, and mountains in the park. (Eventually the road .will run down to Great Smoky Natl.

Park, along the crest of the mountains in Virginia and North Carolina; it will go over the summit of the highest peak in the east, Mt. Mitchell.)

Middle Western Parks Hot Springs Natl. Park adjoining the city of Hot Springs, Ark. While the park is mainly a curative resort, it is becoming a summer resort for the Gulf states and a fall-winterspring resort for the northern states, as the year round climate is favorable. At numerous bath houses in the city, some of the mineral baths may be taken without a physician's approval, although the government does not recommend this. Vacationists generally spend their time hiking, riding, boating, fishing, and golfing. The principal leisurely way of seeing the city and the park is via horse-drawn carriage.

Platt Natl. Park adjoining Sul phur, Okla. Mainly a curative resort because of the sulphur and bromide springs but also known as a recreational center for summer visitors, as the rolling hills of the park are in sharp contrast to the flat countryside surrounding the park. There are trails for hiking and riding; swimming is a popular form of diversion. 

Far Western Parks 

Carlsbad Caverns Natl. Park near 

Carlsbad, N. M., not far from El Paso,

Texas. It is believed to be the largest limestone cavern in the world (although Colossal Cave in Arizona may eventually prove to be larger).

It consists of many chambers, some boldly beautiful, others fantastically weird. To see the cave adequately requires a seven mile, five hour walk. If you don't want to take the walk, you can ride down to the Big 

Room of the cave via elevator; this costs 50 cents for each ascent or descent. The park service, however, recommends that if you can walk, you spurn the elevator; however, many people do ride back. 

As the temperature of the cave is 56 degrees throughout the year, wear a light sweater or a wrap. Low heeled shoes are recommended. 

You probably will leave before dusk, but if you can possibly wait until then, you will see one of the features of the park-the bat spectacle, when millions of bats fly out. of the cave in dark clouds. (As bats hibernate in the winter, this is seen of course only from late spring to early autumn.)

Crater Lake Natl. Park in southwestern Oregon. A lake of deep vivid blue coloring, its amazing beauty set off by lava cliffs which reach upwards from the water for 500 to 2000 feet. The best way to see the park is to drive around the rim of the lake, a 35 mile trip. But no one should fail to take a launch trip if he comes in July and August, when this service is offered, and it is also wise to hike over several short trails. 

On the motor drive, view the Pinnacles at the eastern entrance-many spires and fluted columns carved out of soft volcanic material. Also stop enroute at Watchman lookout station and climb one half mile to Watchman Peak, for the best view of the lake. Allow 75 minutes for the climb. (If you like company, you can join 10

anauto caravan to Watchman station and climb with a ranger-naturalist to the peak.) At park headquarters, another trail is worth taking, this being to the Castle Crest 

Wild Flower Garden. Also from the headquarters, you will probably want to walk a mile and a half to the water's edge as you can't appreciate the full beauty of the lake until you view it as close as that. 

Glacier Natl. Park in northern

Montana. Part of the Waterton Lake- 

Glacier International Park, it is a mountain park of great scenery, perhaps the most striking of all the parks for sheer magnificence and grandeur. Motorists see a great deal of the breath-taking beauty of the mountains from the Going-to-the-Sun 

Highway, which crosses the park from east to west. The highway passes Lake MacDonald and St. Mary

Lake, the latter being so remarkable for its natural beauty that it ranks not far after Crater Lake or Lake 

Louise in Banff park for brilliance of surrounding. Between these lakes, the Going-to-the-Sun Highway climbs high into the mountains. The road, carved out of the mountain side, is immensely spectacular. (While not dangerous, it does require cautious driving.) Numerous small snow covered glaciers are seen along this road and at Many Glaciers, on a side road. Other side roads lead to beautiful lakes; the lakes on the eastern side of the park are far more popular than those on the western side of the park north of the Goingto-the-Sun Highway.

Many trails for hiking and mountain climbing open up scenes of unbelievable grandeur in the mountains behind the road. For those taking long trips, chalets are scattered every 15 to 18 miles through the mountains, and there are also tent camps in the back country. Accommodations for autoists exist outside of the park at Belton and Glacier 

Park Station as well as along the highways in the park. 

Grand Canyon Natl. Park in northern Arizona. Virtually two parks, as it is difficult under ordinary circumstances to cross from one rim of the canyon to the other. (It can be done, however, via mule-back across a swaying bridge just above the tossing waters of the gorge at a cost of $30 for a 12 days' trip or by aeroplane at $8.50.) Of the two rims, the South Rim is the more popular and is reached by railroad. 

A mile deep gorge, beautifully and fantastically eroded, extremely colorful, Grand Canyon is the best known of all the parks. The canyon extends far beyond the limits of the park: on the west to Mead

Lake, which was created by Boulder Dam; on the east to Marble Canyon, over which a bridge carries US 

89. In the vicinity of the park are numerous other sights including 

Boulder Dam, the San Francisco 

Peaks at Flagstaff, the spectacular 

Oak Creek Canyon, several national monuments, ancient cliff dwellings, and the Utah parks. 

Highways run along the rims of the canyon, in either direction from the small villages on both rims at which places are hotels, stores, and bungalows. While the canyon can be viewed very well from the villages, the highways offer additional views of the canyon. On the South Rim, the eastern highway to Desert View is the more scenic and interesting.

There are daily caravans led by rangers. 

Tourists who arrive by train or bus can see a great deal of the canyon via short walks. They are advised to withhold the purchase of sightseeing tours until they have reached the park and are in a position to decide whether they wish to get a more extended view of the canyon. At the South Rim, these sightseeing tours cost $3 for the morning trip westward and $6 for the afternoon trip eastward (or $7 for both). The usual full day's allexpense tour includes both trips and three meals; this costs $10. If you want to economize you can get lower cost meals than the all inclusive rate provides for. 11

Another good way to see the canyon is to ride a mule down to the canyon floor. (Rate from the South

Rim, lunch included, $6; from the 

North Rim, $5-$7.) It is also possible to hike part way down the canyon trails, but the park service discourages this, one reason being that hikers often interfere with the standard mule-back trip. At any rate, the descent should be attempted only by experienced hikers; at the best, it is a very hot trip, even though the canyon rim is cool. 

Every evening at the Hopi House on the South Rim, the Hopi Indians entertain by dancing. At night in the summer there are campfire talks, and at the South Rim also illustrated talks and other entertainment. The 

South Rim is open throughout the year; the North Rim is closed in late fall when the snow blocks the roads.

Grand Teton Natl. Park south of 

Yellowstone Park. Embraces the grandest portion of the Teton range, mountains of majestic peaks and deep canyons. While the highway through Jackson Hole offers fine views of the mountains, the only ways across them are by trail and by

Teton Pass to the south. The park adjoins dude ranch country, and there are dozens of opportunities for pack trips into the interior.

Jenny Lake, on the highway, provides rowboating ($2 a day) and motorboating (50 cents for short trips, $6-$10 for a full day's use of a motorboat). Horses can be hired for ascent of the trails to lakes, glaciers, and the high mountains which offer widespread views of Jackson 

Hole (most trips cost $5). Mountain climbing is available not only for those of great skill and experience but also for both those of moderate experience and beginners. 

Guides are available at $12 a day; beginners are urged to make use of their services even on easy slopes. 

The most difficult climbs, some of which take as long as five days, absolutely require guides; daily costs for a party of two climbers and a guide should come to $18-$20. in 

Lassen Volcanic Natl. Park north central California. A mountain park set around the only recently active volcano in the country. A road of thirty miles winds past Mt.

Lassen, through a country of steam vents, mud pots, and lakes. Before reaching Emerald Lake, provide yourself with a slice or two of bread. At this lake, rainbow trout rise out of the water to catch crumbs. 

Lassen Volcanic Park is primarily a resort in the southern part of the Cascades, a beautiful mountain country, and is better developed for vacationists than many of the resorts in the numerous national forests surrounding the park. Fishing and swimming are good. 

Visitors can take short trail trips from the main highway to a hot springs area (2½ miles, r.t.) and to the summit of Mt. Lassen (5 miles, r.t.). Both hikes require about three hours. If you can afford the time, visit Cinder Cone, where lava beds present an unusually beautiful sight. 

Cinder Cone, while in the park, can be reached only by a long detour. 

Mesa Verde Natl. Park in southwestern Colorado. A canyon region, in which, in the shelter of huge caves, remain hundreds of cliff dwellings constructed seven centuries and more ago. Only one of these can be seen without the help of a ranger; it is reached by a short footpath from the park headquarters.

Other Indian ruins (earth lodges, pueblos, cliff dwellings, and a Sun 

Temple) may be seen in free conducted tours, guided by a ranger, who heads a motor caravan. Two tours are offered: one at 8 or 10; the other at 1:30 or 3. In the evening (at 6:30 or 7) a caravan heads for Park Point, a peak of 8500 feet, from which height the colorful desert sunset is viewed. At night there are campfire lectures, and very often the Navaho Indians, if persuaded by small contributions, will sing and dance to native tunes.

Some of the best ruins can be 12

reached only by trail. Hiking and riding are much recommended, as this is a country of canyons, caves, great cliffs, etc. with considerable desert beauty. Various trips via horse cost $3.50 and $5.50. 

Mt. Rainier Natl. Park near Seattle, Wash. Mt. Rainier is the second tallest peak in the country. With its numerous glaciers, its gardens of wild flowers, and its sheer mass rising 11,000 feet from the surrounding countryside, it is spectacular. The park is used as a popular winter and summer resort by residents of 

Seattle and other nearby cities. 

Of the four entrances to the park, the two popular ones are Nisqually River Entrance, which provides access to Paradise Valley, and White 

River Entrance, which leads to Yakima Park. From another entrance, a road goes to Ohanapecosh Hot 

Springs; from the fourth entrance, to a public campground. No road encircles the park; to go from one entrance to another means proceeding via trail or a long detour. a- The country from either of the popular entrances is magnificent and worth seeing. Vacationists hike, fish, climb mountains. At Paradise 

Valley, the popular sport in the summer is "tin pants" sliding, which means sliding down the steep snow slopes of a glacier. Guides are vailable for other trips up glaciers and mountains and are available in many cases to beginners (rates, $2- $7 a trip). Mt. Rainier itself may be climbed but only by experienced climbers. It is a thrilling climb, over glaciers and crevasses. Also near

Paradise Valley are numerous nature trails and a lake on which there is boating. From Paradise Valley, from the country near it along the main highway, and from Yakima Park, many opportunities for extended hikes have been provided by the park service. From both Paradise

Valley and Yakima Park, various saddle horse trips are offered at prices ranging from $3.50 to $5. 

Winter sports include skiing and are offered from December to June. (All winter sports equipment can be rented.)

Olympic Natl. Park on the Olympic Peninsula in northwestern Washington. A mountain wilderness with glacier, flower-covered alpine meadows, impressive lakes, canyons, and jungle-thick forests. Created a national park in 1938 and greatly extended in area in 1940, the park has not been much developed and the interior is available principally to hikers, campers, and pack riders. 

The Olympic Highway, US 101, circles the park and goes through a small stretch of it. The Olympic 

Highway is one of the great scenic drives of the west, passing hot springs resorts, deep forests, and 

Indian villages. Along its route are many tourist cabins, inns, hotels, etc. 

To see the park via horse costs $3 a day for the hire of the horse and $6 for salary of a guide. A week's riding through the deep, beautiful wilderness costs $54 for all expenses per person in a party of five. A similar week's hiking tour would cost $36. 

Rocky Mt. Natl. Park, 85 miles north of Denver. Some of the finest mountain scenery in America is found within this park; for visitors from the east or the middle west it is the most accessible of the mountain parks. Its scenic attractions include many peaks above 10,000 feet in height, glaciers, and lakes.

For the short-time visitor, the outstanding feature of the park is the breathtaking 240 mile Circle Tour from Denver, one of the greatest mountain roads of any considerable distance in the entire world. The first part of the drive, after reaching 

Loveland, goes west through Big

Thompson Canyon, one of the most magnificent canyons in the country. 

In the park, the road climbs high into the mountains, to an altitude of 12,000 feet, from which point there are vast views. The road crosses the mountains to a beautiful blue lake, Grand Lake, a famous mountain resort, and returns to Denver 13

via High Berthoud Pass, close to the timber line, and also via the scenic 

Denver Mountain Parks. This trip generally takes two days, with the night spent at Grand Lake.

The two centers for visitors are Estes Park on the east and Grand Lake on the west, the former being more crowded with vacationists.

From Estes Park and Grand Lake, numerous short and long trails wind into the mountains to badlands, peaks, glaciers, and glacial lakes (guides are recommended for the longer trails). At Estes Park several short roads lead to scenic points.

Estes Park is a well developed tourist resort, with an 18-hole golf course, and tennis courts. There are numerous opportunities for fishing, hiking, riding (and winter sports). 

Visitors to Denver who are not taking the Circle Tour through Rocky 

Mountain Natl. Park are advised to take a tour of the Denver Mountain 

Parks, a memorable 65 mile trip. 

This costs $4. Another outstanding trip from Denver is to Mount Evans. 

It takes all day and costs $7.

Sequoia and General Grant Natl. 

Parks, on the western slope of the 

High Sierra, midway between San 

Francisco and Los Angeles. Known throughout the country for their groves of giant redwoods (some of which are 40 feet wide, a size few people visualize until they pace out the distance). Undue emphasis upon the trees, however, has caused travellers to overlook the amazing mountain scenery of Sequoia, a park where the strong beauty of the magnificent

Sierras is seen to even better advantage than at Yosemite. 

Sequoia and General Grant parks are quite close to each other, and all visitors to Sequoia are urged to take the loop highway which touches General Grant park. At Sequoia, in addition to viewing the big trees, a minor sight is to watch the feeding of the bears at Bear Hill. Drive out to the great monolith, Moro Rock, as grand a sight as famous El Capitan at Yosemite. Be sure to climb to the top of Moro Rock via the convenient stairway. From the summit there is a sheer drop of 4,119 feet (1,000 farther than the drop from

Glacier Point at Yosemite). The view embraces the high mountains of the Sierras and also the hot, level San 

Joaquin valley and the shimmering

Coast mountains in the distance. Another side trip that is advised is to Colony Mill (only nine miles from the Giant Forest). Here is a wide view, and a trail leads to a view of 

Marble Falls, which drops 2,000 feet in a series of seven waterfalls. 

Visitors who also go to General 

Grant park are urged to drive to

Park Ridge for an almost unparalleled view. The grandest portion of the entire Sierras is in the Kings 

River Canyon, just to the north of 

General Grant park. A state highway leads part way into the canyon from General Grant park; it should be taken as far as possible. (The 

Kings River Canyon is the nucleus of the proposed John Muir Natl. Park.) 

The entire Sierra Nevada range provides spectacular opportunities for pack trips. The trails at Sequoia to Kern River Canyon (another great scenic canyon) and Mt. Whitney, the highest point in the United States, are recommended. At General Grant park, pack trips into the Kings River Canyon reveal the great beauty of this region. A week's trip for a party of four would average about $10 a day a person. Short rides cost $3 and $4.

Yellowstone Natl. Park, mainly in northwestern Wyoming, with small stretches running into Idaho and

Montana. While Yellowstone is perhaps the most famous of the parks, its fame is more legendary than specific. What makes Yellowstone so popular is perhaps Old Faithful, the best known single spectacle in all the parks. There is no more fantastic an area in all the world, its only competitor, in New Zealand, offering its visitors a great concentration of geysers, but none of the other scenic attractions of Yellowstone. Yet to casual visitors Yellow- 14

stone often proves disappointing, as Americans tend to accept the potholes, the high flung geysers, the mud volcanoes, and the colorful springs without much thought as to the unusual nature of the spectacle. 

For this reason, it is perhaps more important to join the conducted parties and nature walks led by the rangers at Yellowstone than it is at almost any of the other parks, for Yellowstone, in most places, is not a park that overwhelms the visitor with its own magnificence. Such tours are offered free every day at the main points of interest. At night, at the various communities, campfire talks given by the rangers supply extra help.

Only two points in the park are so grand that they are sufficient in themselves. One is the Canyon of the Yellowstone River, a colorful, beautifully eroded gorge, made wildly spectacular by the huge waterfall at its upper end. The other, hardly of as great brilliance, is the view from the top of Mt. Washburn, 10,- 

317 feet high. Comparatively few cars (and none of the standard sightseeing tours) go up it. It is approached by a steep dirt road and should be attempted only by those whose autos have surplus power. 

Better than anything in the park for magnificence of country (except the canyon of the Yellowstone) are the approaches to the park. No visitor to Yellowstone should leave the park without at least visiting Shoshone Canyon on the way to Cody, and if time permits, he should also travel over the Red Lodge-Cooke 

City highway, and down to Jackson

Hole and over Teton Pass into Idaho. 

In the park, in addition to Old 

Faithful, the Canyon, and Mt. Washburn, the chief points of interest are the pinkish colored hot springs at Mammoth (these can be approached via a steep trail or can be viewed from a loop highway which winds above the springs), the rock of volcanic glass known as Obsidian Cliff (drive very slowly as it is approached, otherwise it is passed without being seen); some of the geysers and paint pots. The other standard sights are minor: a petrified tree that makes little impression on the bystander; Tower Falls, after the canyon falls of the Yellowstone River, an anti-climax; a natural bridge.

As Old Faithful erupts at intervals of 65 minutes, a good plan is to visit this section of the park at meal time so that if there is a long wait before the next eruption of the geyser, the time may be spent profitably. Shortly before the next scheduled flow of the geyser, a ranger delivers a lecture concerning the geyser.

The roads in the park are in the shape of a figure 8. The central road between Norris Basin and the Canyon of the Yellowstone does not reveal any sights of importance and is meant for ease in getting from one end of the park to the other. The western road is less scenic than the eastern road, a fact which can be considered when autoists enter from one gateway and want to leave via another gateway without duplicating too much of their trip. (If there is any choice about leaving via the northwestern gateway, the Gallatin 

Gateway will be found more scenic than the Gardiner Gateway.) 

Buffalo are sometimes seen from the road north of Mt. Washburn but bears are seen on all roads. They have developed a technique for halting cars and waddle up to the passengers begging for food. If you have nothing to give them they know instantly and tramp off to the next car. (The park service warns you not to feed them.)

The park is known also as a vacation resort, and Yellowstone Lake and Yellowstone River near the lake (particularly near the bridge) are great fishing waters. 

Yosemite Natl. Park on the western slope of the Sierra Nevada, 200 miles southeast of San Francisco. An all-year park, set in superb mountain scenery. The center of the park is Yosemite Valley, a high level floor at the foot of cliffs towering 3,000 15

feet. From the valley floor can be seen several waterfalls, one of which drops 1,430 feet in one sheer fall, and cascades farther for a total fall of 2,425 feet. Nearby are other waterfalls of considerable depth. (The falls are seen best in May and June; in July they are a drizzle, nonetheless spectacular; by the end of August, they are virtually dry.)

From the valley, various roads lead across the Sierras to the eastern slope of the mountains, to Glacier 

Point, 3,000 feet above the valley floor, and to the famous Mariposa 

Grove of Redwoods, where one tree is so huge a natural tunnel in it has been carved wider so that autos can drive through. The two tours to Glacier Point and the red woods are the popular side trips from the valley; they are convenient to a loop road so that the park may be entered via one gateway and left via another.

While the view from Glacier Point reveals the great beauty of the Sierras as the mountain tops seem to flow grandly into the distance, still better view can be taken from a little known half mile drive from Glacier Point (and thence a short climb) to Sentinel Dome, almost a thousand feet above Glacier Point.

One of the three great monoliths of the Sierras, El Capitan (the others are in Sequoia park and in Kings 

River Canyon) can be seen both from the valley floor and, still better, from Glacier Point. 

Another section of the park leads across the Sierras via a road built over a half century ago by coolie labor, who struggled to find a way into mine-rich Nevada. Only in the past few years has the road been improved, and the worst turns smoothed out. It is one of the most thrilling and scenic passes through the Sierras and worth taking, particularly if you are coming from the east via 

Reno (in which case, you can circle 

Lake Tahoe and pass Mono Lake, a mile high Dead Sea, in which the only life is a form of brine shrimp). 

If you can't take the entire trip over the mountains, do go as far as 

Tuolumne Meadows, a beautiful alpine field of mountain flowers in a setting of great peaks. (Autos may leave the Valley only on the even hour, 6, 8, 10, etc.) season

The park is at its greatest beauty in the spring when the waterfalls are terrifying sights and in the fall when the forests are in their brilliant autumn colors. In the winter, Yosemite is a famous winter sports center. 

But the summer is, of course, the of greatest activity. During the summer, there are free campfire lectures, free vaudeville, auto caravans, etc.; the climax of the night is the firefall, a huge ball of fire tumbled down the mountainside from 

Glacier Point at nine each evening. 

At the various hotels camp- grounds, and there are many stores, a swimming pool, a motion picture theatre, etc. Fishing is good. Deer are quite tame; hundreds of camera shots of them can be taken. Every evening at 8, the bears are fed in the valley. are Of the many trails, some are short ones that take only a few hours.

Some of the more spectacular ones climb high into the mountains, two of them to Glacier Point. (Taxis will take you from your camp or hotel to the foot of the trails; fare 25c a person.) During the summer, there is a seven day, 70-mile hike through the park under the guidance of a ranger. It starts every Monday in 

July and August and costs $4 a day.

For entertainment of other sorts, you can join riding groups (at $3 or $5). On Mondays in July and August, a six days' riding tour goes high into the mountains. At other times there are similar three days' riding tours. (The longer trips cost $46, the shorter $23.)

In the winter, sleighs take passengers around the Valley at rates of 50 cents to $1; horses with skijoring harness and rider are available for ski parties at $2 an hour. 

While there are more camps and hotels at Yosemite than at most of the other parks, advance reservations here are almost essential as the 16

parks are week-end resorts for San 

Francisco and Los Angeles. The park concessionaires operate offices in both San Francisco and Los Angeles, where reservations can be made. 

If you come from any other point, send in your reservations. 

Wind Cave Natl. Park in southwestern South Dakota. A limestone cave practically without stalactites or stalagmites, the chief feature of which is the currents of wind that blow in or out of the mouth of the cave. The highway is in a game preserve and numerous wild animals are seen frequently-large herds of buffalo and small groups of antelope, elk and deer.

As the cave is very cool (47 degrees), wear warm clothing. Of course use comfortable low-heeled shoes for the walk through the cave.

At the park the only accommodation is a free public campground, but many accommodations are available ten miles to the south at the resort city of Hot Springs. 

The Utah Parks, Zion Natl. Park, Bryce Canyon Natl. Park, Cedar

Breaks Natl. Monument (and the 

North Rim of the Grand Canyon, which, while in Arizona, is best reached from Utah).

Zion is known as another but more colorful Yosemite. Bryce Canyon is a collection of immense canyons, many greatly eroded into temples, spires, and minarets, all highly colorful. Cedar Breaks is a region of natural amphitheatres, eroded as much as a half a mile, and so unusually tinted that 47 different colors have been found; in effect, it is a series of Grand Canyons. No other part of America is so highly colorful as the desert area embraced by southwestern Utah, northwestern 

Arizona, and southeastern Nevada (where two virtually unknown state parks-Valley of Fire and Cathedral 

Gorge-rank with the grandest exhibitions of brilliant, even flamingly colorful erosion).

At Zion, the sights are the valley tour via a good highway, the trip into the mountains above the valley via the famous tunnel road, and the mile trail trip from the Temple of

Sinawava (the sibilant name given to a natural amphitheatre) to the 

Narrows, where cliffs rise abruptly from the river below (allow two hours for this trail). While the trail to the Narrows is of macadam, other trails in the parks are for strenuous hikers.

Bryce Canyon offers a fine road along the brink of the canyon with numerous spurs to the best viewpoints. Every afternoon in the summer, auto caravans tour the highway.

While Zion is an all-year park, Cedar Breaks, being higher in the mountains, is blocked by snow in the winter and Bryce Canyon is open only from April 15 to November 15. 

Canadian Parks 

Banff and Jasper Natl. Parks, on the Continental Divide, west of Calgary and Edmonton, Alta. Adjoining each other, they are now that the 

Banff-Jasper Highway is completed, virtually one park. Also connected with Banff and Jasper are Yoho and

Kootenay Natl. Parks, both generally considered part of Banff-in the mind of tourists, at least, even if the Canadian Government thinks otherwise. 

Banff's mountains have received great acclaim, but they are restful rather than grand. To imaginations that have been stunned by the awesome magnificence of Glacier Natl.

Park in the United States, they offer welcome relief, just as do the restful Litchfield and Berkshire Hills of New England. It is easier to live amid the temporal beauty of Banff than amid the unearthly grandeur of Glacier. 

The glory of Banff is its lakes -Lake Louise, jewel set in the mountains, and Emerald Lake, both dazzling in their glacial jade green. 

When you have seen these lakes, you have seen two of the world's grandest showplaces among mountain lakes. 17

Jasper is one of the most beautiful of all the parks, as it combines high peaks, canyons, waterfalls, and numerous glaciers, among which the Columbia Icefield on the Banff- 

Jasper Highway is outstanding. The 

Columbia Icefield, one of the most accessible glaciers in the world, is a real glacier-great ribbons of ice, SO completely different from the small snowfields known, in the United States, as glaciers. 

If time is essential, the best way to go is to drive on the plains to the east of the parks as far north as 

Edmonton and thence west to Jasper and south along the main highway. If time is not essential and you don't mind mountain driving, drive into the parks at Banff Village, head north to Jasper and return this way, leaving the park at Golden. At one time, this was an eventful way of travelling, but 1939 saw a great deal of road-building in this section so that once-terrifying trips through 

Windemere Canyon are now boulevard journeys. (There's no through road all the way from Banff to Vancouver. You can drive a good part of the distance but between Golden and Revelstoke, you must put your auto on a train.

The car will be carried for $12.50 up, and driver and passengers for $3.65 each. Eventually, a road will connect these points.)

The miscellaneous sights of the parks are these: in Kootenay Natl.

Park: Radium Hot Springs, where there is a fine swimming pool and a small community, and Marble Canyon, just a short distance from the main highway (however, while the road through Windemere Canyon has been paved, the road through Kootenay is very slow, poorly maintained with numerous sharp curves); in

Banff Natl. Park: Lake Minnewanka, where launches offer delightful trips, the hot springs and falls near the 

Village of Banff, and Johnson Canyon on the road to Lake Louise; Yoho Natl. Park: Yoho Valley with its natural bridge and falls; Jasper Natl.

Park: the totem at Jasper Station, the lakes near the town of Jasper, the Miette Hot Springs, Maligne Canyon and the potholes nearby, the evening drive from Jasper Village to 

Punch Bowl falls when much wild life (deer, moose, bear, mountain sheep and goat) is seen, and the beautiful Glacier of the Angels. The greatest sights of all of course are 

Lake Louise, Emerald Lake, and the

Columbia Icefield. The Banff-Jasper 

Highway is easily one of the grandiose motor drives of the world.

Projected National Parks 

These include three areas already set aside by the National Park Ser- Ivice as well as several sections which have been suggested on account of their great beauty. The latter include Hell's Canyon on the Idaho- 

Oregon border and Kings Canyon in California (see the discussion of 

Pacific Northwest resorts and Sequoia Natl. Park). Hell's Canyon is the deepest, weirdest canyon, the most magnificent canyon of them all, a wilder, grander sight than the 

Grand Canyon in Arizona.

The projected parks include Big 

Bend in Texas, an area of semi-arid plains, part of a proposed international park; Everglades in Florida, notable for the tropical jungles, the mangrove forests, birds and unusual fish; and Isle Royale, a resort island in Lake Superior.

Big Bend is extremely hard to visit; the only way of getting to this region without a car is to accompany the rural mail carrier who makes a weekly or semi-weekly trip. The Tamiami Trail crosses the Everglades but reveals more prairie than jungle. 

To see the Everglades well requires a boat trip from the Florida towns south of Coral Gables. The nucleus of the park, however, will be Royal 

Palm State Park, 45 miles southwest of Miami. Here is a wild-life sanctuary in which 140 varieties of birds have been identified; on the eastern end of the park is a region of luxuriant West Indian-type vegetation including towering giant roy- 18

al palms and great oaks. There is a lodge at the park.

Isle Royale is visited from Hough ton, Mich., Duluth, Minn., and the small towns on the Michigan shore. 

The usual visit costs $10 for the round trip and from Duluth takes 

12 days. From Michigan, there are some trips at $3.50. The shore of the island is fringed with small hotels and cottages catering to vacationists.

The interior is not developed, but several trails lead into the wilderness to points offering excellent views of the lake and to an ancient copper mine, believed to be the source of the copper used by the American 

Indians before the white man came.

Death Valley Natl. Monument, southeastern California. Larger than all national parks except Yellowstone. A winter playground southern California, consisting sand dunes, wide stretches of salt and alkali, intricately carved rocks of -a collection that offers many unusual days of sightseeing. Situated below sea level, the monument offers a view of Mt. Whitney, the highest point in the United States. National Recreation Areas 

Throughout the country are forty small recreational demonstration areas, often next to state parks and part of which they may eventually become. Those that offer vacation possibilities have been mentioned in connection with the state parks. 

The only recreational area developed for national use is Boulder Dam in 

Nevada, which backs up an immense artificial lake, Lake Mead, which extends to the upper reaches of the 

Grand Canyon, one hundred miles away. Most visitors view the Dam and go inside it for a view of the machinery that regulates the flow of the water to far-away Los Angeles. The next most popular thing to do is to take a short ride on Lake Mead. The ambitious take a 12 days' all expense trip to the Grand Canyon, spending the night in a tent.

THE NATIONAL FORESTS 

The national forests are many times more popular than the national parks, but for all that, they are the great unknown. More than thirty million people drive through the forests each year; more than twelve million make use of their recreational facilities. Yet most Americans, particularly easterners, have only vague notions as to what are the forests.

The national forests are different from the parks in both administration and purpose. The forests are under the supervision of the Department of Agriculture, while the parks are in the Department of the Interior. The parks are mainly great areas of scenic beauty, well developed for sightseeing and recreation. The forests are mainly utilitarian-they protect the watersheds of great regions of America, provide grazing lands for sheep and cattle, and, incidentally, offer recreational facilities. While extremely scenic in many cases, some of the most scenic sections as well as those noted for historic reasons are set aside in national monuments. In some cases, notably in the White Mts. of New 

Hampshire, Mt. Hood in Oregon, and 

Mt. Baker in Washington, the national forests are great scenic and resort attractions, while the forests near Los Angeles are crowded with vacationists.

In the United States, Alaska, and Puerto Rico are a total of 158 national forests, occupying 175,000,000 acres, mainly in mountainous country. The forests are in all sections of 

America, from east to west, north to south. It takes the National Forest 

Service 35 pages just to list the forests and their scenic and recreational facilities; an adequate description would cover at least 500 pages. 

The National Forest Service is act ively at work to encourage more vacationists. It has set aside over 2,000 improved camp grounds, where log shelters, camp fireplaces, comfort stations, sometimes even swimming pools, are available for campers (and in most forests, campers may pitch their tents wherever they wish).

They have granted permits to resorts and hotels so that many of the most scenic areas are available to tourists. Further, they welcome people who will build summer homes in the forests. 

Except in the White Mts. of New

Hampshire and Maine and the Wichita forest in Oklahoma, where no more sites are available, the forest service has or will set aside desirable regions where summer homes can be built. These sites, from one third acre to one acre each, are set in attractive country with good forest cover within convenient distance of fine recreation areas (but not within sight of highways or lakes, along fishing streams, near public use areas, or near scenic attractions). Sites rent for $10 to $25 a year, generally $15. The summer homes must be approved in design by the forest service and must cost at least $500, but the value of the leasee's labor, if he should erect the home himself, is considered. The homes must be occupied at least fifteen days during the year. If they are used for more than nine months of the year, rental will probably be somewhat increased. 28

The supervisor of the forest on which. you've set your plans will grant permits.

While great portions of the west are preserved in national forestsvirtually the entire Sierra Nevadas are national forests or national parks and most of Idaho is in national forests there are opportunities even in the crowded east to get home sites. 

Here the chances are more limited, yet Allegheny forest in Pennsylvania offers home sites at Ludlow and Arroyo, George Washington Natl. Forest in Virginia near Front Royal and 

Snowden, Monongahela in West Virginia at Middle Mountain, etc.

Activities at the forests include hiking, mountain climbing, saddle trips, often fishing and swimming; in the fall and winter, hunting and winter sports are common. Some of the forests have set aside especially wild regions, of great beauty, known as primitive areas, which are to be kept permanently undeveloped without roads or homes, eternally preserved in their wilderness character. Campers, however, may visit these regions. 

The best thing for any vacationist to do is to send away to the Forest 

Service of the Department of Agriculture at Washington, D.C., for the 

35 page booklet already mentioned.

It is called "National Forests-Condensed Recreation Data". A mimeographed booklet, it gives brief information about the scenic features of all the forests, an outline of the fishing, hunting, and other outdoor possibilities, and an account of the number of camp sites and private tourist developments in and near the forests. When you have that booklet, you can see the forests that are within your vacation orbit and can write to the local forest supervisors for specific data about the forests that interest you.

STATE PARKS 

Recreationally, one of the greatest advances in the last few years has been the amazing development of the park and forest systems in the various states. Stimulated by the great manpower made available by the CCC program, the area under state ownership has been enormously expanded, and considerable work done upon building trails, shelters, etc. This immense work and the tremendous acreage now available for recreation constitute one of the remarkable achievements of the past ten years.

Yet the publicity given to these park and forest areas has been woefully small. Scenically, some of the parks, like Gingko Petrified Forest

State Park and Dry Falls State Park of Washington or Cathedral Grove or the Valley of Fire of Nevada, are greater than many a national monument. Yet they are virtually unknown outside of their respective states. 

Even in respect to picnicking facilities, the parks have achieved not more than restricted local fame.

While residents of the Catskills, for example, might know of North Lake 

Campsite, a beautiful mountain lake fringed with great trees, yet New 

Yorkers only 3 hours away have never heard of its fine beach and its forest picnic grounds. Similarly, they know little about Lake Waramaug, only three hours from the city in 

Connecticut, or the dozens of other parks, forests, and campsites in a fifty to one hundred mile area of their city. 

For week end and vacation possibilities, the parks are amazingly popular despite their general lack of publicity. This is chiefly due to their moderate rates for housekeepng accommodations, most of the park cabins holding four to eight persons- 29

as that is, one or two families with children at weekly rates that run around $15-$20, and sometimes low as $45 a month. These rates compare most favorably with those of privately-operated housekeeping cabins and summer cottages.

In this vacation guide, the possibilities of these parks can be only indicated. The best plan is to write to the state park authorities of all neighboring states at the addresses given below for a full list of state parks and forests and the facilities there available.

Briefly, however, the major weekend and vacation possibilities are indicated below. Rates are given wherever available. RESORTS and RESORT AREAS 

In one sense, the entire country is a resort, for week-enders and vacationists go to any place at all which offers a chance to rest or exercise. Wherever there are a great many people, there too are a great many resorts. The task of finding each and every town where people vacation is too great even for the 

Federal Writers' Project, which is compiling monumental guide books to the American countryside. Even within the 30,000 pages that these guide books will eventually occupy, half the resorts are not mentioned and most of the others merely nodded to in passing, with no detail concerning them at all. 

Naturally, then, what follows is a selective list of the major resort areas, sufficiently described so that their chief appeal to vacationists is indicated. Detailed information concerning the scores, often hundreds and sometimes thousands, of places to stop can be obtained from the sources mentioned. For the western states in addition to other guides by all means provide yourself with the Shell guides to resorts, hotels and tourist courts, which in themselves are adequate price guides for the entire west. 

If you want to know where to golf send $2 to Golf Magazine, 52 

Vanderbilt Avenue, New York City, for their annual "Golfer's Year Book" which contains a list of golf courses in the United States and Canada, together with important information concerning them. 

A vacationist's guide to resorts and resort areas is naturally also a motorist's guide to places to visit and spend a day or two. The first axiom of all travel, of course, is that the longer you stay in one place, the less you need to spend per day. 

Most hotels offer a weekly discount, which runs anywhere from five to twenty-five per cent. For still longer stays, the best way to save is naturally to rent a furnished bungalow or furnished apartment, a fact mentioned principally for the visitor from the northeast, who has perhaps the least acquaintance with this means of reducing costs of anybody in the country. From the tourist standpoint, however, the most remarkable feature of the past few years has been the great development of auto courts, situated today not only along main highways but also on lake beaches, along a river, or at one of the thousands of springs (hot springs cold springs, and mineral springs) in America. For those who wish to save still more, housekeeping cabins, where full meals can be cooked, flourish everywhere today.

A Note as to Climate 

The first lesson about climate is that with few exceptions, North America is a hot continent during the summer. The inland plains are generally blazingly hot, and this applies from the Mexican desert to the wheat fields of Alberta. Alaska is generally warmer than New York. The coastal regions of both the Pacific and the Atlantic are cool only quite near the water. On the Pacific Coast, for example, there is easily a 20 to 30 degree difference in temperature between the coast and the orange groves.

The really cool sections are generally in the mountains of the west; the valleys between the many mountain ranges, however, are often too hot for comfort. Mexico, below famous "Thomas n'Charlie" on the Pan- 

American Highway, and all of Central America except the coasts are cool throughout the summer, as the altitude is high.

There is no place in America, except heated pools, where swimming can be enjoyed throughout the year. 

Southern Florida is comfortable in 41

the winter but too hot in the summer. The Gulf of Mexico is warm in the summer, perhaps too warm for energetic sport. The Atlantic north of southern Florida is uniformly cold during the winter; even the famous winter resorts from Jacksonville north are decidedly not comfortable until late spring. For real comfort, not a shivering time, Georgia and South Carolina become popular only well on to Decoration Day, which is generally the official opening day for most of the beaches on the Atlantic. Following the beaches north, there's a month's difference in degree of warmth from South

Carolina to New York. The Maine 

Coast is never really comfortable for swimming; bathers grit their teeth when they take their first plunge. 

Gaspe is virtually unbearable even in August. However, the Jersey 

Coast, much more turbulent than

Long Island, is on the whole considerably warmer than New York; the islands off Massachusetts-Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket-are warmer than Cape Cod, as they are nearer to the Gulf Stream; Northumberland Strait, which separates 

Nova Scotia and New Brunswick from 

Prince Edward Island, is perhaps the warmest of all the waters from 

Maine north. The St. Lawrence is cold throughout the Gaspe peninsula but near Montreal, the chill is gone. 

The Great Lakes are of course considerably more comfortable than the cold Atlantic, and this holds true of all inland lakes except those in high mountains, where the emerald green of the water from melting glaciers is warning to stay out. Fortunately, however, most of the northern mountains contain numerous hot springs-as many as fifty in the 

Montana mountains.

The Pacific Coast is coldest where it would seem to be warmest. That is in Southern California, where the waters are comfortable only in midsummer and then, only when the warm sun is not obscured by the almost daily fogs. The Olympic Peninsula, however, is on the whole more comfortable for swimming than 

Southern California, as the Japanese

Current, which makes southern Alaska warmer in winter and summer than most of the United States proper, gives a modicum of warmth to the Washington beaches. The great change in the pleasantness of Mexican Pacific waters begins south of the Gulf of California; here is an almost sudden change from cold northern waters to warm tropical waters.

Sarasota has one of the finest beaches in America, which unfortunately is far from town and requires an auto ride. 

At Miami and its neighboring towns, every kind of sport than can be imagined for a tropical holiday is available from horse or greyhound racing down to miniature golf. There are numerous places for shuffleboard, roque, chess, and checkers.

Jai Alai is played at Biscayne Fronton; tennis is free at most of the many municipal courts; golf costs $1 at most of the nine courses in 

Greater Miami ($3 at the Miami Bilt more, $2.50 at the Bayshore Golf 

Club, $3.25 at LaGorce Golf Club). 

Aside from such ways of spending one's time, the chief form of entertainment should be swimming (which is free at Miami Beach between Biscayne and 14th St., at Surfside Park at 71st St., and 25 cents at South Sea Island-appearing Tahiti Beach at Coral Gables). But actually all Miami is a show place. 

Its magnificent streets Biscayne 

Blvd., Flagler St., and Lincoln Road -are worth parading just as are the boulevards of Los Angeles. The large estates in Miami Beach and elsewhere and the beautiful homes, apartments, and hotels around Loomis Park are colorful and present ideas in architecture which are being tried out in the north (somewhat with garish effects, as the palm lined streets of Miami permit more brilliance than seems right for a shivering northern city). Down around the County Causeway Approach is another Coney Island. In 

Miami, no one should miss the City 

Curb Market (SW 2nd Ave. and 

Miami River), where tropical fruits and vegetables are displayed in colorful profusion. Other sights of 

Greater Miami are the University of 

Miami at Coral Gables, Bayfront

Park, the Municipal Yacht Basin (for trips in glass-bottomed boats), the

Miami Aquarium (built into a shipwrecked Danish barkentine-admission 25 cents), and the Musa Isle 

Indian Village in Northwest Miami, where every so often during the day men wrestle with alligators (admission 25 cents). 

Drives to the Everglades and over the highway to Key West must be included in a Miami itinerary. Airplanes and overnight boats run to Nassau and Havana. (No passport is required of American citizens; rates $18 r.t. to Nassau, $27.50 r.t. to Havana.)

In Miami are numerous auto courts, housekeeping apartments, tourist homes, inns, and hotels; virtually any price can be paid for accommodations from $1 up. Meals at the cafeterias are quite inexpensive, 47

and if you get a housekeeping apartment, you will find vegetables at rock bottom levels but meats higher priced than up north.

Hotel rates undergo several changes. They are low in the summer, higher in the late fall, still higher in December, and at their peak in January, February, and 

March. Rates sometimes double between Dec. 1 and Jan. 1. At the beginning of any busy year, the lowest priced single room with bath at the smaller hotels is about $20 a week. 

As there are hundreds of places to stop, consult the already mentioned booklet "Tropical Trips"; to be sure of accommodations make advance reservations. 

Gulf Coast. This term includes the entire coast from Pensacola west to

New Orleans and can be extended not only to Brownsville at the Mexican corner but also to Tampico. The region is featured by pleasant winter bathing and by warm summer bathing. The water is generally less rough than the Atlantic. Tampico has a beautiful wide beach, sloping gently. Living costs are quite low, particularly since the occupation of the nearby oil refineries by the Mexicans. Galveston has a long beach, quite pebbly in the downtown section, where the hotels and the many housekeeping cabins are found. 

Tampico and Galveston are too hot for summer vacations. The Gulf

Coast, however, generally refers to the small strip of Mississippi that juts out to separate Louisiana from Alabama. It is a summer resort (often a commuters' resort) for New 

Orleans and a winter resort for the north. The Gulf Coast is known as 

America's Riviera, although the term is woefully misused and disappoints those who come expecting Mediterranean beauty. It is a beautiful place, but not so beautiful as all that.

The main resorts are Pascagoula, Ocean Springs, Biloxi, Gulfport (an engineer's built city, with all streets carefully laid out, and hence SO standardized it lacks the imaginative carelessness of the other towns that just grew), Pass Christian, and 

Bay St. Louis. There are fine sand beaches along the entire coast, excellent swimming, boating, etc. 

Biloxi has two seasons, and is continuously occupied with vacationists from just before Christmas, when the winter vacationists arrive, to Labor Day, when the summer vacationists depart. It has a mild winter temperature and is somewhat cooled during the summer by trade winds. Numerous boats offer fishing excursions and in the summer, there is a regular boat service to the outlying islands. The summer, a gayer season than the winter, has many boat races and an annual regatta (in July). Golf (18 holes) at the Country Club, riding, many oyster bakes, community sings, concerts, bridge, tournaments, picnics, and such homey sports as horseshoes, croquet, and roque provide entertainment for all types of visitors. Practically all the hotels, apartment houses, and other accommodations for visitors are near the beach. 

Two good hotels charge $4.50-$7 a day (A.), $2.50 up (E.). Other hotels are $1-$2 (E.), $3 up (A.).

Apartments can be rented from $15 to $45 a month (see the Chamber of Commerce).

New York Adirondacks. The Ad irondacks, already described briefly under state parks, is an immense area with many vacation centers. Lake

George offers one of the most beautiful lakes in America, its shores lined with numerous camps, summer homes (some of imposing size) and hotels; Schroon Lake, to the east of Lake George, is a center of a popular family resort section; the southwestern section of the Adirondacks is an almost treeless plateau; the lake district from Old Forge to 

Cranberry Lake and thence east is a region of many boys' and girls' camps; Saranac is noted as a resort for the tubercular, with dozens of tourist homes available to the healthy at rates of $1 (E.p.); Lake Placid, in the western corner of the most spectacular portion of the entire Adirondacks, is primarily a winter resort, but even so, quite famous for its summer activities. There is hardly any more beautiful mountain country in the entire east than the Keene Valley and the nearby gorges and steep mountains (including Whiteface, up which a toll road-$1 a person-ascends). Summer activities at Lake Placid include not only the usual resort activities, but also an ice skating rink. Winter activities (the season opening December 

16) include ski-joring on Mirror 

Lake, outdoor and indoor skating, bobsledding on an Olympic bobsled run, and skiing in the nearby mountains (to which taxis and special buses take skiers). At Lake Placid is the only ski-jumping school in America. Cottages average about $3 a day (A.), inns, $4-$8 (A.); hotels charge from $5-$7 up (A.). For further information see "Holiday Haunts in the Adirondacks and Thousand

New York Catskills and Shawangunks. The Catskills and the adjoining Shawangunks are largely a region of family resorts, with hundreds of summer boarding houses, adult camps, and large hotels. Here is some of the most scenic country to be found within a short drive of a large city. For unexpected beauty, Kaaterskill Clove between Palensville and Haines Falls is without an equal, at one time, before roads were built through the Far West, being 'the most famous gorge in America east of the Grand Canyon. No more beautiful resorts can be found, either, in the entire east than Lake Minnewaska and Lake Mohonk, two summer communities set high upon the mountains. These are privately owned lakes, with private trails, private roads up the mountains (the dirt road up the western slope of 

Lake Mohonk Mt. is far more scenic than the paved road from New Paltz), plus tennis courts, a putting green (and, nearby, golf courses), facilities for canoeing, swimming, etc.

Rates average about $5 a day (A.p.) but some rooms go higher in price.

Appalachians. The Appalachians extend from Maine to Georgia. South of New York State, they are on the whole much wilder than in England, where the mountains have been developed into a vast resort region. From Central Pennsylvania south, the mountains are crossed by relatively few main highways, and some portions, particularly below the 

Mason-Dixon Line, are still isolated. Nonetheless, the Appalachians have become a popular vacation center, where any lake near one of the principal highways is a resort. The popular resorts include Frostburg and Cumberland in Maryland, Hot 

Springs in Virginia, Caesar's Head in South Carolina, White Sulphur 

Springs and Blackwater Falls in 

W. Va., Middlesboro in Ky., Monteagle, Gatlinsburg, Elkmont, and the high mountains (Lookout and Signal) behind Chattanooga in Tennessee (and Georgia). But the Appalachians in the south reach their principal tourist development in the mountain country of which Asheville is the center. Here are a score of resorts of varying importance. The outskirts of Asheville, Blowing Rock, Linville, Lake Lure and Chimney 

Rock are the principal ones; the minor ones include Waynesville, Lake

Junaluska, and Tryon (the last of these, a year-round resort where the emphasis lies upon the healthful, pleasing fact that "dew, fogs, and frosts are almost unknown here"). 

Blowing Rock gets its name from a curious stone perched above the 

John's River gorge. Wind currents sweep upwards so that light objects tossed into the canyon return to the sender. In August, during the horse show, Blowing Rock is a fashionable resort; the Mayview Manor here is one of the finest hotels in the mountain district and costs from $5 (A.). 

There are five other hotels, boarding houses, and both furnished and unfurnished cottages. At the Norwood Golf Club is an 18 hole course (greens fee, $2). Adjacent to Blowing Rock is a park with fine bridle paths, walks, and lakes; hikes can also be taken to several falls. 

Linville, at the foot of Grandfather Mt., offers mountain climbing and a scenic drive to Linville Falls, which drops 200 feet into one of the two spectacular gorges of North

Carolina-this one and Nantahala 

Gorge below Bryson City. At Linville are two golf courses (greens fee $2), a large cottage colony and three summer hotels ($3-$5 A. up).

Middle West

The Great Lakes. Wherever there is a beach, there too is a summer resort. Hence the entire coastline of the Great Lakes, from the St. Lawrence to Duluth, is virtually one continuous resort. Along the shores of 

Lakes Ontario, Erie, Huron, Michigan, and Superior are hundreds of communities of auto cabins, which advertise lake swimming, and there are scores of minor hotels and large summer hotels. Back of the Great 

Lakes, wherever there is another body of water, there as well is another resort. For the country to the south of the Great Lakes is one of the most crowded in all America. 

In their efforts to get away from the city, vacationists make a crowded resort of almost any place where 51

they can spend a pleasant day or two.

However, the extremely popular places, east to west, are Put-in-Bay and Catawba Island in Ohio (Catawba being quite beautiful with vineyards, peach orchards, and a rugged setting of stone cliffs), Steuben

County in Indiana (a county of 100 or more lakes), almost all of Michigan, but principally Mackinac Island and Isle Royale (the latter a national park project, and hence discussed in the section on national parks), the lakes in Wisconsin, and the Arrowhead District of Minnesota, the northwest portion of the state, shaped roughly like an arrowhead.

This entire area is mainly a family resort, where every possible type of accommodation is available, from tourist rooms and overnight cabins to good hotels. The sections within a few hours of the numerous cities are naturally jammed with office workers and families with children. 

The only regions of any national interest are the Minnesota Woods,

Mackinac, and the section already outlined: Isle Royale. 

Principal lakes in Steuben County, Indiana are Clear, Crooked, George, James, Snow, and Timmerson. Lake 

James, the largest one, is known principally for Pokagon State Park, which extends along its eastern shore. Here are good riding, tennis, golf, and swimming, and a unique feature of the park is a craft shop where you can sketch, try metal working or wood carving, design pottery, and otherwise see what talents lie dormant. In the winter, there are skating, skiing, ice boating, etc.

Potawatomi Inn, in the park, is a beautiful hotel, charging $3-$3.75 (A.p.). 

Also in Indiana is the swank health resort of French Lick, which provides not only mineral springs for recuperative purposes but all the features of any other first class hotel, and hence is quite fashionable. Rates run from $7 to $14 (A.p.). (Similar in character are other health resorts like the Greenbrier at White 

Sulphur Springs in West Virginia and the Homestead at Hot Springs in Virginia.) Life at a swank health resort is largely like life at any other resort, with golf courses nearer at hand, and the atmosphere-that of a well run hostelry-a greater boon to a pleasant time than practically anything else.

In Michigan, the prime resort is 

Mackinac (pronounced Mackinaw)

Island. It stands in the Straits of 

Mackinac and is a tourist haven for 

Great Lakes boats, as popular with 

Great Lakes vacationists as was Bermuda to ocean travelers. Like Bermuda, it is a combination of quaintness and modernity, with several precious conceits, which mark it off from other resorts, giving it a touristy air. Here, as in Bermuda, transportation is via your own feet or carriage and bicycle as no automobiles may venture upon the island. 

The temperature is comfortable, and the island has a reputation for being free from hay-fever. Activities are the usual ones of any summer resort, plus tours of the old fort on the island which dates back to Revolutionary days, with blockhouses, guardhouse, dungeon, and ramparts awaiting inspection. Cottages, boarding houses, and hotels run from $1.50 -$3 a day (E.), $4-$5 up (A.); the largest hotel is the Grand ($7-$12.50 

A.p.). Fare from Mackinaw City to 

Mackinac Island is 60 cents o.w., $1.20 r.t.

Above Mackinac on the Upper Peninsula of Michigan is one of the better types of Michigan woods resort, Blaney Park, 22,000 acres devoted to the conservation of wild life and recreation. A complete resort, it of fers golf, swimming, tennis, fishing, dinner dancing, picnics, boating, etc. all in the rates ($5 up A. at the lodge and cottages, $7 up A. at the Celibeth). 

In the Arrowhead District of Minnesota, however, the deep woods with their many lakes and streams supply both family-resort vacations and wilderness vacations (see the 52

section on types of vacations). One region in the Arrowhead District which combines both is Lake Vermilion. Thirty-five miles long, with many bays, 65 islands, rugged cliffs, and sandy beaches, it provides good fishing, boating, and swimming and nearby are golf courses and hiking trails. Some of the cottages and cabins are not easily reached by road, and boats go to these points, also taking excursionists. Canoeing far to the north is popular, and long trips extend well into Canada. At the city of Tower, Birch Point Inn charges $4-$5 a day (A.p.), $25-$28 a week (A.p.), while housekeeping cabins here cost from $6 to $18 a week. 

A fishing camp on the lake offers cottages at $18.50-$21.50 a week (A.p.), and housekeeping cabins are also available. 

The small resorts along the beaches of the Great Lakes and the lakes in the back country generally attract residents from nearby towns as well as motorists who want a place to spend the night and hence stop at the first group of cabins that looks inviting. Plains and Mountain States. Here is one of the greatest areas in America, the broad country formed by 

Kansas, Iowa, Nebraska, the Dakotas, and Wyoming. Near the Mississippi, it is a land of rich farms, and Iowa around the northeastern section has local fame as a resort. 

Aside from their parks, most of these states, however vast, offer the vacationist very little. North Dakota entertains hunters in the fall; South

Dakota offers a pleasant mountain resort only in its far southwestern corner; Wyoming, aside from its parks and its dude ranches, all discussed elsewhere, is mainly a plains state with mile after mile of sage brush. 

Montana and Idaho. The third largest state in a country in which great size means immense distances, that's Montana. How goes the old ditty: "The sun done riz, the sun done set, an' we ain't outa Texas yet"? 

With only a sacrifice of rhythm you can moan the same complaint about two other states, California and Montana. And just like Texas and California, Montana is also a huge empire of many different lands. Near the North Dakota border are some 54

outcroppings of the badlands, and it was in these hills and across the border that Theodore Roosevelt learned to love the west. Near Idaho, the mountains begin, but for almost a thousand miles, there is nothing but undulating prairie, hot and often barren. But that strip near Idaho is rich with high peaks and tall timber, an integral part of the great and promising Pacific Northwest. 

The main resort in Montana is 

Glacier park, and one of the main industries is dude ranching in the 

Beartooth Mts. Two resort lakes are in the western mountains, Beartooth

Lake and Flathead Lake. The latter is long and wide but too cold for swimming, as it is fed by melting glaciers. Boating and fishing are the popular occupations; pack trips into the wilderness are outfitted here. 

The chief towns are Lakeside and 

Polson, the latter with a nine hole golf course. Cabins are available at both Beartooth and Flathead 

Lakes; at Polson there are also two hotels, charging $1 a day (E.p.). 

Idaho is almost entirely a national forest. The mountains are high, the forests blanket most of the state, and only along the Oregon 

Trail are there any considerable stretches of prairie. Bear Lake, half in Utah, is a local recreational center for visitors from Salt Lake City, and 

Mack's Inn along the Oregon Trail has more than statewide fame as a place to stop. In the northern strip of Idaho, chief resort lakes are 

Pend Oreille and Hayden, to which flee thousands from Spokane, that splendid city all too firmly planted right in the torrid belt of 

Washington. At Hayden, the famous inn is the Bozanta Tavern, which charges $4 a day up (A.), $1.50 up (E.). Cabins and housekeeping cabins are of course available. Coeur d'Alene Lake, one of the most beautiful in the west, is not well adapted to vacationists, as only a few cabins for short visits are available along its shore.

The Payette Lakes were, until Sun 

Valley eclipsed everything else, the most prominent resort lakes in all the state. High in the mountains, their shores fringed with evergreens, the lakes are beautiful and offer all winter and summer activities.

The chief town is McCall, from which point aeroplanes reach Hell's Canyon in twenty minutes. Take that airplane service, and you'll have seen the most magnificent natural sight in America, completely awesome and grand, deeper than the Grand Canyon, with breath-taking vistas, multicolored crags and pinnacles. And as extra measure, you'll also be taken just a bit farther to the fourth deepest canyon in America, the Grand 

Canyon of the Little Salmon. 

Cabins at Payette can cost anything from $3 a day to $10, $18 a week to $60, the rates quoted at 

Sylvan Beach. Some of those lowest priced cabins offer housekeeping facilities; the expensive ones are five room lodges. 

Sun Valley is an American version of St. Moritz; it is the one resort in all the country to which anybody with a yen for the fashionable wants to go. It entertains everybody from movie stars at $25 a day to college men bunking four to the room at a dollar a head. In the summer, which is the popular season despite Sun Valley's original design as a winter sports center, the resort offers riding, swimming at two pools, golf, tennis, archery, cycling, canoeing, skeet-shooting, fly casting, fishing, and pack trips into the wilderness; in the fall there is hunting as well. In the winter and spring (heavy snow lasts until very late in the season), it offers skiing, skating, bobsledding, sleigh riding behind reindeer, horses, or dogs, ski-joring, riding, and outdoor swimming in a heated pool. Its summer climate is comfortable; its winter climate is none too cold as the neighboring mountains turn back the sharp winter winds.

Sun Valley is reached without extra charge to Union Pacific through passengers. To others, a side trip from Salt Lake City costs $6.75 55

(coach), $7.60 (tourist pullman), $8 (standard pullman). At the Valley, you can stay at any of four lodges or inns. Rates are below: 

Sun Valley lodge, open in summer and winter only, $4.50 (E.s.), $6 (E.d.) and up in the winter, $8 (E.s.), $11 (E.d.) and up in the summer. Challenger Inn, open all year, $3 (E.s.), $4 (E.d.) and up. 

The Cottage, generally set aside for large parties, $6 (E.s.), $7.50 (E. d.). The Chalet open throughout the year except when there are tournaments, $4-$5 a room, whether one, two, three, or four share the room.

Meals at the cafes and restaurants start at $1.75 a day. 

Miscellaneous rates are these: pack trips, approximately $15 a day; skating in the summer, 50 cents, in the winter, 25 cents; skeet shooting, $2; ski lifts, $3 a day, $12 a week; bobsleds, sleighs, and cutters, $4-$8 an hour; dog sleds, $3 an hour; ski-joring, $2.50 an hour; riding, $1.50 for the first hour. Swimming, however, is free.

In the summer the Union Pacific provides all-expense trips of 4 days for $53.

For further data about Sun Valley, write to the Union Pacific R.R. at 

1 S. La Salle St., Chicago. 

Blue and Wallowa Mts. Just across the Idaho border are these snowcapped mountains, a portion of the Rockies, with numerous glacial lakes of which Wallowa is the largest. 

Here activities include riding, pack trips into the mountains, fishing, swimming, boating, golf, tennis, etc. 

From Wallowa Lake, you can travel over a forest road via Enterprise and Imnaha to Hell's Canyon, a region designated to be set aside one day as a national park. Also nearby is Pendleton, whose annual roundup in September is the leading rodeo of the northwest. At Wallowa

Lake are numerous cabins at rates that run from $1.50 up to $7 a day.

The Wallowa Lake Hotel starts its rates at $1.50 a day. 

Cascades. Except for the Blue and 

Wallowa Mts., the Pacific Northwest from Idaho to the Cascades is a vast, endless desert, hot, ugly, desolate, part of the Great American

Desert, which covers virtually all the west from western Kansas to the 

Pacific Coast, from the plains of Canada to the tropics. The only touches of green are those that follow in the wake of irrigation projects or those found high in the mountains.

The Cascades are high and beautiful, thick with forests. They rise so far they block the winds that might carry water to the uncomfortable prairies, but their own great size and well watered slopes are of such beauty that the vacationist at least pardons them the desolation to the east which they have caused. 

The national parks of the Cascades are discussed elsewhere; in addition these mountains offer other splendid resorts like Lake Chelan, Mt. 

Baker, Big Four Glacier, and Mt. Hood. 

Lake Chelan, 60 miles from one end to the other, is in a setting of mountain gorges and glaciers as beautiful as a movie wilderness.

The town of Chelan lies at the lower end of the lake in a broad valley; around the lake are resort hotels, cabins, and camp grounds. Rates start at $1 a day (E.p.), $3.25 (A. p.). 

Big Four Glacier, north of Seattle, is a beautiful ice covered peak, at whose foot is a good resort type lodge ($3 E., $8-$9 A.d.). Mt. Baker still farther north lies amid snowcapped mountains, glaciers, and flower covered alpine meadows. It is designed for hiking, fishing, mountain climbing, glacier trips, swimming, boating, riding, and general relaxation in a region of mountain beauty; it is also a winter sports area. Nearby are many hotels, lodges, and cabins with rates starting at $1 a day (E.p.). This includes Camp

Shuksan in the forest, where a double cabin rents for $2. At Mt.

Baker is a lodge, offering accommodations at cabins and cottages at daily rates of $4.50-$5.50 (A.) and at an inn at $6-$7 (A.). 56

Mt. Hood, reached via the scenic 

Loop Highway from Portland, is a favorite winter and summer resort for residents of that city; Timberline Lodge here, since its completion by the W.P.A., is getting national fame. The Lodge has some reputation as the most luxurious mountain resort in the world, which is naturally exaggerated but indicative of its excellence. Rates are $3.50 (E.s.), $5 (E.d.) but dormitory accommodations for men or women are available at $1.50 (E.p.). Meals can be obtained at a coffee shop or a dining room. A full day's all-expense tour from Portland costs $11.

Farther down the mountain side is Government Camp, an all-year tourist camp with both cabins and hotel, offering activities much the same as those at Timberline Lodge.

Rates at the Battle Axe Inn are $3 up (A.), $1.50 up (E.). 

San Juan Islands. Here are 172 islands scenically worth visiting, particularly the state parks, Deception Pass and Moran, the latter park enclosing Mt. San Juan and Orcas islands are vacation resorts with many cabins and hotels. The rugged coastlines and the intricate waterways offer beautiful country and there are sandy beaches plus opportunities for fishing. British Columbia has little fame as a resort center; only Victoria has any tourist interest, as it looks like a city snatched from Great Britain and transplanted here. Arizona and New Mexico are considered in the southwest, although

Reno and the cottages along the Nevada shores of Lake Tahoe, both of which are also discussed elsewhere in this book (the dude ranches in the section on dude ranches, Lake

Grandaddy Lakes to the east of the city in the Uinta Mts. 

Arizona is divided into two main divisions, a high northern plateau and the low southern desert. The principal resort of the north is naturally Grand Canyon, although Prescott is striving to get fame as a center for winter sports, as the snow falls heavily up here in those months when the warm, dry desert to the south brings a great tourist harvest to Phoenix. 

Phoenix is a beautiful town of wide, palm-lined streets and redroofed, Southern California style houses. Surrounded by miles of irrigated desert, it offers the one pleasant spot in all the long stretch from 

San Antonio to the Imperial Valley 57

of California. Its mild winters are making it an ever-increasingly popular resort for easterners who flee from the prospect of a Florida winter. 

Activities include golf, tennis, badminton, shuffleboard, fishing (in nearby mountains and lakes), riding in the desert and up into the mountains, etc. Hotels start their rates at $1 a day (E.p.); the swank winter hotels are the Arizona Biltmore ($14-$20 a day A.), Camelback 

Inn ($10-$16 a day A.), and Jokake 

Inn ($12-$15 a day A.).

New Mexico is mainly a summer resort, despite its desert to the south, which has not been greatly developed for tourists. Santa Fe, one of the most beautiful cities in America, is high in the Sangre de Cristo Mts., cool in the summer (but often quite freezing in the winter). An old Spanish city, Santa Fe clings to its ancient customs and traditions and so preserves the atmosphere of a foreign community. The place is ideally situated for relaxation; the industrious can fish in the nearby mountains, hike, take pack trips, etc. The city is beautiful in late summer and early fall; over Labor Day week-end the three day La Fiesta de Santa Fe takes place (don't visit it unless you come dressed in a large sombrero; serape, and huaraches). Hotels start at $1-$1.50 (E.p.); the best ones are

La Fonda ($3 E.s., $5 E.d. and up) and the Santa Fe Inn ($8-$12 A.). 

Outside of town is the well-known 

Bishop's Lodge ($5 A., $2.50 E.).

Even an auto court at Santa Fe has a swimming pool (Orchard Auto 

Camp, $1 up E.). 

Nearby is Taos, really three communities, one the Indian pueblo, now a tourist sight of some fame, second, the church at Ranchos de Taos, one of the places to visit, and Fernando de Taos, the art colony which D. H. 

Lawrence and Mabel Dodge Luhan have made immortal. The Sagebrush

Inn is the popular hotel here ($3.50 up E.s., $5 up E.d.), and there are excellent tourist courts. 

Whenever you visit the southwest, be sure to visit some of the canyons, natural bridges, Indian ruins, and Spanish missions that make this a land apart. In August, view the Indian ceremonial at Gallup (last 

Wednesday to Friday of every August) and the Hopi Snake Dance (at a different place each year; information about the current year's time and place available from the United 

States Travel Bureau at San Fran cisco). For further data, write for "Handbook of Old Santa Fe," published by the Santa Fe Press, Inc., Santa Fe, N.M., "Roads to Cibola" (what to see in New Mexico) issued by the State Highway Comm., Santa 

Fe, N.M., "Two Weeks in New Mexico," obtained from the New Mexico

California. California of course is not a state but a state of mind. Its northern counties, distinctly a part of the Pacific Northwest, with deep forests and tall mountains, the fantastically spectacular Sierras on the east, self-conscious San Francisco and the Bay area, and the incredible 

Southland make up four separate tourist sections. 

Vacation interest in the northern counties centers around the many national forests, in which numerous resorts for quiet vacations have been built. Lassen Volcanic Natl. Park is a good northern type resort.

In the Sierras, the western slope presents three national parks, Yosemite, Sequoia, and General Grant, all of which are virtually week-end resorts for the big cities of the state.

On the eastern slope, the nationfamous resort is Lake Tahoe, a deep lake of great size, its shores lined with dozens of cottages and hotels.

It offers every kind of summer and winter activity. More expensive than most of the western resorts, $3 a day (A.) will, however, get some accommodations. Some of the best known lodges charge $4.50-$5 up a day (A.), $30-$35 a week (A.) or $20 a week for hskg. tents, $25-$50 for hskg. cabins. 58

North and south of San Francisco are hundreds of family resorts, principally in the Russian River District, in the hot springs region around Calistoga (where there are more springs than in all Europe), in the big trees country, and on the beaches around Santa Cruz. Such resorts have only local interest, but on the Monterey Peninsula, the San Francisco area presents America with its most beautiful coast, the only place in 

America where anything as colorful as the Riviera can be seen. Paradoxically as well, here is also a rugged coast scenically superior to anything in the new tourist kingdom of Gaspe.

Carmel is the principal tourist community on the peninsula, Monterey its chief city, and Del Monte its world famous resort. In all 

America there is no more beautiful setting for a resort. It consists of two hotels: Del Monte Hotel outside of Monterey ($10 a day up A.) and 

Del Monte Lodge outside of Carmel at Pebble Beach ($8 a day up A.). 

At Carmel itself, rates run from $1.50-$4.50 (E.), $5.50-$8 (A.). No one motoring in this region should neglect the 17 Mile Drive, where 

American scenery at its colorful best is found. At Pebble Beach or Pacific Grove, take a glass bottom boat to the marine gardens.

The Southland is the land of the impossible, so lusty and fanciful it attracts visitors from all ends of the globe. All of its major towns are summer resorts, San Diego with its suburb La Jolla the most beautiful, but the others, Santa Barbara, Santa 

Monica, Long Beach, and Laguna 

Beach (an artist's town), all colorful and brilliant with bougainvillae, hibiscus, and flowering eucalyptus. 

Winter resort cities include San 

Diego, Santa Barbara, and the inland communities like Riverside, which are unbearably hot in summer. 

Rates go up in the winter; good hotels, however, can be had for $2.50- $3 a day (E.d.), cabins for $1-$1.50 (E.p.). 

The principal resorts of national interest in the coastal sections are 

Lake Arrowhead in the mountains east of Los Angeles and Santa 

Catalina Island. Lake Arrowhead, both a family resort and a movie actress' hideout, has pleasant surroundings with many cottages, auto camps, and hotel. The dude ranch here charges $1 (E.p.) for a nonhskg. cabin, $1.50 up for a hskg. cabin, $25 a week (A.p.); horse is $10 a week extra. The Lodge charges $3-$4 up (E.p.), $6-$7 up (A.p.). 

Catalina is a beautiful, well developed tourist resort off the coast. 

Reached from Wilmington at a cost of $3.50 for the boat trip ($4.25 from 

Los Angeles for both the boat trip and the red trolley from the city to the port). Autos may not be taken to the island but may be stored at the pier at 50 cents a day. While it is a year round resort, the island is most popular in summer. 

The boat docks at Avalon, the main settlement and the recreational center. Fourteen miles away by water, 22 by road is the Isthmus, where a conscious effort is made to get a South Sea Island effect in all structures. 

At Avalon, every type of accommodation from a camp, bungalow court, or apartment house to both inexpensive and luxury hotels is available. The fashionable St. Catherine charges $3 (E.) in winter, $5 in summer. Other hotels start at $1.50 up (E.). Apartments rent for $30-$50 a month. $3 will get a bungalow at a court most times of the year.

The Avalon Boardwalk skirts the harbor and is the principal recreation center. Numerous tours are offered via boat and bus. Everybody takes the glass bottom boat to the marine gardens (75 cents) and a lot take the evening flying fish trip (April-Oct., 75 cents). Other good trips are to the seal rock, where you get a close view of several hundred sea lions (50 cents); to the Isthmus (all the way by boat, $1, all the way by bus, $3, combining boat and bus, $2.50); and to the bird park (reached 59

only by bus). All kinds of activities are provided on the island: golf ($1), swimming, dancing at the Avalon 

Casino, etc. plus hunting (principally for wild boar, for which the Channel Islands are noted, and also for mountain goat and quail; charge is $10 a day including guide and transportation). 

The desert resorts of California are chiefly Death Valley Natl. Monument and Palm Springs. The latter is an oasis on the desert, a smart winter resort frequented by movie stars, who go to the El Mirador Hotel ($12 up A.). However, there are also numerous other hotels and bungalows; prices start at $3 a day (E.), $5-$6 (A.). Everybody wanders around in shorts and on bicycles, and the ambitious ride to Palm Canyon or on the desert. To help you spend a boisterous evening, there are many night clubs. Other desert resorts include Twenty-nine Palms, whose fame zoomed high in 1940, when thousands came here. It has many hskg. cabins and two winter hotels charging $5-$7 a day up.

Fur further information about 

Californian resorts, get the summer or the winter vacation catalog (of resorts) published by the Peck-Judah Travel Bureau, 672 Market St., San Francisco.

Eastern Canada (including the 

Gaspe). Newfoundland, even if Americans fondly think so, is not part of Canada, but a Crown Colony. For vacationists, it is not well developed, although it offers amazing fishing. 

The Maritime Provinces (Nova Sco tia, Prince Edward Island, and New 

Brunswick) are rural communities, generally pleasant to look at, as is any rural section, but their vacation possibilities are largely restricted to excellent fishing and, in the warm 

Northumberland Strait, to swimming.

The islands just northeast of the 

Maine border get numerous American tourists, particularly Campobello 

Island as President Roosevelt's sum mer home is here. A swank resort was built by the Canadian railroads at St. Andrews, not far north of the 

Maine border, and Yarmouth in Nova Scotia is noted as a week-end resort for ship travellers from Boston and New York (such a trip coming to about $20 from Boston, $30 from New York). Halifax, as long as visitors are allowed, is a remarkable place to spend a night or two, as the British use it as an outfitting port for their many convoys across the ocean and the city is gay with men in uniform. Grand Pre, not far from Halifax, is known for its romantic association with the 

Acadians of Longfellow's poem. Syd ney and smaller ports are picturesque as fleets of fishing boats add a colorful touch to the harbors. 

(From Sydney to the Prince Edward 

Islands, there is a fine boat trip through a series of lakes; taking 4 days, it costs only $18 for transportation, meals, and berth.) Also at the top end of Nova Scotia is the famous 

Cabot Trail, a difficult, winding road through Cape Breton Natl. Park, passing close to the shore where there are good seascapes and through pleasant valleys. Prince Edward Island is mainly an island of farms, offering a view of hardly another culture as you see in French Canada, but of another adjustment to the fierce cold that makes life in the 

Maritime Provinces so different from anything in the warmer, more blest

U.S. 

Eastern Canada, however, is mainly famous for the Gaspe Peninsula.

It is the extreme eastern end of huge, sprawling Quebec Province. 

The Gaspe Peninsula was the first part of Canada discovered, and the last rescued from isolation. Therein lies its fame. All Quebec is French 

Canada, but the Gaspe is still so 

French, it's like travelling in bleak 

Brittany. If your auto breaks down in the rural sections, you talk sign language with the sympathetic looking attendant at the gas pump, while his children cluster around, too shy to do more than look. If you've lost your way, the children on the street 60

might help you out if you unfold a road map and let them point which way to go (and you'd better tip them at once or else they'd unashamedly ask for it-this is New France!). 

Plenty of flags are out too, but they are not British. France gave these villagers a flag a few centuries ago and the natives see no reason to change.

English is spoken at the hotels and restaurants. But it's English with a Scotch burr (as many a Scotsman came to Maritime Canada in the last few years) or English with so French an accent you often have to do a lot of translating now and then just to get the drift of things. 

Scenically, however, the Gaspe has little to offer. There is nothing on the southern shore. The northern shore is rugged with a few interesting seascapes; numerous towns line the road and besides the houses are many outdoor ovens. The road on the southern shore is in wretched repair; on the northern shore, the holes have been filled in, but the highway twists so badly and is so narrow as it heads straight up a mountain side that it becomes downright dangerous. And if that weren't bad enough, dog carts, even though prohibited by the government, jog ahead of impatient autoists.

Perce is the chief tourist town of the Peninsula. The pierced rock after which the town is named is unblushingly referred to in the guide books as the ultimate of the Gaspe tour, which is like calling Morro 

Rock, over 3,000 miles away, the ultimate of a California tour. The most interesting thing at Perce is the bird sanctuary offshore on Buenaventure Island. Don't miss it, even though $4 is the standard rate for a boat to take you there, whether you have one or four in your party. (When the town is crowded, you can often make up a large party from the many tourists and thus cut costs.) Perce is the most expensive village on the Peninsula, as every tourist spends at least one night here. $2.50 is about the min- imum for a tourist cabin in July and the rates often go higher in August, when the town is so jammed you have to arrive early in the day or make advance reservations-or be chased away to some far away town. 

Nearer the city of Quebec is the resort town of Metis Beach, the most lovely village on the entire peninsula, as it is the only place where the bleakness of Maritime Canada has been hidden by well planned gardens. It is the only town on the 

Peninsula which does not reveal the heart-breaking struggle of the Gaspesians in getting a living from a rocky soil, in a country of short summers and much fog. Metis Beach is quite French but modern, and its attractions for vacationists include golf, tennis, swimming, and even deep sea fishing, as the St. Lawrence is so wide here the opposite shore lies below the horizon. The 

Boule Rock Hotel is a pleasant place, with a heated salt water pool, and charges $3 to $5 a day (A.p.). 

A railroad covers the entire south shore of the Gaspe going to Perce

It's still popular, with tens of thousands annually piling their outfits into automobiles and driving off to the mountains, lakes, or streams even to the beaches. When everybody went camping in the 1920's, before the advent of tourist homes and tourist cabins, it was an adventure 67

to find a good place to camp, and you had to make friends with a farmer before he'd let you camp on his property. Today, the various state governments and the forest and park services of the national government offer thousands of attractive camp sites. Nothing did more to stimulate the re-birth of camping and hiking enthusiasm than the CCC boys, who built forest roads, camp sites, and trails. Today, you can be sure of an extremely pleasant place to pitch your tent, near good water, with every convenience nearby.

The national forests offer numerous camp sites-well into the thousands all over the United States. Some of the most popular places have been set aside as Recreation Areas, where great numbers come. The State Parks of well over half the states also cooperate with campers, New York

State in particular, which has developed some very fine sites in the most beautiful locations in the Catskills and the Adirondacks.

A camp outfit for two should consist of a 9x11 tent, cots, gas stove, gas lamp, table, chairs, hatchet, cooking utensils, and tableware. Of course, you will take along fishing rods, bathing suit, and rough clothes for hikes into the back country. You don't need to own your own equipment. Many stores which sell camp outfits will rent them. Perhaps in your own city some stores which sell camping equipment will rent it to you if you want to test out this idea before spending a lot. 

The Barbour Co. of 3525 South 

Figueroa in Los Angeles, for example, will rent an outfit for two for $5.00 a week (two weeks minimum). Or at Lake George in New York State are two outfitters, Jerry Grussner and Craig-Walker Co., who will rent you not only a complete camping outfit for two persons, but also a canoe at the rate of $30 for two weeks from 

July through Labor Day, and $20 for other two week periods from May through October. On the shores of 

Lake George is a large state camp site, and many of the islands in the lake are available for camping, (The ambitious can take a canoe trip from 

Lake George through Lake Champlain to Montreal.) At one or two of the national parks, you can rent tent equipment; at many of the western ones, you can rent a tent already erected for you.. 

Food costs should be no more than $1 a day for two persons. Thus a two weeks' camping vacation in the mountains of Southern California should not cost more than $30 at the most for two persons, or $7.50 a week a person; or a two weeks' camping and canoeing vacation at 

Lake George should not cost more than $50 for two or $12.50 a week, even in mid-summer.

TRY HIKING 

Hundreds of thousands do it in 

Europe during quiet years between wars and not only do they seem to have an excellent time, but Americans actually cross the ocean for the chance to tote a knapsack through the quiet lanes of the French countryside or into the Alpine passes. 

The tradition of hiking isn't as well established in America. At present, only Boy Scouts are supposed to do it, but the records will show that the national forests and state parks get thousands of hikers who are well along into their adult years.

The American Youth Hostel move ment, about which more later, has done a great deal to make hiking an American occupation. But even more has been the great help of the established foot trails in the East and the West. The Appalachian Trail runs from the wild central region of Maine, from its tallest peak, Mt. 

Katahdin, for over 2 thousand miles south, along the crest of the many ranges which make up the Appalachians to Georgia. It goes through numerous national forests and two national parks, and passes over the most scenic portions of the east, with the sole exception of the Adirondacks. (However, New York State established a number of trails in the 

Adirondacks and also in the Cats- 68

kills, and Vermont has built the Long 

Trail, from Canada, to Massachusetts, which the Appalachian Trail utilizes in part.)

In the West, there is the new Pacific Crest Trail, conceived in 1932 and completed in 1935, two years before the Appalachian Trail, which dates back to 1922, was completed.

The great progress made in the completion of both trails is due of course to the work of the CCC boys. 

In all the national forests, there are also foot trails for trips to Recreation Areas, which are well developed campsites, and also for trips into the primitive wilderness areas, where no automobile road nor any sign of civilization will ever penetrate. 

No more healthful form of vacation than hiking nor a cheaper one will be found Most of the trails are being equipped with open lean-tos where you can spend a night without a tent. These are generally free although some states like Vermont make a nightly charge of 25 cents or 50 cents. You carry a pack with essentials only and generally do your own cooking. If you make use of youth hostels, your daily costs seldom exceed $1. If you are in a region without youth hostels and sleep in lean-tos or in the open, costs should run to $5 a week. Of course, no one will condemn you if you decide to sleep at tourist homes along the roads over which the trails pass or if you eat at lodges and restaurants.

Here is a brief account of the best known trails:

The Appalachian Trail. The Appal achian Trail is primarily a foot trail through the wilderness along the crest of the Appalachians extending from Mt. Katahdin in Maine to Mt. 

Oglethorpe in northern Georgia. For a distance of 2050 miles and through fourteen states, it goes via private property as well as national forests and national parks, and makes use to some extent of the Long Trail in 

Vermont. Shelters, at intervals of about a day's journey, are part of the trail system. The best way to get information about the Appalachian Trail is to join one of the numerous hiking clubs which are active in caring for the trail. Complete details concerning the clubs and the trail can be had from the Appalachian Trail Conference (901 Union 

Trust Bldg., Washington, D. C.). Better send 25 cents for their Publication No. 5, the Appalachian Trail. 

An excellent guide which they issue is called Hiking, Camping, Winter

Sports, and Trail Clearing Equip ment. It discusses in 44 pages the equipment which should be taken for trips along the trail.

The Long Trail. One of the feat tures of Vermont is the foot trail along the crest of the Green Mountains from the Canadian border to the 

Massachusetts border. It is well marked, and shelters for overnight stops are frequent. The Green Mountain Club, Rutland, Vt. will furnish information concerning it; they also publish a guide book to the Long 

Trail at 50 cents.

The Pacific Crest Trail System. A 

newer trail than those above, the 

Pacific Crest Trail System, was suggested only in 1932, and, aided by the great work of the CCC boys, was carried forward to completion by the end of 1935. This trail runs from the Canadian border to Mexico along the summit divides of the mountain ranges of Washington, Oregon, and California, a distance of 

2265 miles. Making use of two already existing trails, the Oregon Skyline Trail in Oregon and the John 

Muir Trail in the High Sierras, the 

Pacific Crest Trail runs for 2000 miles of its length through national forests and national parks. 

The trail runs along the Cascades of Washington and Oregon to Lake 

Tahoe, thence to Tuolumne Meadows in Yosemite, across to Mt. Whitney, and via Tehachapi Pass to Mexico. 

Mountaineering experience is necessary in northern Washington and in the High Sierra. It passes Mt. Baker, Mt. Rainier, and other fine peaks in Washington; Mt. Hood and Crater 

Lake in Oregon; Mt. Shasta, Mt. 69

Lassen, Yosemite, Sequoia, Mt. Whitney, the Mohave Desert, Mt. San 

Bernardino in California. The trips through Washington and Oregon are in real wilderness, northern California is a lumbering country, the Sierras are still wilderness, but the sections in Southern California are in territory well frequented by vacationists from Los Angeles, including 

Big Bear Lake, Lake Arrowhead, and the beautiful mountain district northeast of San Diego.

Complete details about the system, including a guide and a map, can be obtained from the Pacific 

Crest Trail System Conference, 125 

South Grand Avenue, Pasadena, California. You will want: The Pacific

Crest Trail System (a description of the trail), 15 cents; Map of the 

Trail, 15 cents. The Conference will also send you a folder describing their other publications, some of which may interest you.

TRY MOUNTAIN CLIMBING 

About which only a brief note, as hikers are often mountain climbers, and all mountain climbers are hikers. The opportunities for mountain climbing are generally better than for hiking, as there are more mountains than trails. New England, particularly the White Mts., is host to the greatest concentration of mountain climbers, as the slopes aren't too difficult (but every year searching parties go out for a few thoughtless college students who get lost on Mt. Washington). The Appalachian Mountain Club, 5 Joy Street, Boston, Mass. has provided eight shelters in the White Mts. for climbers who don't tote their own sleeping equipment, and accommodations are available at $3 a day. (If you carry your bedding, you can make free use of the numerous shelters provided for hikers.) The mountain parks, state and national, and the national forests in both the east and west also provide numerous opportunities for mountain climbing. If mountain climbing appeals to you, write to the national park service for information about the parks nearest you, to the state park services at the various addresses given elsewhere, and to the national forest service for the recreational guide to the national forests which has already been mentioned. Don't forget, however, that in isolated regions or wherever there aren't well marked trails, it's best to go with a guide; no one who hasn't hitherto scrambled up a mountain should attempt any but the easiest climbs.

It gets cold on the top of the mountains, so it is well to carry warm, windproof clothing. Heavy, well-fitting walking boots are essential. Don't forget a compass, knife, gloves, and first-aid kit.

TRY FOLD BOATING 

You've probably heard of the foldboats-folding canoes. In Europe, they're such a great success that youth hostels have been built just for faltbot (the way they're known in Germany) enthusiasts. Not too heavy (35 lbs. for a one-seater; 60 for a two or three seater), they're easily stowed away into your auto or you can carry them to a beckoning stream or lake. They've been used for one day trips, week-end trips, and also for such adventures as reaching Hudson's Bay or paddling up and down Africa's jungle rivers. 

Their advantage over canoes is that they can be folded. That one point has opened up all of America's waters to the vacationist. Needing nothing more than trunk space in an automobile or a railroad or bus ticket, the vacationist is able to roam wherever he wishes, without all the attendant troubles of figuring out how to take along a 16-18 foot unyielding canoe. These folding boats can also be equipped with sails or outboard motors. 

With two in the canoe, considerable baggage can also be taken along in case you're intent upon overnight camping. The canoe being put together, speedily, in ten minutes, or, leisurely, in twenty, it's simple enough to carry the canoe around a portage or to pack up when the country gets dull and take a bus or train to farther fields. Consult the state tourist departments or the United States Travel Bureau for good canoeing waters. 

The prices of these canoes run as follows: for a one-seater, $39 up; for a two or three seater, $56 up; paddles, $1.90-$5.90; carrying bags, $2.40; sails (40 sq. ft.), $14; and there are a lot of miscellaneous gadgets like spraycovers, cushions, and so on which you might be tempted to get. OR HUDSON'S BAY 

Try something none of your friends has ever attempted-visit Hudson's 

Bay via trading boat. Go to a land of centuries-old trading posts, to immense forests; see hundreds of white whales, polar bears, walrus, and seals. Visit, in fact the unknown wilderness of the Far North. 

And it isn't hard at all. Only a half dozen years ago, a railroad was built across the Ontario forest, plains, and moss to St. James' Bay, the southern tip of Hudson's Bay.

Few people ever go so far north, so the train leaves only a few times a month. Its "sailing dates," like a steamship's, are announced months in advance. But the journey itself, from North Bay, takes less than 24 hours. 

Plan your trip so that you will arrive at North Bay early in the morning. That will allow you enough time to see the Dafoe Nursery at Callander or to take a boat trip on beautiful Lake Nipissing. Your train leaves 

North Bay at 1:05 in the morning, and you will reach Cochrane in the agricultural country of North Ontario in time for breakfast. The rest of the day the train goes first through alternate strips of forests and farms and later through moss covered land near tidewater St. James' Bay. You reach Moosenee in time for dinner at pleasant James Bay Inn, a good hostelry at the end of civilization. 

On an island near Moosonee is Moose Factory, an ancient post of the 

Hudson's Bay Company dating back to 1671, when most of America was 72

still an undisturbed continent. The 

French and British battled twice for its possession a century before the thick buffalo grass of the prairie of our own middle west was first touched by a farmer's plow. From 

Moose Factory, you can rent canoes to take you along the wooded shores of St. James' Bay into Hudson's Bay. 

Or you can buy passage on a motorboat, sailing schooner, or diesel powered schooner for journeys to the north. 

One boat leaves Moose Factory weekly for Charlton Island, a densely wooded island in James' Bay, famous for its speckled trout fishing; the round trip costs $20, and at the island visitors are accommodated in a former trading post at $4 a day. 

Or a trading boat operates past 

Charlton Island to Albany Post (another ancient post of the company, dating back to 1674), Attawapiskat

Post (on a deeply forested, very beautiful river; just above the post, the polar bears come ashore regularly the 25th of every August to den up for the winter); Rupert's House (the oldest post of the company, from which point remarkable canoe trips to fishing and hunting grounds can be taken), Eastmain Post, Fort

George Post (where a large Indian population centers its life around the fur company post), and Great Whale 

River on Hudson's Bay (the home of the Eskimos). The round trip costs $80 plus 75 cents for each meal while the ship is in port. Another ship, this one available to men only, goes also to Hudson's Bay but to another port, Weenusk; rate is the same as for the last trip.

Within such close distance of great cities, you seldom get over your amazement at finding yourself back in the old fur trading days of America. If you come for the fishing, you find some of the best game fishing in America. But if you come just to look, you will never forget your glimpse into an unconquered wilderness. 

Any railroad agent will quote you a through rate from your home town via North Bay to Moosonee. You can also reach North Bay via bus, and most bus companies have all sorts of sightseeing and all-expense arrangements for any trip to North 

Bay, the little Ontario town that leaped into fame when the quintuplets were born in the nearby village of Callander.

Every hostel provides separate sleeping rooms for each sex, separate washrooms, a common kitchen, a dining room, and usually a recrea tion room. Sleeping accommodations are immaculate and consist of bunks for which hostelers supply their own sheets; the hostel supplies beds, mattresses and blankets. Sometimes the hostel offers dormitory accommodations; sometimes, boys and young men sleep in barns. The hostels also furnish cooking utensils and hostelers pay 5 cents to 10 cents for fuel for preparing their meals; food can be bought at the hostel. Some hostels will also provide meals. 73

Rules are simple: you may stay at a hostel only if you come "under your own power." That includes riding a horse, bicycling, canoeing, and hiking but excludes travel via automobile, bus, or rail. You are expected to keep moving but may stay at the hostel during the day if it's raining.

The Youth Hostels have their main membership among young people, but college students are taking to the movement very fast, and many older people are joining. There is no age limit, the slogan of the association being "From 4 to 74." Frequently families consisting of mother and father in their late thirties and forties and children in their teens can be found in the New England group of hostels.

TRY A DUDE RANCH 

You find them all over the country, from New York to California.

The eastern ones of course are artificial ranches, especially built to resemble a western ranch, with imported cow hands and a good stable.

But they're no more artificial than many a western dude ranch, for this kind of vacation resort has become so popular, every place in the west that owns a horse is calling itself a dude ranch. The west is jammed with mountain lodges, hot springs resorts, fishing camps, and even farms that blithely call themselves dude ranches. 

Montana and Wyoming are the centers of dude ranching. Many of the ranches are true stock ranches, with a lot of cattle, the sort of dude ranches most people think of. There's a very short season, and rates are fairly high. In Montana, dude ranching has become a major industry. 

Most of the ranches are situated in the mountains or on the plains near to mountains. Guests take part in general ranch activities, are taught how to ride, and take pack trips into the mountains and often into the national parks.

After Montana and Wyoming, most dude ranches center in the southwest, particularly in the desert, which is beautiful and comfortable in the winter. Many of these desert ranches are only winter hotels with a stable. In the mountains of Arizona and New Mexico are numerous stock ranches open all year, and in Texas near the New Mexico border are stock ranches and ranch farms. California's ranches are of two types, desert resorts and, in the Santa Barbara Mts., very comfortable guest ranches, that is, ranches especially built for "dudes". 

Colorado has some of these guest ranches and Nevada many around Reno, to which flee divorcees-to-be. The 

Pacific Northwest hasn't many ranches, most of which are in the Cascades, some of them so far from the main roads you either have to fly to them or ride in via horse. 

Accommodations on the stock ranches on the whole are comfortable, being what would be expected in an average home of good taste. The western guest ranches are often swankier affairs; some of them pro- 75

vide all the frills, like concrete swimming pool, tennis court, badminton, ping pong, archery, etc. There's more informal atmosphere at a true operating ranch, generally fewer forms of entertainment, but guests are invited to help in roundups, roping calves, and driving cattle.

Meals consist of plenty of fresh vegetables and milk, generally from dairy cows on the ranch, much meat, fish from nearby brooks and lakes, and also game during the season.

The less formal ranches make a point of serving meals family style. 

The housekeeping cabin isn't absent even from ranches, and it provides a good way to cut costs for families. 

When you arrange for a stay at a ranch, you will be told what to wear. "Chaps" are only for the very dude-ish ranches. At most places, you are urged to wear "levis" (overalls) and cotton shirts. Some ranches advise old clothes, several pairs of old shoes, riding clothes if you like, plenty of warm things if you're going to a mountain ranch, and a rain coat. "Levis" and other apparel can be bought near the ranches.

WAYS TO TRAVEL 

To see everything, there's nothing better than auto travel, unless it might be to travel over the dullest parts of the country by train and to go the rest of the way by auto. 

The bus and the auto share the distinction of giving you an intimate acquaintance with all the country through which you pass, but the private auto doesn't need to follow a schedule and you can head for unfamiliar country roads where great elms cast deep shadows or can roam over slow mountain highways where each new turn brings new vistas. 

No one should go on a long auto tour without sending first to the 

Continental Oil Company at Denver,

Colo. for a booklet of maps on which your exact itinerary is charted. All of the other gasoline companies will also route you, and some of them, particularly the Texas Company and the Standard Oil of New Jersey, will give you a valuable guide to road conditions. But none of them gives as good a road chart as the Continental Oil. Best of all, it's free; if you're grateful, you will use their oil and gas. But don't ask the company to do all your thinking. Tell them not only when you're going and if you want a fast or a scenic route, but use the hints in the preceding section on routes to the national parks and the following section on cross country routes to instruct them in general how you wish to go. one

Also get the handy city maps, travel guides, and accommodation directories issued by the Shell Oil 

Company (at New York, St. Louis, and San Francisco). In the east,

Shell issues tourist court directories and brief guides to all the states; in the middle west Shell might again issue tourist court directories but it does publish brief state guides; in the west, it does the best job of all, offering splendid guides to hotels, resorts, and tourist courts and beautiful, informative mile-by-mile guides to the chief highways. All of them are free.

The subject of auto travel is covered in some detail in HOW TO 

TOUR THE U.S. IN 31 DAYS FOR 

$100, a supplementary book to TWO 

DAYS TO TWO WEEKS. Briefly, some of the points it makes about auto travel are these: the best party for an auto trip would be 3 adults, all good drivers, who can alternate every 100 miles. (However most auto parties consist of four adults.) A very easy program would be 200 miles a day in the east, 300 miles in the west. Actually, drivers stay longer hours on the road and cover over 

300 miles a day in the east, well over 400 in the west. The deserts of the southwest are best covered at night; many parties which travel all day and then cross the desert at night make 850 miles in 24 hours.

Most people stop at tourist courts, 82

which are just about everywhere, their number having mushroomed since 1938. The modern tourist court is extremely comfortable, quite clean, and its convenience is one of its success-making features. Rates were once standardized at $1 a person, but they now run all the way from 

75 cents a person in the southwest to $2 a person in the north for the luxurious quarters. The earliest tourist cabins were built for just two guests; modern ones are larger, accommodating four or six; some have housekeeping accommodations. Most passengers manage quite well on less than $3 a day expenses. The usual figures which are supposed to indicate the cost of operating a private auto are all grossly inflated as they include depreciation, insurance, garage rental at home, etc., none of which mean direct cash outlays on your specific trip and go on even if you travel via train. Hence it will be found that for modern small cars -Plymouth, Chevrolet, Ford-an operating cost of 12 cents a mile is sufficient in the United States, 2 cents in Canada where gasoline is more expensive. One half cent a mile extra should be sufficient for all other jobs like washing the car (it will get very dirty on the road), possible flat tires, and the other casualties of the road. Joining the A. 

A.A. might be a good idea if you're going on a long trip, as the association will provide free tow service anywhere in the United States and 

Canada. In Mexico, the Pemex association mentioned in the discussion of Mexico has a corps of autos on the road to provide any help American tourists might need. The Mexican government wants American autoists, and the Pemex Travel association will almost mother you, it's so careful of getting your good will. 

When travelling on a long trip, it's only elementary of course to start with good tires, a motor tuneup, and plenty of money (for emergencies). Take chains for winter driving, shovel and old potato bags for sand driving should you desire to venture upon infrequented desert roads, and a tow line (unless you want to depend upon the A.A.A.'s free tow service). A good plan is to chart each day's travel in advance.

For exact mile-by-mile road information, in addition to the Shell information already mentioned, turn to the bibliography for the list of 

Federal Writers' Project guides.

HOW MUCH CAN I SEE? Below are condensed tour itineraries for vacationists in the east, middle west, south, and far west. They are based upon spending the minimum time at the national parks and upon the hard, fast driving which most auto nomads find best suited to their.temperaments; i.e., 300 miles a day in the east, 400 in the west. They are also based upon a 16 days' vacation, from Saturday to Sunday night, two weeks later. The itineraries cover a great deal of territory, more than most people normally believe can be done within such a limited time. Yet if you enjoy constant driving, and in the summer the cross country highways are jammed with those who do, you will find it possible to cover all these routes easily. 

They are not designed for the fanatics who start before sunrise and stop after dusk, who could easily add thousands of miles to each itinerary. 

The routes are suggestive of course; their design is to show what can be done and hence they are subject to infinite variations, dependent upon the place where you live and your own inclinations in speed of travel. 

They show convenient ways to make use of the section on how to reach the national parks to be found elsewhere in this book and the preceding section on major scenic and crosscountry routes in America. If you'd rather visit other places than those mentioned below, the outlines that follow help to indicate just what territory could fit within a two weeks' vacation.

VIA TRAIN 

Most travellers know that there are three types of rail travel west of the Mississippi: (1) standard pullmans with lower and upper berths, sections, drawing rooms, etc.; (2) tourist pullmans with lower and upper berths; and (3) coaches, which may consist of either day coaches or reclining chair cars, also known as sleeper coaches. In the east and in Canada, there are no tourist pullmans. 

Tourist pullmans are the same as standard pullmans although older.

Two persons travelling together often share the same lower berth. Such a great improvement has been made in modern chair cars, however, that many former pullman passengers are now going via chair car, particularly as there is often as much as a $20 difference in rail fare in just an overnight trip. Many railroads are offering trains which consist only of chair cars or else of chair cars and tourist pullmans. These are the well known economy trains, as meal costs in the dining cars have been scaled down considerably. VIA PREPAID TOUR 

Which simply means that before you start on your way, you pay one definite fee to cover transportation, hotels, meals, and sightseeing. It is about the easiest way of all to travel, and it eliminates all trouble in finding hotels and deciding what sightseeing you are going to do. You can either travel independently or in groups with a conductor. The latter way is generally quite expensive, as most groups use only pullmans and stop at the best hotels. But you can get a good trip. The American Express Company annually offers several of the finest conducted tours of the United States and Canada, ' and their rates compare most favorably with those of other tour operators. (Write them at New York, Chicago, or San Francisco. If you live in a city of any size, you will probably find a branch office of this company listed in the 'phone book.)

All the railroads in the summer also sell conducted tours of the west. 

When you travel independently, you can adjust your schedule to your 

Own inclinations. You can select boat, bus, or rail as you choose and please. However, you will find that most itineraries are fairly well standardized, as you haven't any choice, for example, as to the railroad or bus route that will take you to Banff or other parks. Hence you will welcome the dozens of bus and rail itineraries already worked out for you in TRAVELWAYS, the tours companion book to TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS. (TRAVELWAYS is also known as FOREIGN LANDS AT STAY-AT-HOME PRICES as it de scribes hundreds of low cost steamer trips to foreign countries.) 

Travelling independently, you can buy all of your hotel accommodations and sightseeing in advance, you can buy only part of it (a common plan if you intend to spend a good deal of time in any one city or if part of your trip includes visiting friends, who will put you up), or, finally, you can buy nothing except transportation in advance. This is the most economical way of all, for you generally can get much lower priced hotels than the travel agents will send you to (after all, low cost hotels generally don't give travel agents any commissions) and you don't really need to go on sightseeing buses. Only the very much rushed for time and the lazy let sightseeing buses take them around when trolleys, local buses, and subways are available. (If you travel with two or three friends, you will find taxis lower in cost than sightseeing buses.) Late in 1940, the publishers of TWO DAYS TO TWO WEEKS will issue a guide to the United States, Canada, and

Mexico, which will discuss in part how to get around the leading cities via trolley, bus, and your own foot power. If you contemplate any travel in Canada, a good firm to write to for details about independent travel is Canadian Travel League, Coristine Building, Montreal, who have an eye out for low cost travel throughout the Dominion.

American states are being covered by the Federal Writers' Project guides. The entire series will be available by the end of 1941. The guides are an essential adjunct to seeing America, and one good thing the depression did bring forth was this collection of state guides. Some of the guides are excellent, a few, like the Idaho guide (which doesn't even mention Sun Valley by name) are poorly conceived and lacking in detail. Perhaps because so many had a hand in preparing them, none is touched by a strong personality: the color, the reason that makes people go to these states and live there, is completely forgotten. None of them has the amazing unity and character of the Baedeker guides to Europe, to which they have so often been compared. But one and all are informative. 

A comparison of the California guide prepared by the Federal Writers' Project ($2.50, Hastings House) with Aubrey Drury's guide to the same state ($3.50, Harpers) reveals at once the difference between a well prepared one man guide and a group 94

project. The former is infinitely more detailed, with much more data on such matters as taxi fares in the different cities and exact instructions for reaching specific points.

The best plan would be to get both. 

Together they cost $6, which you will spend many times over before you finish any visit to California.

A good group guide is the Florida book ($2.50, Oxford) superbly printed with masses of detail. State by state, the other guides that we have examined can be viewed as follows (even though some criticisms are censorious, that doesn't mean the books aren't masses of facts worth having at hand): "Connecticut" ($3, Houghton Mifflin), over-detailed, poorly organized for easy grasp of the highlights of the state, and much too talkative about minor sights; "Delaware" ($2.50, Viking), an excellent, thoughtful, well planned guide; "Iowa" ($2.50, Viking), a thorough verbose guide principally of interest to libraries; 'Kansas" ($2.50, Viking), similarly thorough and of interest to those who are spending considerable time in the state; "Massachusetts" ($3, Houghton Mifflin), one of the first guide books completed and hence while well stocked with information, lacking in those points which later became essential features of the books, like information as to nearby swimming pools, golf courses, ways of getting around the cities, etc.: "Minnesota" ($2.50, Viking) inadequate for vacationists but thorough as to the history of each town; "Mississippi" ($2.50, Viking), a book with some feeling, quite good for the tourist except for insufficient cost information; "Montana" ($2.50, Viking), worth getting if you're spending a few days in the state but poorly conceived so that it's difficult to get instantly the specific facts you want, and none too good for vacationists; "Nebraska" ($2.50, Viking), thorough, with good road information; "New Jersey" ($2.50, Viking), cynical, amusing, and informative; "North Carolina" ($2.50,
