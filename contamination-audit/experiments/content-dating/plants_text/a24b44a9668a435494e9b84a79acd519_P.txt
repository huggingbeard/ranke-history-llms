Small Secretary Mr. Winthrop S. Greene I. HISTORICAL A. PREPARATION (a) Invitation 1. An invitation to the Government of the United States of America to participate in the Conference on the Limitation of the Manufacture of Narcotic Drugs was contained in a note from the Secretary General of the League of Nations, dated June 14, 1930, which quoted the following resolution adopted by the Tenth Assembly of the League of Nations on September 24, 1929, in accordance with a report of the Fifth Committee of the Assembly: The Assembly, Impressed by the disclosures made in the report of the Advisory Committee as to the large quantities of dangerous drugs still passing in the illicit traffic; Recalling the proposals made in connection with the Geneva Conference of 1924-25 for the direct limitation by agreement between the Governments of the manufacturing countries of the amount of such drugs manufactured; Taking note of the important declaration made in the course of the present meeting of the Assembly by the representative of France that his Government has decided to impose such limitation on its manufacturers, and of the declarations made by other Governments as to limitation; REPORT OF THE DELEGATION OF THE UNITED STATES Recognising that the Geneva Convention of 1925 provides indispensable machinery for the national and international control of the traffic in drugs, the effective application of which should be secured in all countries at the earliest possible date; but that, owing to the delay in bringing the Convention into force, its full effects can not be realized in the near future; Desiring that, if possible, steps supplementing the Convention should be taken without delay to limit the manufacture of dangerous drugs to the amounts required for medical and scientific purposes; (I) Regards the principle of the limitation of the manufacture of the drugs mentioned in paragraphs (b), (c) and (g) of Article 4 of the Convention of Geneva by international agreement as now accepted; (II) Requests the Advisory Committee to prepare plans for such limitation, regard being had to world requirements for medical and scientific purposes and the means of preventing an increase in price which would lead to the establishment of new factories in countries which are not at present manufacturing countries; (III) The Committee's report will be submitted to the Council, which will decide on the convening of a Conference of the Governments in whose countries the above-mentioned drugs are manufactured and the principal consuming countries in a number not exceeding that of the manufacturing countries, and whether certain experts proposed by the Opium and the Health Committees should be included; (IV) Recommends that the Advisory Committee be enlarged in order to ensure more effective representation on that Committee of the nonmanufacturing countries; (V) Agrees that the sum of 25,000 s Swiss francs shall be included in the budget of the League for 1930 in order to meet the expenses of such a Conference.
2. On October 27, 1930, the Secretary General of the League of Nations notified the Secretary of State that at its session in October, 1930, the Council of the League of Nations had decided "to convert the Conference on the Limitation of the Manufacture of Narcotic Drugs, which was originally limited to a certain number of States, into a general Conference of all States".
3. A note from the Secretary General of the League of Nations, dated March 11, 1931, enclosed "the report of the Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs on the plan drawn up by it with a view to the limitation of the manufacture of narcotics, and also a draft Convention for that purpose, likewise drawn up by the Committee at its Fourteenth Session". The Secretary General stated that he had been instructed by the Council to draw the attention of this Government to the fact that "the provisions of the draft Convention are somewhat wider than the resolution of the Tenth Assembly, which provided for the limitation of the manufacture of narcotics referred to in Article 4 (b), (c), (g) of the Geneva Opium Convention, whilst the draft Convention proposes to extend certain of the provisions of the Convention to other drugs not covered by the Geneva Convention ".

(b) Opium Advisory Committee Discussions 5. Although this Government has no member on the Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs of the League of Nations, the discussions of that Committee during its Thirteenth and Fourteenth Sessions concerning a plan to be submitted to the Conference in Geneva on May 27 were followed by a representative of this Government, as were also the discussions at the Preliminary Conference of the Governments of countries in which narcotic drugs are manufactured which was held, on the invitation of the British Government, in London from October 27 to November 11, 1931.
6. The Draft Plan produced by the Advisory Committee, which was referred to above, was the subject of some two weeks' discussion at each of the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Sessions of the Advisory Committee. The basic idea of the Plan was the determination of the legitimate narcotic-drug requirements of the world by estimates of consuming countries, and the allocation among the exporting manufacturing countries on the basis of a predetermined percentage of the total thus ascertained. The author and chief proponent of this Plan was the British member of the Advisory Committee, Sir Malcolm Delevingne.
(c) London Preliminary Conference 7. On invitation of the British Government, a Preliminary Conference of the Governments of the countries in which narcotic drugs are manufactured was held in London from October 27 to November 11, 1931, in the hope that the manufacturers would themselves reach an agreement concerning the percentage of the export trade which each country should have under the "Quota Plan" and that the Governments of those countries would agree upon those percentages as the basis of discussion at the May Conference. The suggestion that such a Conference be held was made by the Opium Advisory Committee and approved by the Council of the League of Nations in May, 1930. (See note from the Secretary General of the League of Nations, dated June 14, 1930.) T REPORT OF THE DELEGATION OF THE UNITED STATES 8. The London Conference was participated in by representatives of the Turkish Government, who insisted that one third of the world's requirements be allocated to the manufacturers of Turkey, on the ground that Turkey is one of the principal producers of raw opium and that in recent years very large exports of manufactured drugs from Turkey have taken place. The Turkish delegate insisted that these exports were in accordance with Turkish law, and gave the names of the alleged countries of destination of very large shipments. However, the representatives of the countries of alleged destination who were present stated that there were no records of the receipt of such shipments in their respective countries.
9. After long negotiation, an agreement concerning percentages was reached by the manufacturers of the principal exporting countries, with the exception of Turkey, which was assigned no quota.
10. Although representatives of British manufacturers participated in the manufacturers' discussion and agreed to the percentages worked out by that group, the British Government's representative refused to accept the percentages agreed upon, basing his objection on the fact that the percentages were not worked out in accordance with the Plan of the Advisory Committee, which stipulated that the basis should be the legitimate export business of recent years..

CALDWELL Chairman of the American Delegation APPENDIX OBJECTIVES AND CONTRIBUTIONS OF THE DELEGATION OF THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA An analysis of the contributions made by the Delegation representing the United States of America must necessarily take into account the aims and policies of this Government in accepting the invitation to participate.
Practically all of the contraband narcotic drugs seized in the United States have their origin outside the Western Hemisphere.
Obviously, from the standpoint of the United States, a limitation of the world manufacture of narcotic drugs to the quantities required for medicinal and scientific purposes only should tend to reduce the quantity of contraband drugs arriving at American ports, should aid in the solution of the problem of drug addiction and should simplify the administration of laws and regulations governing the distribution and uses of these drugs in the United States.
It was the opinion of this Government that indirect limitation of the manufacture of narcotic drugs, resulting from the application of measures restricting imports and exports, should be supplemented by other measures designed further to control the distribution of such substances and to limit the manufacture of all dangerous and potentially dangerous narcotic drugs.
As a matter of practical administration, it was considered impossible to apply effectively any scheme for the limitation of manufacture without some means of limiting the quantity of raw materials which factories are permitted to transform into narcotic drugs.
It was apparent to the Government of the United States that the successful operation of any scheme for the limitation of the manufacture of narcotic drugs involved the transmission, by all countries, of estimates of the quantities of narcotic drugs required for domestic consumption and the control of exports from manufacturing countries.
The Delegation was instructed to give consideration to the possibility of obtaining an international agreement for the limitation of the manufacture of narcotic drugs, based upon the following principles: (a) The submission to a central office of annual estimates of needs of dangerous or potentially dangerous narcotic drugs 121332-32- -5 APPENDIX TO THE REPORT for domestic consumption and for conversion into nondangerous substances; (b) an undertaking on the part of governments of countries in which factories are situated to permit factories to obtain only such quantities of raw materials as would provide for the manufacture of the quantities of narcotic drugs which in their opinion, based on available information, are clearly essential for medical and scientific purposes; (c) the checking by a central office of proposed exports of dangerous and potentially dangerous narcotic drugs, in advance of the issuance of an export permit, in order to prevent exports of such drugs to any country in excess of the quantity which it should be allowed to import on the basis of its estimates.
The American Delegation was able to have incorporated into the Convention for Limiting the Manufacture and Regulating the Distribution of Narcotic Drugs substantially all the principles outlined above, in keeping with the policy and desires of the government.
The Delegation was specifically instrumental in causing to be incorporated into the Convention certain of these principles, in that it (a) Supported and insisted on the adoption of the proposal that all dangerous and potentially dangerous opium and cocaleaf alkaloids and salts, and the derivatives of such alkaloids and salts, should come within the purview of limitation; (b) Proposed and insisted that all such dangerous and potentially dangerous derivatives of opium and the coca leaf, including drugs synthetically prepared without the use of opium, coca leaves, or their derivatives as the parent substance, should be subjected to the same restriction in the matter of limitation; (c) Supported proposals that each government should furnish annual estimates of its needs for narcotic drugs, based solely on medicinal and scientific requirements, the estimates to be examined by a supervisory body, and also took part in formulating the method of analyzing estimates thus submitted; (d) Proposed and insisted that annual estimates to be submitted should include the quantities required not only for narcotic drugs as such but also for preparations containing narcotic drugs, thus making more completely effective the limitation of manufacture which is based upon estimates; (e) Supported the proposal finally incorporated in the Convention that no newly discovered product obtained from the dangerous alkaloids of opium or from the ecgonine alkaloids of the coca leaf should be placed upon the market until the government of the country in which the manufacture of the product was contemplated had ascertained that it was of medical or scientific value; and that when trade in such a product was permitted, notice should be sent to other parties to the Convention, the APPENDIX TO THE REPORT Health Committee of the League of Nations being obligated to determine, through its consulting bodies, whether the particular product was a dangerous habit-forming drug as such or one potentially dangerous by reason of its convertibility into a dangerous habit-forming drug, a provision which ensures that newly discovered drugs will be subjected to control by import and export certificates pending determination of their dangerous or potentially dangerous properties; (f) Proposed that the quantity of drugs manufactured in any country should be required to come within the limits of the estimates for medicinal and scientific needs, thus placing a definite limitation upon manufacture, and strongly opposed a proposal to allow a 5 per cent tolerance of excess manufacture, a proposal which was defeated; (g) Proposed strict supervision, by each party to the Convention, over amounts of raw material and manufactured drugs in possession of each manufacturer, including accountability for drugs produced and disposed of, and proposed the limitation of quantities of raw material in possession of any manufacturer to amounts required during the ensuing six months or, in the discretion of the government concerned, based on exceptional conditions, during the ensuing year; (h) Proposed the requirement that each manufacturer make quarterly reports of drugs received, manufactured, disposed of, and remaining on hand, and proposed that, with reference to raw material received, each manufacturer should state the quantity of alkaloids contained in or producible therefrom as determined by a method prescribed by the government concerned; (i) Supported miscellaneous detailed proposals designed as additional checks against possible excess manufacture, and contained in Articles 7, 8 and 9 of the Convention; (j) Supported proposal prohibiting exportation of diacetylmorphine (heroin), its salts, or preparations containing diacetylmorphine or its salts except to a country in which diacetylmorphine is not manufactured for medicinal purposes, and then only to a department of the government in the said country, upon request of the said government; (k) Supported proposals dealing with the imposition of more stringent control over imports and exports, as outlined in Articles 12, 13 and 14; (7) Supported proposal to require such disposition of narcotics seized in illicit traffic as will eliminate the probability of subsequent diversion of these drugs to the illicit traffic; (m) Supported the provision that solutions or dilutions of morphine or cocaine or their salts in an inert substance containl APPENDIX TO THE REPORT ing 0.2 per cent or less of morphine or 0.1 per cent or less of cocaine should be subject to the same control as other preparations containing a higher percentage of the respective drugs, thus avoiding a possible loophole for avoiding the general provisions of the Convention; (n) Proposed that control measures applied to drugs included in Group I of the Convention should be extended, not only to preparations made from morphine and cocaine, but to all other preparations made from the other drugs in Group I, i. e., from all other dangerous habit-forming alkaloids and salts of opium and coca leaves; and (o) Proposed that, in case at the end of the year excess manufacture of narcotic drugs is found to have taken place in any country, the government of that country, in forwarding its annual statistics to the Permanent Central Board, should be required to give the reasons for any such excess manufacture.
The provisions for exchange of statistical information embodied in the Convention under discussion are in effect an amplification of the provisions of an already existing treaty to which the United States is a party, the Hague Convention of 1912.
The Delegation respectfully submits that the inclusion in the Convention of the numerous features set forth above, many of which are already found in the legislation now in effect in the United States and all of which are in accord with the existing system of control under our Federal laws, constitutes an additional reason for ratification of the Convention by the United States.

The particulars given shall indicate as far as possible: (a) The kind and quantity of drugs involved; (b) The origin of the drugs, their marks and labels; (c) The points at which the drugs were diverted into the illicit traffic; (d) The place from which the drugs were despatched, and the names of shipping or forwarding agents or consignors; the methods of consignment and the name and address of consignees, if known; (e) The methods and routes used by smugglers and names of ships, if any, in which the drugs have been shipped; (f) The action taken by the Government in regard to the persons involved, particularly those possessing authorisations or licences and the penalties imposed.
(g) Any other information which would assist in the suppression of illicit traffic.
ARTICLE 24 The present Convention shall supplement the Hague Convention of 1912 and the Geneva Convention of 1925 in the relations between the High Contracting Parties bound by at least one of these latter Conventions.
ARTICLE 25 If there should arise between the High Contracting Parties a dispute of any kind relating to the interpretation or application of the present Convention and if such dispute cannot be satisfactorily settled by diplomacy, it shall be settled in accordance with any applicable agreements in force between the Parties providing for the settlement of international disputes.
In case there is no such agreement in force between the Parties, the dispute shall be referred to arbitration or judicial settlement.

ARTICLE 26 Any High Contracting Party may, at the time of signature, ratification, or accession, declare that, in accepting the present Convention, he does not assume any obligation in respect of all or any of his colonies, protectorates and overseas territories or territories under suzerainty or mandate, and the present Convention shall not apply to any territories named in such declaration.
Any High Contracting Party may give notice to the Secretarygeneral of the League of Nations at any time subsequently that he desires that the Convention shall apply to all or any of his territories which have been made the subject of a declaration under the preceding paragraph, and the Convention shall apply to all the territories named in such notice in the same manner as in the case of a country ratifying or acceding to the Convention.
Any High Contracting Party may, at any time after the expiration of the five-years period mentioned in Article 32, declare that he desires that the present Convention shall cease to apply to all or any of his colonies, protectorates and overseas territories or territories under suzerainty or mandate, and the Convention shall cease to apply to the territories named in such declaration as if it were a denunciation under the provisions of Article 32.

FINAL ACT The Conference also adopted the following recommendations: The Conference: I Recalling the proposal made by the Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs in the Model Code for the Administrative Control of the Drug Traffic,* which was drawn up at its eleventh session to the effect that, in countries the administrative organisation of which allows of such a procedure, the supervision of the trade in narcotics as a whole should be in the hands of a single authority, so that all supervisory measures over this trade may be unified; and that, in countries where this supervision is in the hands of several authorities, steps should be taken to establish co-ordination among them: Recommends that such Members of the League of Nations and non-member States as do not at present possess such a single authority should forthwith consider the desirability of establishing one, with the duty of regulating, supervising and controlling the traffic in opium and other dangerous drugs and of preventing and combating drug addiction and the illicit traffic; and that they should report to the Secretary-General of the League of Nations within a period of one year from the present date on the results of their examination of this question.
The Conference: II Recognising that the Model Code above referred to has been of considerable value to a number of Governments as a guide in the framing of legislation and administrative measures for the application of the Geneva Convention in their territories: Recommends that a similar Code should be drawn up before the entry into force of the Convention signed this day, and should be circulated to Governments with a request that they should be guided as far as possible by the Code in framing the necessary legislative and administrative measures for the application in their territories of the said Convention; Requests the Council of the League of Nations to ask the Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs to prepare such a Code.
The Conference: IV Recommends that Governments should consider the desirability of establishing a State monopoly over the trade in, and, if necessary, over the manufacture of, the drugs covered by the Convention signed on this day's date.
[The German delegation stated that it could not accept this recommendation.] The Conference: V Considering that, in order to combat more efficiently the smuggling and abuse of the substances covered by the Convention of this day's date, it is necessary by means of an international agreement to supplement the penalties provided for in Article 20 of the Hague Convention of 1912 and in Article 28 of the Geneva Convention; Considering that the Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium and Other Dangerous Drugs has been presented by the International Criminal Police Commission with a draft international convention for the suppression of the illicit traffic in narcotic drugs the main features of which are based on the Convention of April 20th, 1929, against Counterfeit Currency: Expresses the wish that, on the basis of the work undertaken by the Advisory Committee, a Convention may be concluded with the least possible delay for the prosecution and punishment of breaches of the law relating to the manufacture of, trade in, and possession of, narcotic drugs; And requests the Council to draw the attention of Governments to the importance of such a Convention, in order to hasten the meeting of a Conference to conclude a convention on this question.
