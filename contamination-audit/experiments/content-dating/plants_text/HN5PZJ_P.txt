But, as it had no license plate the authorities did not know what to do with it. A night patrolman had seen the car pass through town at about three in the morning.

I told them who it belonged to, but didn't mention the fact that the owner was drowned. 

From these later developments I came to the conclusion that when Rathbone almost ran into the taxi and lost the golf bag, he probably went back and made a frantic search for it. Failing to find it, he must have been certain that the ownership would be traced to him in some way, and immediately planned a getaway.

The chances are that if he were taken in on any charge a great deal would come out involving the ring—and Sutro. Being a close friend of the District Attorney's, he had at once raced to the port where he thought the yacht was. Then he-but wait a minute!

The night patrolman saw the car pass through town at three o'clock. Sutro's car was seen by Mr. Quirt at two. 

At two the storm was rising; at three it must have been raging. The yacht, therefore, probably sailed before the yellow coupe reached the port. If that were true, it entered town without Rathbone at the wheel. Then how did his body come to be washed ashore fifteen miles down the coast? And where was David Sutro?

While waiting for Dr. Rath I sat in the lobby of the hotel and smoked two pipes of tobacco down to the core thinking it over. At last I arrived at another conclusion, more guess work it is true, but at any rate a deduction. 

Rathbone arrived in Sutro's fast French car, and Sutro in Rathbone's coupe. How and why did they change cars? 

Again a supposititious but reasonable deduction. Rathbone, being a close friend of the District Attorney's, had gone to the garage occupied by Sutro and changed cars. 

Knowing that the French car was much faster, and prob-

ably being in a panicky hurry of fear, did this without taking the trouble to notify the owner of the French car. 

Then, it seemed likely to me, Sutro had become worried over my phone message, especially when he should have heard from Bohn long before, and decided to still-hunt the Inspector. He found Rathbone's coupe in his garage and his own car missing. But why he took the coupe and drove to the port was a mystery to me. He could not have contemplated going out in the "Norwahl." That was highly improbable. Then where had he disappeared to? 

Now, all this was just deduction, and I admitted to myself that it might be all wrong. But later, when I knew the facts, I patted myself on the back for a clever sleuth. 

My deductions proved to be absolutely correct, except for a few minor details.

When Dr. Rath and the morgue wagon arrived we drove at once to the spot where Art Jacobs was watching over the drowned man. Poor kid, it was a gruesome job for him. We found the young fellow on the top of the bluff, as far as he could get away from the body and still keep an eye on it.

The coroner didn't have much to say, but I could see that he was fully convinced this was another Syndicate job. I didn't see how it could be. The Adjustment Syndicate was powerful, there was no getting away from that, but I hardly gave it credit for creating a storm and destroying the "Norwahl."

And it's a bulletin of the Syndicate!"

The indictment against Judge Welsh had infuriated them. He had been infamous for his discharge of criminals for a long time, and now this evidence was too bald and complete to leave room for doubt. The State Highway ran from Dunston through the city, then curved and twisted its way across the state.

As the big trolley car rumbled through the city and I sat looking out of the open window with the warm early summer breeze fanning my face, I relaxed and let my thoughts wander where they would.

That was a trick I learned at college, and a mighty good one to practice-total relaxation when laboring under strain. Notice a cat at rest and how like lightning it can move when startled. That's because it is totally relaxed. With the uncertainty and possible danger ahead of me, I relaxed and let my subconscious do a little work. 

Well, the subconscious did exactly that-came to the surface with a bounce. In my worry over Norma and the two murders of the past few days I had been strung to a high tension and had not thought of much else. Now I did. 

I suddenly remembered that another execution by the Adjustment Syndicate was due before noon of the next day.

Chief Murphy had not left town and his time limit was set for noon of that day, Sunday. That he was still in the city I knew because I had seen him at Police Headquarters the day before. True, he looked scared, but I did not think he had decamped since.

The Chief had a big summer home on a hilltop three miles from Dunston, and the State Highway passed within

half a mile of it. Was he there now, I wondered? Murphy knew that his time was up and that he had been given twenty-four hours, or even less, to live. And now my orders from the Syndicate.

Then I sat back and laughed at the idea. This had nothing to do with the execution of Daniel Murphy. Sergeant Josephski was the Chief's tried and trusted henchman. He thought Daniel Murphy was the greatest police chief in the country, and would have died defending him. 

No, that was out. There was something else in the wind. 

Then another brilliant idea blossomed. Josephski probably had charge of the detail that was undoubtedly surrounding the Chief at the moment, and Murphy was doing what Desartus had done, inviting me to keep him company during his hours of peril. The headlight was out but the corner light shone back from the copper's cap ornament and shield, and glistened on his polished Sam Browne belt and puttees.

I had seen Sergeant Josephski many times and knew him by sight. It was with a certain relief that I hailed him.

It was Sergeant Josephski, all right, but his face seemed dead and lifeless. The scowl for which he was famous was there, but it seemed set and immobile. It gave me a shudder to look at the man. However, I did as I was told and climbed into the side car. As I passed around the front of the machine to get to it, the sergeant turned on his headlight.

There was something very distinctive about that headlight. In the center of the glass there was a disc of yellow about two inches across. I wondered what that was for as I settled back in the seat and the sergeant, already in the saddle, started the machine.

Without a word of any kind he swung the cycle into the State Highway and raced away toward Dunston at a speed that only a motor policeman dare hit. But what right had a uniformed policeman from Merriton to hit any speed at all outside the city limits?

For a time I said nothing, waiting for the sergeant to speak. As he did not, I at last essayed a few remarks of my own. The man made no response for some time, then, as I continued my companionable chatter, he made a remark consisting of just two words. "Be quiet." 

The words were a decided command. But it wasn't that that made me shrink back in my seat and remain silent. 

It was the dead, lifeless tone in which they were spoken; a tone as dead and lifeless as the face I had looked into at the crossroads. Then came an incident that made me want to leap from that side car, even at the risk of my life, and in spite of its speed of almost a mile a minute. 

We had swung around a long curve and were bearing down on a roadhouse, next to which was a brilliantly lighted filling station. A car was being filled with gas and the owner stood watching the operation. Just as we whizzed past he took off his hat and the light fell full on his face. It was only a flash, but enough! My mouth went dry and I almost fainted.

The man by the car was in civilian clothes, but-it was Police Sergeant Abram Josephski!

Then who and what was driving the motor cycle by my side? Was it a robot, a mechanical man-this silent, menacing thing guiding the machine along at such breakneck speed? I don't mind saying honestly that I was thoroughly and unqualifiedly scared.

Suddenly from the roadside well ahead of us a flashlight flickered three times. The silent figure at my side reduced speed and approached another motor cycle parked by the roadside, its head turned toward the sea. A policeman in the city's uniform stepped into the road as we drew near.

He reached out and took a slip of paper from my driver's hand, and our speed promptly increased until we were once more racing toward the port.

About five miles from Dunston my conductor swerved unexpectedly north into a side road and sped on. Half a mile further another flashlight flickered and we slowed up as another motor cycle policeman stepped into the road and reached for a paper. There was something terrible, something awe inspiring in this nightmare. Not a word had passed in either case, only a piece of paper. And never once had my companion even turned his head in my direction, let alone speak more than those two words.

And then I knew! Somehow; someway; in a method known only to those awful unseen powers, Chief Daniel

Murphy was about to die-to die at the hands of those strange, dead, robot-like men, if men they were!

The sound of shots had grown fainter and it was plain that the whole gang was working out toward the point of land from which the attack came. 

Ike, being satisfied that there was no one left in the cave, led us on the run, presently emerging into a great cave, dimly illuminated by several ship's lanterns hung about the walls. A sixty-foot power boat lay in the large pool, moored to a ledge of rock, with a gang plank deading to her deck. Not a soul was in sight, and we could still hear the firing from outside. The four policemen fired only one volley and then appeared, shadowy figures at the edge of the channel. For a moment they stood looking across it, past the cave, to the spit of land. The firing there had ceased abruptly. Then the whole detail entered the cave and ran swiftly around the edge of the pool to our side where the gang plank was. Then they stopped and waited, backed against the wall and guns ready.

Ike rose to his knees and called to the sergeant in command. "We have the boat, Brown. They can't get beyond the gang plank." 

Sergeant Brown whirled around, saw us, and laughed shortly. "They won't get that far, Ike," he said tersely. 

Then they came running. At the cave's entrance they looked wildly around, as though expecting to be greeted by a volley from its interior. When none came they entered slowly, not seeing the policemen in the shadows against the wall. After a moment's hesitation a short, stocky man with a bull neck ran along the narrow ledge of rock toward the gang plank. The others followed. 

The last to emerge from the outer darkness was a well dressed man who backed in, his gun ready to fire at pursuers. "Stick 'em up, Rickershaw!" The sergeant's voice snapped.

The stocky man stopped in his tracks almost at the gang plank and whipped up his gun.

Rickershaw jerked his head around and saw the muzzle of the detective's automatic a few feet from his head and pointing at him over the low gunwale of the forward deck. I turned my head as I ducked, and saw the man who fired. It was David Sutro. I also saw another figure appear behind him, saw an arm swing in a lightning-like half circle, and heard a sickening crack.

I HAVE heard it said that all human beings are innately shy, and that there are times when they would much rather sink through the ground than to be the focus of all eyes. It was difficult for me to refrain from fumbling with my collar and adjusting my tie while the professor spoke.

For a moment I actually forgot to listen to the professor. 

She was gorgeous in the black dinner gown that accentuated her dark beauty and the pallor of her finely formed arms and neck.
