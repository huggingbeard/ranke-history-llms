But it should not be thought that Modern Medicine is purposely and wholly to blame for this sad plight; it is only a part of its heritage from ages grown dim in our histories. The phrase "only a part of its heritage" is purposely used for the reason that the solution to this chaotic problem is also a part of the heritage, but it is the part that somehow has been overlooked or deliberately cast aside.

Today, then, we still have the sound and fundamental theories and proven facts as to the real cause of disease, and it merely remains for us to recognize the truth of such and delegate its usefulness to ourselves. This is why the previous statement was made that moody spells are symptoms of an unbalanced, "sick" condition and that tuberculosis itself is no more than a symptom. 

Right at this point there is, no doubt, a mental hue and cry of "Treason!" emanating from the

reader, but his indulgence is begged until the point of proof is reached. Just now we are more concerned with the formulation of a definition of sickness. 

The peculiar technique of this definition is its own proof. First, we look at the "well person,' the healthy person. Be it a man: We see, then, that he is suffused with a vibrant energy and a personal magnetism that bespeaks of his treasure of health and red blooded virility even to his fingertips. There is not a pepless, sluggish moment in his days or night. 

His smile is contagious as he seems to say, "Come on, Life I'm ready and anxious to fight!" This condition, mind you, does not mean a nervous aimlessness nor an overly optimistic fool who grins ceaselessly. 

This condition does mean that here is a man who is happy to be alive, and whose health has given him the punch necessary to realize his purpose in life, fearless of competition—which he actually welcomesand glad for a hope and a chance at the future.

Morally, mentally, and physically he is head and shoulders above the crowd, and when you meet him you can't help saying, "That fellow enjoys life."

This man is the vibrantly healthy man. His condition is as nearly perfect as is possible for the human machine to be. Any other man, be he leper or loafer, is most certainly a sick man.

Likewise the healthy woman shows her joyous health so unmistakably clear. She is literally enveloped in a radiating health that makes itself known by rosy cheeks, sparkling eyes, glowing skin and a captivating poise. Here is the woman whose delightful presence is felt by those around her, and whose smile and magnetic laugh carries with it a lively in-

telligence. She may not possess the facial features that are so blandly described as "pretty", but her permeating health of mind and body constantly guarantees her the justification of being described as -Beautiful. No less than the man is she anxious to wrest from life's disappointments and obstacles her share of greater and greater peace and happiness. Nor are there any of those hundreds of disconcerting little ailments about her which would necessarily subtract from her peace of mind and exuberance of physical health. Here is the Healthy 

Woman whose beauty can not be bought over a cosmetic counter. Any woman less fortunate in mind and body is the sick woman.

As for the child, when in full health it is fully awake to the pleasures and demands of its childsized world. Laughingly it greets the challenges which are to it as serious and important as any of those of the adults. This child drains the last ounce of "fun" from its activities, and is always on the look-out for more. The competition which he naturally craves is sought in the company of his companions. His energetic mind bespeaks its health by a well mannered and consistent inquisitiveness, and his healthy body speaks for itself in his seemingly well-nigh inexhaustible energy. His alternating attitudes are those of "up and at 'em!" and restful, earnest seeking for information. Nor does this mean that the healthy child is characterized as a cyclonic essence- nor, what is worse, a replica of little Lord Fauntleroy. It is saying that a child on a lower level, mentally or physically, one that is denied his childhood happiness by some morbid or

nervous mental inertia, or by the very least of physical ailments, is a sick child.

Understanding these truths, we do not find it difficult to accept the meaning of the statement that the person suffering from fits and spells of aimless day-dreaming, depressed spirits, or the "fidgets," is as surely sick as is the person suffering from the conditions called rheumatism, pellagra, or influenza. 

The only difference is that some symptoms show themselves more severely and more clearly than others. But the basic fact is that any and all such symptoms are caused by one and the same fundamental thing.

The reader will reach here the logical conclusion that this theory holds that all sickness, ailments, and mal conditions have a common underlying cause. 

The truth is that the theory states and proves that there is only one sickness or disease, but hundreds of symptoms which are no more diseases than a sneeze.

CHAPTER II DEFINITION OF THE "GAP" 

Having read and studied over the definitions and examples of health and sickness in the first chapter, some one will say, "That's all very good, but where do I fit in? I suffer from no such inhibitions or ailments as those, yet I know my health is not what it should or could be." Such a person may also correctly describe himself as the "average" personneither ill nor healthy. My answer is that it is this type who fills to overflowing what, for want of a better term, we may call the "gap"-the almost indefinable condition between health and sickness.

Strictly speaking, there is no such gap; a person is either sick or healthy. However, for a finer understanding of this condition and a fuller appreciation of its dangers, further description and explanation must be given. For the description we must think of that person who has become so accustomed to his condition of mental and physical sluggishness and general lassitude that he is not aware of his pepless demeanor or his dazed existence. The burden of a mediocre condition has weighed on him so long that a general numbness has more or less suffused his very being. His own mental and physical condition makes his life and often times his daily routine so utterly commonplace, uninteresting, and uninspirational that it serves to keep him in the rut of mere animal existence. This person is not unlike the slave

of ancient days whose back became so accustomed to the lash of the leather whips that the slave-drivers had to load their whips with metal barbs to make them felt. Just so in this "gap" condition: The sufferer reaches the point where he is not disturbed by his partially alive condition, and it usually takes the jolt of a hard sickness to wake him up to the fact that he has been beaten by illness for years. 

To be useful for recognition of one's own condition, a more concrete example must be studied. The woman whose misfortune it is to be in this gap has, as her foremost symptom, an absence of interest in her daily work, whether it be household duties, office work, or the pursuit of a career. This is assuming, of course, that her particular work is hers by choice and suitability. By following an occupation that is extremely unpleasant and unsuited to the talents, one not only seriously handicaps one's own fight for health, but also denies one's self the profit and appreciation of that health, even though that treasure was already possessed. 

But it is this woman of the "gap" who reluctantly drags her body and mind every morning from the unrestful sleep of the night just passed, and who half-heartedly thinks and looks upon the threat of "just another day." After a couple of disdainful yawns she finally decides she must be up and about her work. Her resolution that she "must" do it is probably the best description of her daily attitude and a fuller explanation of how that work will be done. Somehow she prepares breakfast, and she and Mr. Gap and little Johnnie and Mary Gap get through the meal.

And "getting through" the meal signifies how each of this Gap family gets through the day and its routine. Pitiful as it may seem, none of this group are living; they are merely moving dazedly about in their self-confined sphere of automatic-instinctive, if you please plodding. They do not feel ill enough to consult their physician, nor do they have that degree of health that keeps them vibrantly alive.

They are just eating, working, and sleeping by habit and instinct. Even their entertainments and recreations are prompted and guided likewise. Those of the "gap" are really more to be pitied than the violently ill. The latter know they are sick, and are striving to regain their health; the former do not know they are sick, and so on they go, permitting a gradual but sure and dangerous undermining of mental and physical stamina.

Cast in the role of Mr. Gap is the man who drags himself out of bed in the morning with a bitter, "dark brown" taste in his mouth, and with a humor to match. His first shock every morning is the rush of drudgery thoughts, those disconcerting thoughts that he must get into some clothes, must take on a meal, must do this and that in his morning routine (among which, for the more fortunate wives, is a peck of a kiss on the cheek), and must get to his work. Hundreds of misgiving thoughts clamber through his mind as he goes with a heavy heart and leadened steps to his post in the work-day world. 

His physical feeling, his mental attitude, his general demeanor and his facial expression-coincide: a benumbed lifelessness. There is lacking the vital punch and purpose. Even if through some phenom-

enal blessing he had any of a thousand commendable ambitions, he does not possess the qualities necessary to "put it over" or see it through: pep, drive, direction-or the will.

The children who help fill this gap between health and sickness are probably the most pitiful victims. 

It is this type of child who drags around the house mornings, hunting up excuses for not going to school or out to play with other children. Don't think for a minute that the child who has to be begged and coaxed and finally chastised to get him off to school is necessarily dull, adverse to receiving instruction, or just frankly recalcitrant. Give the child half a chance by talking sensibly with him and by observing him, and you will find that his indisposition to follow your instructions, whether it be to attend school or run errands or join other children at play, is caused primarily by his "half sick" condition. Were he vibrantly healthy, he would respond to the parents' wishes more promptly and more eagerly, especially if in these suggestions and admonitions there is some hope of competition with his own world. This child is in all probability no different from his parents who, it is safe to say, are in the same physical condition and are afraid of contemporaneous competition and positively refuse to think of the future demands of life.

More pitiful than such a child's present condition is his future health, the building or undermining of which begins with his earliest years. This particular problem will be discussed more fully in a later chapter.

CHAPTER IV THE TEST FOR HEALTH 

Unless our symptoms are so severe and so easily recognized that your associates describe you as being "sick", no one on earth can know better than you whether or not you are well, healthy. The unfortunate thing is that hardly anybody knows anything at all about the few simple tests which ascertain the condition of the entire body. The human body being the extremely sensitive mechanism that it is, you may depend upon the invariable correctness of the result of such tests.

The best time to make these tests, most of them at least, is the first thing in the morning; that is immediately upon waking. As soon as you open your eyes don't, for heavens sake, spring out of bed in the manner of a jumping jack. Such a sudden jerk and movement is too great a strain on the heart and other vital organs which have been more or less at rest during your hour of sleep. Give them a chance to readjust themselves gradually to their increased work. Begin the day's activity as naturally as an animal; watch carefully your dog or cat as it slowly, deliberately stretches and yawns and gradually comes to full life and action. 

Turn over on your back, if you haven't awakened in that position, and—think. Unless you are already used to it, this early hour mental gymnastics will bother you at first, but after becoming accustomed to it you will soon swear by it as your

best "thinking time." But back to those thoughts:

Think of the day as an entity and not as an hour-byhour diary. Does the day seem good and promising? If it does, the healthy person will be eager to embrace every joy of it and live it to its fullest; the healthy person is only too happy to be up and about his work. Even though the day may promise riches in joy and happiness, the person who is "sick" will immediately try to devise multitudes of excuses to just lie there and wish and hope and dream aimlessly -and expect the day to close thusly. If, to the healthy early hour thinker, the world seems all wrong and out of step, or even a bit bleary, he will not lie there cringing and making wry faces at the ceiling; he will challenge such a bitter world with his determination and eagerness to leave his bed of rest and put things to rights. This mental test is simple yet true. It is merely ascertaining one's health by one's mental reaction to the first waking thoughts of the day, a most valuable test for mental and physical health.

Now you may bring yourself to a sitting position on the edge of the bed, relieve yourself of a couple of yawns with gestures, and then-smack your lips. 

Three or four times more. Right here, then, we are again at the parting of the ways. In the perfectly healthy person's mouth there will be, as best as it can be described, a velvety sweetness that is not at all unpleasant to the taste. But the person who is either in the "sick" group or the "gap" group will have a musky bitter or sour taste in his mouth. The healthy person's early morning breath, although not quite comparable to the baby's milk-sweet breath, is

not at all offensive to anyone with whom he may come into close contact. On the other hand, the sick person's breath is foul and will not only be offensive to others around him but will even be offensive to himself, usually the last person to be offended by such a condition.

Although the person with the early morning bad mouth taste and foul breath may not be ill enough to be confined to his bed, he is as surely ill as he has ever heard of anyone being, and it will be well worth his time, expenses and effort to take a quick inventory of himself and consult a competent physician at once. 

Now you may leave your bed-and walk at once to your nearest mirror. Bare your chest to at least well below your arm-pits, and scrutinize your skin, your complexion. During the night has there appeared a redness resembling rash? small pimples? or those peculiar brown "splotches" that seem not to be there during the day. If so, you are-if not violently ill-one of the "gap" members; you are sick. The specific reaction in the body that causes the above mentioned skin blemishes will be explained later on, but in passing, so that it will not seem like a dark shrouded mystery, it will be well to tell you that those dark brown spots are caused by a mal functioning liver.

The healthy person upon making this examination will find his skin to be soft and resilient, pleasantly smooth and fairly glowing as the blood increases its vigorous flow through the body. Too, after the morning bath the healthy person will feel the added buoyancy and a pleasant surge of potential power, pep and punch.

Now that you have tested yourself four ways, by thoughts, by taste, by breath, and by your skin, there remains one other test that is more deceptive in its results than any other. This is the constipation test.

Whether or not you are in the habit of having a complete bowel movement immediately upon arising every morning, you should by all means have a more complete evacuation within thirty minutes after finishing your breakfast. Should you habitually have a bowel movement upon arising and not have one at least thirty minutes after breakfast, you should begin at once to habituate yourself to the latter time. Even with both these "habit times" the average person has some difficulty in guarding against constipation. 

Until we reach the chapter dealing with constipation let it suffice to say that you may have two or three bowel movements daily and still be constipated. 

The healthy person will feel revived and yet more energetic after his after-breakfast bowel movement; the sick person will feel sluggish—if he was sluggish before the movement, dull-if he was dull before the movement, and like his "old cussed self"-as he usually feels. Nor is breakfast time the only time for this constipation test. Every meal offers the same testing opportunity; that is, within thirty or forty minutes after each meal there should be the natural urge for a complete bowel movement. CHAPTER V RELATION OF PHYSICAL AND MENTAL Health 

It may seem peculiar to you that now and then through these first chapters there has been a repeated promise to elucidate on certain points in later chapters. This is done in an effort to keep this discussion as simple as possible without running the danger of its becoming befuddled with more than a few fundamental matters at once. 

Whatever the learned arguments between the various camps of the professoriat regarding the power of the mind over matter, there is no success in denying the fact that the mind cannot properly function independently of the body. Furthermore, there is no successful functioning of the mind, at least sustained functioning, without a correlation between the mind and body to a certain degree of nicety. 

Any underlying weakness or mal condition robs your mental capacity of a proportionate part of stamina and will power. Having been robbed of this part of its stamina and will power, your mental capacity will, under the ever increasing demands of life, be unable to sustain your usual equilibrium. The mind and body, as you know, constitute the most delicate and most intricate mechanism mankind has ever known. The lightest touch of your toe against any object is instantaneously registered in and through your brain. We have become so accustomed

to such registerings and flashed messages "to ourselves" by and through our brain and nervous system that we cease to think of its being about the greatest wonder in the world. Yet, that it is.

For instance, going about your work one day you somehow become aware of a gnawing pain in the pit of your stomach. Probably you tried to "throw it off your mind" for a while at least and carry on with your work. Maybe you were successful in throwing it off your mind-and maybe you weren't!

The pain increased, and you decided to lie down and rest a few minutes, hoping that such inaction would calm the gnawing discomfort. It might have; the chances are it didn't. Then you decided to purge yourself of the cause of that pain, and you coaxed a movement of the bowels. And if that didn't help the situation, you forthwith took a strong purgative and hoped the pain would not increase until the purgative began to act. This is a very crude illustration, but it will suffice. 

The disturbance and mal condition in your body robbed your mental capacity of a certain part of its work-output, reserve energy and balance. This is quite evident by the generally bad disposition of the sick person. He is irritable and cross, not because of any of his inherent qualities, but because of his physical weakness and its direct effect on his mental sphere. The logical conclusion is that the best guarantee for a healthy mind is a healthy body. 

Practically every unnatural mental condition in the entire range from the habitually "forgetful" person or the so-called "absent-minded" person to the one whose mind is so severely affected that we

call his condition a diseased mind or insanity, has as its first inception and cause a weakened, sick, or mal condition of the physical body. Indeed is this true of the incorrigible criminal. 

With the increasing number of daily suicides you have doubtlessly wondered as to the fundamental cause of all these seemingly absurd and cowardly acts. Do not be too severe in your criticism of the suicidal-bent person. His actual act of suicide is less deserving of scathing criticism than his passive permission in letting his body become so mal-conditioned that his mind was cheated of its rightful, normal share of strength and energy wherewith he would have been able to withstand the onslaughts of the greatest misfortune. Put another way, the suicidal person has-or had-a physical condition which reacted upon his mental capacity so as to deprive him of the ability to meet the reality and demands of life. 

"But,' some one says, "I know personally and through history scores of brilliant men and women who have reached almost unattainable heights in every field of human endeavor, and yet these same men and woman had terribly diseased and mal functioning bodies." That is certainly true. But did you ever stop to think of what greater degree of success and attainments those same people could have reached had they but been in splendid physical condition? Certainly no one will offer the senseless argument than an improvement in their physical health would have impaired their mental health, energy and capacity. Rather would it have most assuredly aided and increased it. Along this line we have as witness,

among thousands of others, Robert Louis Stevenson who, with his pen as his means of expression and his death-bed as his means of support and rest, wrote some of the world's masterpieces of fiction and fact, overflowing with hope, ambition and inspiration. 

Guy de Maupassant is another example of what can be done under the most harrowing physical and mental conditions. He himself directly admitted that his condition, both physical and mental, had finally become so disconcerting that he had begun to constantly wish to die. In fact, he tried to cut his throat with a razor-but died in a madhouse. CHAPTER XIV MENTAL HYGIENE 

Too often when the subject of Mental Hygiene is mentioned many people get the vague idea that an equally vague discussion of metaphysics will immediately ensue. Some seem to think that the subject of a "clean mind" is necessarily either a highly technical subject fit only for the boresome obfuscators of the super-learned professoriate, or an equally boresome sermon. Certainly it is neither.

Nor is Mental Hygiene a form of practice of some mysterious rite of which admittance can be gained only by genius or those with impossibly great will power. There are so many volumes written on Mental Hygiene and most such volumes being without the slightest tangible proposition or proof that the reading public who honestly desire—and feel the need of intelligent guidance along such lines have many times thrown aside the books with a sense of utter loss, loss of time, effort and confidence. The truth of the matter is that mental hygiene is no more a segregated ritual for the chosen. few than the simple ritual of regularly washing your face and hands. The former ritual is in reality far more important and desirable than the latter. "Clean your mind!" is the present command.

Sounds like a command that is easier to give than to obey, doesn't it? Yet, it is as easy to obey this

command as it is to voice it. And what fine results can be obtained for your own personal health, personality and comeliness, if you but observe a few simple rules for the cleansing of the mind! 

One of the most foolish admonitions to a fellow human being is the blank statement: "Don't worry." 

What a useless expression! You might as well shout to a corpse, "Don't die!" In the first place, he cannot understand you, and in the second place the impotency of the command is engulfed in its own futility. Of course the admonition of "Don't worry" is given through the desire to help and relieve the mental anguish of the one so advised. Out of respect for the other's intelligence, don't ever give such bland advice without showing him a remedy for his predicament. In other words, don't tell him to quit worrying unless you can tell him how to quit worrying.

It would be a most marvelous thing if we could effectively command our mental regime as Jesus Christ commanded the waves of the sea when He spoke those powerful words in defining its boundaries: "Thus far shalt thou come, and no farther."

Would it not be a wonderful thing if we could as effectively say to ourselves, "Thus far shall you worry, and no farther!" Although we cannot passively repeat such magic words with any degree of accomplishment, we can most certainly get practically the same intended results if we will but apply a little common sense to our dilemma.

Your mind has its particular mission to fulfill, and it has a hard enough time doing it under the best influences, environment and encouragement without

having its way retarded and, as is too often the case, permanently side-tracked by such unnecessary blockage as the great weights of Worry, Anger, Fear, and Ignorance.

Some of these conditions are really a part of the others, but we shall not waste time here in any technical hair-splitting. We must leave that to the professional thinkers who have nothing better to do. 

What you want-and what it is hoped these pages will give you is a practical, common sense method to relieve yourself of these above mentioned mental weights.

If you must-and it seems that most of us dogo ahead and worry. But don't strangle yourself by stopping there with your real or imaginary anxiety. Do something with your worrying. Any human being who is at least one degree removed from a moron will naturally worry. Those who have the mentality that will allow them to walk safely through the streets of his town without the aid of a guardian will be continually set upon by importunities. Yet these demands, vexations, anxieties and "troubles" should not be allowed to shove that person into the rut of morbid worry, inactive anxiety. 

The truth is that all those things that worry you are in themselves stimuli that keep you fighting the good fight for Happiness. If you must worry, then -work! 

When you are worried you have a problem before you, a problem that demands immediate solution. 

It is up to you, then, to start some action-plenty of action. Of course, the direction of your action may be all wrong at first, but do something. A wrong

step is better than no step at all. If you have patience enough you will finally stumble onto the right mode of action and at last—yet sooner than you expected-you will solve your baffling problem that has caused the mental condition of balked decision which we call Worry.

Even the slightest worry generates a toxin within your system that is distributed to practically every organ in your body, and this toxin is indeed powerful enough to misdirect the influences and usefulness of such organs to your permanent detriment. It destroys your outward comeliness and literally eats away your inward calm and your whole health. This poison's dastardly effect is all too plainly evidenced on the faces of thousands upon thousands of chronic worriers who do not know how to counter-act it. 

The antidote for this condition has already been given in the one word-WORK.

Anybody can just sit and hold their hands and let worry kill them—and this quite literally. The sensible thing to do when you are weighed down with care, vexation and anxiety is to immediately recognize your chief problem as such and immediately lay your plans toward the overcoming or complete solution of the situation—and then act upon those plans. 

You will not only find yourself freed from the enslaving worry, but you will also have automatically given yourself the best known antidote for the poisons that have been thrown out into your bloodstream by your condition of worrying. Action is the natural enemy to melancholia.

CHAPTER XIX CRIME, INSANITY-AND "SIN" 

A discussion such as this can hardly be complete without at least some consideration of its relation to society in general. What city, town or hamlet has escaped the grimy fingers of crime? Dr. Berman, as a result of his work there, is convinced that crime is caused by faulty functioning of the different glands. In his study and research he found that certain crimes correspond to certain gland disorders. Then our next question: What causes these gland disorders?

The glands will not become unbalanced and nonfunctioning by and of themselves. Therefore, since there are no gland diseases known, the writer's opinion is that this impairment of proper gland functionings is primarily caused by the toxin in the body, the same toxin of

the intestinal parasites. From our previous study of these parasites and their toxins, and their effect upon the body and its organs, we can readily see how this theory is more than probable.
