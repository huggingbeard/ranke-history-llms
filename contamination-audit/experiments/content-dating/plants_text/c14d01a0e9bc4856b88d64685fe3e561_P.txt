CHRIST ON THE BEACH The disciples never again allowed discouragement to deepen into doubt, nor doubt to degenerate into despair, nor despair to so blind their eyes that they could not see Jesus standing on the beach, near by.
When later on, the gaoler's lash came whizzing through the air to cut into the tender flesh of their backs, they knew the Master was comfortingly near.
When they were mobbed by their own countrymen and even stoned by their one-time friends, they felt Him reassuringly close at hand.
When they were faced by the flashing sword of the executioner and deafened by the bitter curses of their foes, they heard His quiet voice again saying, “I am with you, with you now, with you always." Thus the Christ-directed life becomes the confident life.
As we view this demoralized group of fishermen in the empty boat, before the Lord made His presence known, the contrast they form to Him makes us gasp in amazement.
They were beaten men. Their carefully-built and sacredly-treasured castles in the air had been blown away by the breath of those who had cruelly crucified their Lord.
But there was no sign of defeat in that glorious figure on the beach. The whirlwind of hate had hurried Him to the Cross, but its passing had transfigured Him. It had robbed Him of a material body but it had set free a spirit that should yet wing its way to the uttermost corners of the earth with its lifting force and healing power. The explanation of this phenomenon surely lies in the contention of the psychologists and the theologians that Mind, Personality, or Ego is something apart from either body or brain.
Sir Oliver Lodge, the world's profoundest student of the spirit, sums up the situation splendidly in his book, "Why I believe in Immortality" thus:-"If the brain is the mind, if all memory is stored there, if it is, not only the instrument for reproducing and manifesting thoughts and ideas, but is the actual human being a strange notion-then indeed we are feeble, ephemeral creatures, living our thousand months and then returning to the dust from whence we came. A futile sport without permanence and without meaning.
All our hope, faith and charity, all our joy, sorrow and self-sacrifice going for nothing, blotted out and ceasing as a tale that is told." What is the truth of the whole matter? Surely it is that there is behind both body and brain, a being that uses them as instruments for accomplishing its purpose.
True it is, that both body and brain, if damaged by disease, become weak and ineffective media for the THE CHRIST OF THE DAWN transmuting of affection, ideas, plans, hopes and ambitions into concrete results. So does the broken file, the dulled saw or the stalled motor limit the will of its owner.
Not so many months ago, a professor of theology had just completed a course of lectures on immortality when he was stricken with a fatal disease. Within a few days, his doom was pronounced by learned physicians. Just before his earthly end came, he called his students to his bedside and, with an earnestness he had never before displayed he said, "Gentlemen, all I told you in my lectures about the soul and heaven is true. I have called you here to tell you that I have seen the place to which I am going and it is beautiful, too beautiful for words." Any clergyman who served as chaplain at the front during the World War, could give instance after instance of hard-boiled soldiers passing over the great divide, with smiles upon their faces and sentences upon their lips that described the dimly-seen glories of another world. It is easier to believe that what they saw was true, than to accept the pronouncement of certain sceptics that all they said was a result of fever, and that the light in their faces was due to muscular contraction.
So then we come to the conclusion, that behind and beyond body and brain is a mysterious something called personality, mind or soul, which survives the disintegrating forces of death.
In view of this conclusion what are we to say regarding the destiny of that soul? After death, what is there? Does the spirit of man journey directly to heaven or plunge headlong to hell as certain dogmatists assert?

AFTER DEATH-WHAT?
As if in answer to that question comes the murmuring word of our crucified Lord as He hung on the cross and said to the repentant thief at His side, "Today thou shalt be with Me in Paradise." How strange it is, that so little is heard of this realm of purification and progress known as Paradise; especially as Christ went there rather than to Heaven, when He passed away. Students, who in thought, carefully follow Him into every nook and corner of Galilee and Judea, seem to be infected with the same virus that caused the disciples such fear that, at His last hour, "They forsook Him and fled." Why cannot we see that Christ's journey to Paradise, or the place of departed spirits, was but the fulfillment of the promise He made some time before, "I go to prepare a place for you?" Moreover, if "No man hath ascended into Heaven," why should we hope to be exceptions to the rule?
Heaven is the place of perfection, the immediate surroundings of God. Who of us mortal men is fitted to enter its thrice holy precinct's when we die?
Is there anything in the Bible to assure us that at death we are miraculously changed and thus prepared to step within the portals and to be at home in the unutterable glories of Heaven? On the contrary, there is everything to assure us that one minute after death we shall be essentially the same as we are one minute before death, minus, of course, the crippling, clinging body which we then slough off.
Death is but the end of the earthly stage of our spiritual development. It is a graduation from a material environment into an ethereal one. The soul of us, whether it has profited by instruction here below THE CHRIST OF THE DAWN or not, must enter upon the second period of its growth, but amongst more favorable surroundings.
Heaven, with is supernal glories, is the goal at which we aim, the ultimate reward of our quest. But not even the holiest saint, at death, is anywhere near prepared to enter its ineffably pure state. There must be a further preparation and the place of that preparation is Paradise.
Churches which have a threefold arrangement of their interior, called in sequence, Nave, Chancel and Sanctuary, exemplify the truth of the three spheres of spiritual existence. The Nave corresponds to our life on earth. When a person dies, his body is carried to the foot of the Nave to indicate that his earthly journey is run. Steps lead up from the Nave into the Chancel where the white robed choir chant anthems to the glory of God. Under their white surplices however, is worn a black cassock signifying, that in Paradise, purification is not yet complete. At the farthest reaches of the Chancel, a rail stretches from wall to wall called the Altar rail. This separates the second section of the Church from the third, called the Sanctuary. Beyond this rail is the Altar, which represents the Throne of God. It is within this sacred space that the priest invokes God's blessing and administers the sacrament to those who humbly kneel at the rail.
Symbolically the Altar rail represents the judgment bar of God as the Sanctuary represents Heaven. The final judgment will take place after the soul has either responded to, or rejected, the blessed ministrations which will be provided for it in Paradise. Before that bar we shall all stand. The wounded hands of the Redeemer will open its central gates, or close them to AFTER DEATH-WHAT?
us, according as we have laid the foundations of the Temple of our Soul here on earth, and in Paradise shall have built a superstructure that can stand the test Christ will apply to it.
If our eternal destiny of Heaven or hell were decided at the moment the breath leaves our mortal body, how few of us would be fit for Heaven and how few deserving of hell. For there is good and bad in us all.
No one is entirely good nor yet entirely bad. There must be a place of further preparation where the good can be worked into us and the bad worked out of us. That place is Paradise.
No one passes into the realms of Paradise unnoticed and unwelcomed, any more than a baby is born into this life without loving hands being stretched out to supply its needs.
The Mohammedan's Paradise is a place of selfish ease where the faithful loll under shady trees and bask in genial sunshine. The Christian's Paradise is a place of unselfish service. There is activity without hurry, labor without strain, effort without anxiety.
Those who have reached a high state of spiritual development and have learned the secrets of holy living while here on earth, will undoubtedly be given the task of teaching other, less-accomplished souls, the fundamentals of righteousness.
I feel, with the writer of that beautiful story of Paradise, entitled "Intra Muros," that schools and colleges will abound, within the walls of which many of us will be privileged to sit at the feet of such teachers as St. Paul, St. Francis of Assisi, St. Augustine, and indeed more modern saints of God who have nearly attained perfection on earth through tribulation.
I like to think that yonder will be many maternal THE CHRIST OF THE DAWN souls, whose love for little children will have fitted them to receive and care for the millions of tender infants who pass from the earth sphere every year.
Undoubtedly there will be a compassionate reception for those who, under the unbearable strain of earth life, have decided to end it all by suicide. Spirit beings, who themselves once plumbed the depths of sorrow and were confronted by mountains of difficulty, but who, in this fairer realm called Paradise, have learned to laugh at fear and smooth away anxiety with a patient smile, will be waiting on the other side of the dark river to receive, comfort and re-educate those who, in a flash and utterly unprepared, hurl themselves over into the spirit world.
And coming from Heaven itself, will be those glorious Creatures, angels and archangels who have been God's faithful servants from the beginning, with their touch of compassion, their smile of cheer, and their word of wisdom for the poor, the halt and the blind who patiently "climb the steep ascent" towards perfection.
But best of all, the glistening figure of Him who appeared on the beach to his weary and discouraged disciples, after they had spent a fruitless night of toil, will give food to the hungry, water to the thristy and assurance to those who doubt.
After death, what? Why Paradise with its cordial welcome, its loving ministrations, its light after darkness, and its Christ after chaos.
