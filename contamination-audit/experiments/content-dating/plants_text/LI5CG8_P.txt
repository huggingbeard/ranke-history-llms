The study is also designed as a guide for the retail druggist. By comparing his rent cost with that shown for stores of like volume. he may readily determine the extent to which such cost is excessive. 

Also the section dealing with prescriptions will enable him to ascertain the extent to which he varies from the average for his class of store in the number of prescriptions filled. The relative importance of each of the several departments which make up a modern drug store is shown, and by a proper adjustment, after a judicial comparison, it may be possible to increase the volume in those departments which are falling short of average results. 

The druggist who contemplates opening a store may also use the study to advantage. From it may be determined what rent should be paid for a given location by estimating the annual sales volume that can be developed. It also shows the relative importance of each department and enables him to measure roughly the amount of prescription business that he may reasonably hope to develop.

MERCHANDISING IN CITY DRUG STORES INTRODUCTION 

For the purpose of this study a city drug store is defined as one which is located in the metropolitan area of a city in which there is one or more drug wholesalers. 

The data for this study were gathered through personal contact with 250 retail druggists located in the metropolitan area of St. 

Louis, Mo.

In gathering this information the Department of Commerce representatives making the contacts were guided by a list of 100 questions, all of which were reviewed and approved by the trade before the study began.

The stores participating in the study were selected by a method which allowed no one type a disproportionate representation. The study, therefore, constitutes an accurate cross-section of the retail drug trade of the city in which the study was made. 

It was found that some of the druggists could not answer every question accurately. All inaccurate or vague answers were discarded, with the result that the same number of answers was not tabulated for each question. In

Several questions did not lend themselves to specific answers. tabulating the answers to such questions considerable care was taken to attach a proper value to each answer.

Some questions represent only the opinions of the druggists interviewed. In such cases it was only these opinions that were tabulated, and no effort was made to check their accuracy from other sources. 

The data contained in this study may be applicable to the drug trade in other cities, unless trade conditions in such cities differ materially from those which obtained in St. Louis at the time the data were gathered.

John R. Bromell, of the Merchandising Research Division, Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce, aided in collecting and compiling the data for this report.

CHARACTERISTICS OF CITY DRUG STORES 

Drug-store longevity.-City drug stores were found to have been in business in their present establishments on an average of 7.94 years. There was no relation between the annual sales volume and the length of time stores had been in business. 

Business organization.-Of all city drug stores, 74.5 per cent were proprietorships (owned by one individual), 8.82 per cent were partnerships, 16.18 per cent were corporations, and 0.50 per cent were estates under the management of an executor or administrator. 1

A relation was found to exist between the size of city drug stores and their type of business organization. Over 83 per cent of the stores with less than $30,000 annual volume were proprietorships, while less than 45 per cent with more than $30,000 annual volume were thus organized. Only 9.43 per cent of the stores with less than $30,000 volume were corporations, while 40 per cent of those with. more than $30,000 annual volume chose the corporate form of organization. Also, partnerships were far more common among stores with large sales volume than they were among stores with small sales volume.

Origin. Of the city drug stores studied, 55.40 per cent were acquired from previous owners and 44.60 per cent were originated by present owners. There was no relation between the size of city drug stores and the method by which present owners acquired them. 

Single or multiple units.-In 75.44 per cent of the cases the business consisted of only one store, and in 24.56 per cent of more than one.

Owners employed in business.-In 93.1 per cent of the cases the owner was employed in the store, and in 6.9 per cent of the cases the owner was not so employed.

Location of store. It was found that 84 per cent of all city drug stores studied were located on a street corner, and that 16 per cent were located in the center of the block. 

Customer entrances.-The study indicated that 19.5 per cent of the stores had more than one customer entrance. In 19.5 per cent of the cases the main customer entrance was in the center of the store front, and in 80.5 per cent of the cases customers entered from a corner. 

Position of soda fountains. Of all city drug stores studied, 96.5 per cent had soda fountains. The soda fountain was located in the front in 59.16 per cent of the stores with fountains, in the center in 

34.55 per cent, and in the rear in 6.29 per cent.

It is seen that the vast majority of city drug stores had their soda fountains located in the front of the store, a few had them in the center, and a small number had them located in the rear. 

As a general rule druggists located their fountains in the place that was most accessible to customers. In the majority of cases where the fountain was located in the rear, the store was almost square and had little depth. The customer was, therefore, inconvenienced_very little, if at all, by the fountain's location in the rear. A few druggists whose fountains were located in the rear were of the opinion that this arrangement enabled them to increase the sale of emotional goods, since each fountain customer viewed more merchandise than he would had the fountain been placed in front.

Orders from retailers to drug wholesalers.-The average order given drug wholesalers by city druggists amounted to $6.80. City

druggists ordered merchandise from each drug wholesale source used on an average of three hundred and ninety-nine times per year. Each order was found to contain on an average 6.28 items, carrying an average item value of $1.08.

Each druggist interviewed stated that it was his policy to order merchandise from each drug wholesale source once each day, with an occasional special order for items which were overlooked when the regular order was given. The above, therefore, indicates that a drug wholesaler receives from the retailer each week six regular orders and approximately two emergency orders.

Since all city druggists followed a standard policy of ordering merchandise from drug wholesalers, there was found to be no relation between the annual volume done by a druggist and the frequency with which he ordered merchandise from drug wholesalers.

The majority of city druggists could suggest no means by which the wholesaler could become a more satisfactory source of supply. 

This may reasonably be interpreted to express satisfaction with the wholesaler and his present methods; and, so far as their relations with the retailer is concerned, the majority of wholesalers were thought to be performing their function well. 

Practically one-third of the city druggists interviewed expressed dissatisfaction with the service received from wholesalers. Investigation shows that in many instances this charge is unreasonable. 

Individual retailers deal with an average of 3.15 wholesalers, each of whom offers one or more deliveries per day. It is therefore possible for a retailer to have as many as six orders filled and delivered each day. This seems far too generous in many instances. It is believed that a well-managed drug store could operate efficiently on a total of four deliveries per week from all wholesale sources with whom it deals.

The credit service offered by wholesalers was also found to be reasonable. A cash discount averaging about 4.5 per cent of sales is offered retailers for the payment of their bills within the period allowed. Only about 70 per cent of the druggists were found to take advantage of this generous allowance, though practically all of them claimed to do so.

It was found, however, that wholesalers made many errors in filling orders and in calculating the totals of the invoices. They could therefore institute a system for checking the accuracy of each invoice with gratifying results for both parties. 

Approximately 1 city druggist in every 10 interviewed complained that the wholesaler's line was not sufficiently broad to satisfy the

66 needs of the retailer; that too many items ordered were marked out." This criticism was found to be only partially justified. Collectively the retailers to whom a wholesaler caters carry such a wide assortment of merchandise that it would be impractical for the wholesaler to stock every item that is occasionally demanded. However, the number of "outs" could be materially lessened if the wholesaler would make a careful analysis of the demands of his trade and then attempt to synchronize his stock with this demand.

The first item in the foregoing statement indicates how much might be accomplished by a better understanding of sound merchandising principles and greater mutual interest in each other's problems. At least nine ways by which retail druggists could make less costly the function of wholesaling seem apparent. A few things which retailers might do to reduce the cost of wholesaling in general are: 

1. Concentrate purchases with few drug wholesalers. 

2. Study stock requirements more closely so that no special orders are necessary. 

3. Order each item of merchandise in larger quantities so that fewer orders will be given and less delivery service required. 

4. Have order ready at a given time so that "call-backs" will be eliminated. 

5. Adhere to the terms of sale more closely and pay bills more promptly. 

6. Do not deduct the cash discount when paying a bill several days after the discount period has expired. 

7. Check all merchandise when it is received so that the deliveryman can verify any discrepancy between the merchandise received and the claims of the invoice. This will obviate the necessity for an investigation by the wholesaler. 

8. Reduce returns by keeping merchandise sent by mistake if such merchandise can be used. The wholesaler should allow a reasonable discount in such cases. 

9. Delay deliverymen as little as possible when goods are delivered. 

Sales to nondrug stores by wholesale druggists.-Of the city druggists contacted, 55.39 per cent believed that wholesale druggists should not sell drug merchandise to nondrug retailers, while 44.61 per cent did not object. 

Cash discounts taken.-In answer to the question, " Do you take all cash discounts offered on purchases?" 89,71 per cent of the druggists answered in the affirmative, and 10.29 per cent stated that they did not. However, a review of the wholesalers' records indicated that not more than 70 per cent of them discount practically all bills. 

PRESCRIPTIONS 

Prescriptions filled.—All city druggists interviewed filled some prescriptions. The per-store average for all stores was 13.61 per day.

Of all prescriptions filled, 73.76 per cent were new prescriptions and 

26.24 per cent were refills.

There was found to be a direct relation between the annual sales volume of city drug stores and the number of prescriptions filled. 

Stores with less than $25,000 annual volume filled on an average of 10.57 prescriptions per day, while those with more than $25,000 annual volume filled on an average of 22.43 prescriptions per day. 

The larger stores also had a slightly larger percentage of total prescriptions represented by refills than was true of the smaller stores. 

Refills for stores with less than $25,000 annual volume amounted to 24.63 per cent of total prescriptions, while for stores with more than $25,000 annual volume they equaled 28.49 per cent of all prescriptions. 

Prescription blanks.-The study indicated that 71.57 per cent of the city druggists interviewed furnished physicians with prescription blanks bearing the names of their stores. A larger percentage of druggists with large sales volume furnished physicians with prescription blanks than was true of druggists with small sales volume. 

Only 68.79 per cent of the druggists with less than $25,000 annual volume furnished physicians with prescription blanks, while 77.78 per cent of those with annual volume of more than $25,000 furnished physicians with prescription blanks.

Discounts to doctors. Each druggist interviewed was asked, "What special discounts do you give doctors?" The answers to this question indicated that 67.66 per cent of all druggists interviewed allowed doctors some type of discount on purchases; that 36.32 per cent sold to doctors at cost; and that 31.34 per cent allowed discounts ranging from 5 to 30 per cent to doctors. The average discount allowed doctors by the druggists who allowed discounts amounted to 14.62 per cent of sales.
