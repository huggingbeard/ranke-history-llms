IX LETTER OF SUBMITTAL FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION, Washington, December 20, 1930.
To the Senate of the United States: This report, which deals with the movement of electric energy across State boundaries, has for its principal purpose the determination of the State and national totals for such interstate movement in the year 1929 with which to compare the generated and consumed quantities by way of ratios for the United States as a whole and for the individual States. While the totals compiled can scarcely be expected to be absolutely complete and exact, it is believed they are sufficiently comprehensive and complete to yield throughly reliable results.
The total quantity of electric energy generated in 1929, considered as the basis for comparison, was 95,582,000,000 kilowatt-hours.
The results found for interstate movement can be conveniently expressed by way of ratios to this and related figures. The gross ratio of quantities exported across State lines to quantities generated during the calendar year 1929 for the 48 States and District of Columbia is 15.18 per cent. The similar gross ratio of quantities imported to quantities consumed is 19.65 per cent. The difference between these two ratios is partly due to losses between points of generation and of consumption, which reduce the total quantity received by consumers by as much as 15,000,000,000 kilowatt-hours. The size of the imports ratio is also affected by the fact that imports over international boundaries into the United States are 1,400,000,000 kilowatt-hours in excess of exports. Aside from this difference between exports to and imports from Canada and Mexico, the aggregates for exports and imports across the boundaries between the several States are naturally identical, since exports of one State are imports of another.
After excluding railroad operations from both terms of the above comparison, the ratio of exported to generated electricity becomes 15.41 per cent and that of imported to consumed electricity 21.49 per cent for the United States as a whole. The exclusion of certain figures for municipal and governmental plants would further increase these ratios of exports to generated through deduction from the generated figure of an amount greater in proportion than the amount deducted from the interstate-movement total.
The ratios above given are subject to qualification from the point of view of the counting of quantities moving in interstate commerce because of the fact that in some cases the same electricity crosses two different State boundaries, or crosses the same boundary more than once.
This results in duplication, which may be eliminated by deduction of the quantities crossing twice or sometimes crossing the same boundary more than twice. When these quantities, so far as ascertainable, have been deducted from the totals for interstate X LETTER OF SUBMITTAL ΧΙ movement the ratios become 13.42 per cent for the ratio of exports to generated and 17.57 per cent for the ratio of imports to consumed.
These figures include the data for railways and municipalities, but exclude duplications, as above noted.
A certain qualification as regards the significance of the above ratios is of some practical as well as technical interest. The quantity moving interstate is determined in effect as of the points of crossing of the State boundaries, though in fact the figures submitted, if metered, are usually as metered at one or the other end of the movement, sometimes at the sending end and sometimes at the receiving end. It is proper to assume, however, that, in the totals or on the average, the interstate-movement figures exclude losses between points of generation and points of crossing and include those between points of crossing and points of receipt or of consumption. In order that the two terms of such ratios be on a strictly comparable basis, all the exports should be as measured at the point of generation, thus including losses from that point to the State line; and all the imports should be as measured at the point of consumption, thus excluding losses between points of crossing and points of consumption.
Thus, with reference to strict comparability, the ratio of exports to generated is too small, and the ratio of imports to consumed is too large.
Since the losses between points of crossing and points of consumption are doubtless larger than between points of generation and of crossing, the imports ratio is presumably more affected by this condition than the exports ratio. For purposes of comparison of exports ratios among themselves, and of imports ratios among themselves, this matter, however, is rarely, if ever, important.
It is of interest to follow the comparison of ratios into the figures for individual States. In this connection it is perhaps best to make the comparison after elimination of duplications. The point at which the duplicated quantity is to be subtracted thus becomes important, since it determines the State from whose total exports or imports the deduction is made and whose ratio is accordingly modified.
The basis of the choice here made between alternative methods is explained in the report in the discussion following Table 3. Certain State ratios (as they stand after the deductions indicated) for exports to generated and for imports to consumed are notably high. As regards the ratio of exports to generated, Vermont shows the highest figure, namely, 76.26 per cent; Maryland is second, with 72.2 per cent; Idaho third, with 58.48 per cent; Rhode Island fourth, with 54.89 per cent; West Virginia fifth, with 44.99 per cent; Louisiana sixth, with 40.88 per cent; and North Carolina seventh, with 38.84 per cent. As regards ratios of imports to consumed, Mississippi ranks first, with 107.49 per cent; Arkansas second, with 103.7 per cent; Delaware third, with 79.1 per cent; Nevada fourth, with 64.81 per cent; Utah fifth, with 64.16 per cent; Missouri sixth, with 58.28 per cent; and West Virginia seventh, with 50.44 per cent. The ratios in the latter group are affected by the importation to some extent being balanced by exportation or reexportation of electric energy.
The real reason in the foregoing two cases of an excess of imports over consumption is the large quantity of exports from these two States, the exports-to-generated ratio being 22 per cent for Mississippi and 26 per cent for Arkansas.

Some attention is paid in the text to indications of the prospect of further increase in the interstate-movement ratio, which appears to have increased considerably in the limited period for which any sort of data are available. Existing reserve capacities of interstate electric lines and proposed additional construction, according to the information available, suggest future increase. Certain technical considerations relating to the trend as regards costs of transmission lines and of generation per kilowatt-hour likewise suggest a further increase in the interstate-movement ratio. But in an industry so subject to revolutionary change as that of electricity supply, great regularity in such matters can not reasonably be expected. That the United States, by reason of technological developments generally, and not least in applications of electricity, is becoming in effect smaller, year by year, would seem to suggest an increasing disregard of State lines in electricity supply as in other matters of industry and trade. To this reference to technological developments might be added mention of the increasingly national scope and the increasingly complicated character of corporate interrelations and aggregations, particularly in the field of electrical utilities-a development doubtless not unrelated to the physical movement of electric energy across State lines.

The commission shall further inquire into, ascertain, and report at the same time upon the percentage of electrical energy generated in each State which is transmitted to other States, and the percentage of electrical energy consumed in each State which is imported from other States.
The commission shall, in connection with its report, where such information can be furnished without unduly delaying the reports herein requested, give the names of persons, firms, corporations, and associations generating and transmitting such electrical energy in the manner herein described, the points at which generated and from which transmitted, and the names of the persons, firms, corporations, or associations to whom such energy is transmitted and the points to which transmitted.

Electric energy may move out of a State and back into it. Even though this probably involves the company so operating in interstate commerce, it may be questioned, at least, whether the second movement back across the same boundary should be counted in determining the quantity so moving. Likewise, a movement of energy from New Hampshire through Massachusetts to Connecticut may be said to be New Hampshire-Connecticut commerce not to be counted at both boundaries. The position taken in this report is that the interstate movement should be compiled for each crossing and the element of "duplication" determined as a separate matter for later subtraction as may seem appropriate. For statistical purposes, it is important to deal with objective and definite physical facts. "Interstate commerce" is not of that nature. Moreover, contact with the details of the reports made shows that in many cases the extent of "duplication" is indeterminate.

Section 2. Statistical Considerations Regarding the Nature and Completeness of the Totals Obtained.
General considerations as to the comprehensiveness of basic data.-- Since the ratios for individual States and for the United States as a whole are the gist of the answer to Senate Resolution 151, it is important to call attention to the dependence of the correctness of these ratios upon the reliability, and particularly upon the comprehensiveness, of the basic quantitative returns of electric energy moving across State lines and of totals generated and consumed. With regard to neither of these classes of figures can it be assumed that absolutely every item that ought to be included is included. But the presumption is that the interstate movement is comprehensively covered, at least as regards important companies participating in such movement, although the figures may sometimes be reported net rather than gross, and thus not show accurately the total. It is true there may be some small interstate movements omitted entirely, but it is believed these movements are not only of small quantities but at low voltages; that they occur in connection with the final distribution of energy to consumers or for railway use; and that they are thus both qualitatively and quantitatively not very significant.
With regard to the comprehensiveness of the totals for energy generated and consumed, the situation is less well defined and less satisfactory than for totals of interstate movement, although for a very different reason than that controlling the results obtained for the latter. The most important qualification as regards both energy generated and energy consumed, as it affects the comprehensiveness of the totals, is the difficulty of getting returns from the smaller enterprises, whose operations are usually entirely intrastate. It has been necessary to meet this situation by estimation for a considerable number of the smaller plants, to the extent indicated for each State in Table 5. The difficulty of getting data in such cases is most generally due to the carelessness and ignorance of those in charge, which can be completely overcome only by the visit of a field agent to the plants.
It is generally true that comprehensive statistical data can not be obtained except by the exercise of compulsion. The employment of compulsory processes, even if the power of the commission to do so in connection with getting comprehensive data of energy generated and consumed by all operating electrical plants were unquestionable, would be so time consuming as to be impracticable for use to complete the data for a report made so soon after the close of the period coveredinterstate MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY at least where the report is not a recurrent affair, but an initial compilation and presumably made once for all.
There is one specific cause of deficiency in the totals of both quantities generated and quantities consumed. This occurs through the omission of operations of certain small plants for some fraction of the year 1929 due to transfer of ownership. Such omissions are not quantitatively important, but it was found difficult to obtain reports relating to the operations of a number of small companies where the company had gone out of existence or, in the case of a municipality, where the records had become obsolete and were no longer anybody's business. Furthermore, where an electrical enterprise was dropped from the list of operating companies on the basis of a report that it had been sold, the report was in some cases not specific as to the date of the transfer, and it did not appear to be worth while to tax the patience of those making so much of a return by further correspondence to find out whether the transfer occurred prior to the beginning of the calendar year 1929. If a report might properly be expected for part of the year, it would often be impossible to find anyone who could and should furnish it. The aggregate of omission due to this situation is small.
In a good many cases duplicate reports were received, partly as a result of duplication in the mailing list, partly perhaps as a result of the sending of two copies of the questionnaire, one of which was intended for the files of the reporting company. The latter was apparently in some cases sent in by some other officer than the one making the regular report. The checking of the tabulation, however, included a reference to such possibilities of duplication and can be relied on as having eliminated any appreciable duplication in the total, unless it may have occurred where a consolidated report was made for a system or merger and also a separate report for some one or more subsidiaries or divisions. This possible source of error, however, is not quantitatively important.
There is a possibility of error in such statistics as those presented in appendix Table I, by reason of the duplication or omission of quantities sold and purchased for resale. This can not affect totals generated, but might lead to over or under statement of quantities consumed, depending on whether some selling companies may have included in sales for consumption some quantities resold by the buyers, or whether some of the reported sales for resale failed to be reported by the buyers so as to be included in totals consumed.
Appendix Table I makes no use of the returns of quantities sold for resale, which were called for by the report form chiefly for the sake of internal checks upon data reported. But the electric energy in question should appear (less losses) in that table as sold to consumers by the purchasing companies. It is possible, however, that some quantities reported as sold for resale may have been finally consumed by the supposed intermediate buyer instead of being resold. In that case the quantities consumed, as compiled, tend to be incomplete.¹ There is also, with a similar effect, more likelihood of failure to report among small purchasing companies than among other classes. But industrial companies that purchase all and resell some energy to utilities or to the public are very few. Those that purchase some but generate more than they resell may properly be considered as not purchasing for resale.

The obtaining of comprehensive totals for energy generated and energy consumed, as well as for that crossing State lines, depends to some extent upon metering practice. Totals of energy generated are quite definitely ascertained by the generating companies, though minor qualifications of reported quantities may require recognition, due especially to difference in practice as regards whether or not all power used for generating station auxiliaries is properly excluded so as to show such quantities on a net station-output basis. The measurement of energy consumed- as in fact appears in the manner of handling this matter in appendix Table I 2-is on a less satisfactory or less accurate basis than that of energy generated. In some cases, however, there is no adequate metering, even of quantities generated. A few small enterprises still appear to supply electric energy to consumers on a flat-rate basis. Moreover, where a plant is operated by a small municipality for street lighting and public buildings only, there is no occasion to meter the energy at the point of consumption, and only such occasion as may arise from attempts to secure greater operating efficiency even to meter the energy generated.
Returns and estimates in place of returns. In statistical compilations there are bound to be gaps in returns, unless authority to compel them is available and exercised, and also unless much field work be done. For the smaller companies and municipalities, neither the exercise of compulsion nor the expense of field work is worth while, if bases are available for making reasonable estimates of the quantities required. Although the number of items thus estimated may be considerable, the kilowatt-hour element is not likely to be large.
As the data of Table 5 suggest, no important company or municipality failed to make a return. It is proper to expect that the extent of the element of estimation entering into the totals, however, be shown.

Unfortunately they appear sometimes to be rather careless estimates, but there is no reason to suppose that they are often biased. Conditions affecting interstate-movement totals.-To consider more in detail questions of comprehensiveness directly affecting the data of interstate movement, some companies assume that power purchased at the State boundary is purchased in the State to which it is sent and that the movement does not involve interstate commerce. This idea is legally indefensible. It has affected form rather than substance in connection with the present inquiry, however, because on occasion it has been explained that this report deals with the physical facts and is not concerned with points at which the legal transfer of electric energy may be effected. Nevertheless, ideas resulting from legal theories often affect persistently the practices of the companies, and it is possible that some interstate movement has escaped compilation because of such ideas.
Ordiwhere the legal transfer occurs well within the State boundary, the situation is such that it could not be expected that the buyer (or seller) within his own State would always appreciate the possibility that his purchase is a transaction in interstate commerce.
narily, in connection with the present inquiry, in such a case the seller's report would show the interstate movement and the delivery to itself at the receiving end. This inquiry has been concerned only to obtain the physical facts.
Net movement either in or out of a State, rather than gross movement both ways, may often have been returned for a different sort of technical reason, the totals thus being made smaller than they should be. This tendency results partly from metering practice, but more fundamentally from the nature and purpose of a large part of the interchange of energy between electrical companies. Where a company purchases its entire supply, the movement of electric energy is in one direction only. The relationship of buyer and seller, however, frequently does not adequately describe the situation as regards intercompany and interstate movement. Much of it occurs by way of an interchange in which one party pays in energy supplied for energy received, the exchanged energy, of course, being supplied at different times by the one and the other party to the arrangement.
Interconnections for the purpose of emergency service, or for improving load-factor and operating conditions for both companies involved, result in interchange rather than purchase and sale. Times when company A can in part supply company B more efficiently than cominterstate MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY pany B can itself generate its total supply may alternate with conditions and times when company B can in part supply company A to better advantage than company A can generate all it needs.
Economical utilization of stream flow is an important factor in the exchange of kilowatt-hours.
Under such circumstances, payments between companies are for net kilowatt-hours only. It may then be difficult to get the accounting and statistical departments of a company to appreciate the significance of a kilowatt-hour exchange that involves no payment or receipt of money. The companies, however, appear generally to have conformed to the commission's request that data be returned on the gross basis, and have made the estimates frequently necessary to put the figures on such a basis. The resulting totals can hardly be expected to be absolutely comprehensive, however, and especially a question may be raised as to the adequacy of some estimates made under such circumstances.
Some utility men are inclined to minimize the significance of such exchanged energy and appear to seek to convey the impression that only sales over the boundary for money are true interstate commerce.
In fact, of course, barter is commerce and a much older and more fundamental form of commerce than is purchase and sale for money.
That the alternation in the direction of movement of electric energy may take place within the same day, moreover, is a result of the impossibility of economically storing electricity and merely one phase of the peculiar importance of load-factor economy in the industry.
Maryland buys many potatoes from Florida early in the year and probably sends a good many to Florida later, because of cyclic alternation of deficient and surplus local supplies. The fact that the cycle in this case is annual instead of diurnal does not change the commercial character of the movement.
With regard to the degree of estimation entering into the totals of interstate movement, it might be supposed by the reader that what is not metered at the State line is estimated as of that point. In that case the element of estimation would be much larger than it is. In fact, metered data for points other than at the State line are usually what is reported. Sometimes an estimated allowance is made to bring the figures to the basis of the State boundary, but usually not.
Hence, the data compiled are in general metered, but not metered at the State line. This is not surprising, and, indeed, it is possible to question whether so much metering at the boundary of power crossing Ŝtate lines as is actually done under present conditions is economical, although done only with reference to the purposes of the electrical companies. The situation by which quantities crossing State lines are so largely either as estimated for such points or as metered elsewhere must, at any rate, be taken as it is.
If this factor in the situation affects only a short distance between points of actual metering and State lines, the results should be accepted as being practically as good as those obtained by metering at the State line. It may be necessary, indeed, to accept the metered data almost regardless of where the metering occurs-that is, at some distance from the State boundary, provided, of course, that the energy metered moves on an interstate electric line.
The nature of the resulting inaccuracy of the compiled figures of interstate movement should be understood. The error involved in INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY accepting the quantity metered at the point where delivered to a receiving company 10 or 20 miles inside the State line is one which tends to be balanced by a similar error in the other direction where the measurement is made some distance from the line on the sending side. Hence, the interstate power is understated by reason of the line loss between the State boundary and the point of metering if on the receiving companies' premises; and it is correspondingly overstated if given as metered similarly within the senders' side of the line; for example, at the generating station. In the grand totals, therefore, on the assumption that the points of metering on the sending and on the receiving side of State lines are about equally important, the errors should tend to balance each other. Adequate information as to the details of this situation is not available.

The problem of what to include in totals generated and consumed.- What should be included in the total of electricity generated for the purpose of computing a ratio between the quantity moving interstate and such a total is not a simple and definite question. The generation of electricity is by no means exclusively an affair of central-station companies that supply the purchasing public.
Industrial plants are generating electricity for their own use in increasing volume. The drawing of the line between these and publicutility plants is not so simple as might be supposed, since industrial plants frequently have a surplus of kilowatt-hours which they dispose of through sale to companies that distribute it or which they themselves may distribute. Especially where the vicinity of the industrial plant, for instance, of a mining corporation, may not be served by a public-utility company, the industrial concern will frequently, as a matter of convenience, supply electricity to its employees and others.
In some States, in order to do this, it has to obtain a franchise as a public utility, and frequently for such reasons it will organize a separate corporation for its electrical department. This does not essentially change the situation; nor does it do away with the difficulty of satisfactorily drawing the line for the total generated to be used in such a report as this.
If the electrical enterprise in such a case is merely an incident of industrial operations, and if none of the power generated and used is used for other than industrial operations, it is hardly correct to include all the electric energy generated by such a plant in a total for comparison with interstate movement, if it is to be assumed that the latter, as determined, occurs in connection with the operations of public-utility enterprises. The existence of a separate organization to operate the entire generating and distributing electric plant of such an enterprise, however, will frequently bring it within the class of public-utility companies for all statistical purposes, even though its chief customer is the associated industrial corporation.
On such grounds and because the reports made by them were appropriate to public-utility companies, certain power-generating subsidiaries of the Aluminum Co. of America have been included in this report.¹ Where the electrical enterprise is generally accepted as a publicutility company, the present inquiry does not try to avoid treating its kilowatt-hours generated as public-utility generation, even though its purpose is fundamentally industrial. Where the plant is evidently industrial, however, only such portion of the total generated as is sold, either to the consuming public or to electrical companies for resale, is included in the totals generated as compiled for this report.

How the line is drawn in appendix Table I.-The above facts regarding the general situation need to be summarized with specific reference to their application to the drawing of the line in obtaining totals of electricity generated for the present inquiry. It is reasonable to infer from the conditions so far indicated that the line of distinction must be rather ragged and indefinite at best.
The general basis of compiling the inclusive totals for generated and consumed is that all public utilities, including railroads, are embraced. Municipal electrical enterprises also are included comprehensively. Industrial companies selling surplus power are included only to the extent of the kilowatt-hours sold by them, either to public-utility companies or to the consuming public; that is, such portion of their generated power as was sold is included in the generated totals. Thus, if they supply adjacent villages, the amount so supplied is included in the total generated and, of course, in the total consumed. Sometimes such plants are supplying tenant houses that the corporation owns, and often the quantity of electricity thus supplied is not metered, and so must be estimated, no doubt, rather roughly. Sometimes such a corporation will regard electricity supplied in this way as a part of its own consumption for industrial purposes. This situation, however, is not important quantitatively and is doubtless a vanishing one; hence, whether, in the compilation of totals, such items are treated consistently or not is of no great importance.
The fundamental principle followed in drawing the line, as indicated above-namely, to stop at public-utility applications and enterprises (both central stations and railroads)—in itself clearly excludes isolated plants that are correctly so described.
Muncipal electrical enterprises are, of course, included. In this connection, where a municipal electric enterpise does not sell to the general public but generates electricity for public purposes only, including street lighting and public buildings, the amount generated and consumed is included in the totals. Such procedure is not in accordance with the analogy of the treatment of industrial companies, in which case, although the industrial enterprise purchasing its energy is a consumer for the public utility from which it purchases if, instead of purchasing, it generates for its own use, the generated quantity falls outside the tabulation altogether. A reason for this difference of method-in addition to the fact that instances where a municipality generates electricity merely for its own use are comparatively fewis suggested by the meaning attaching to the words "public" and "public consumption" and "public supply." Doubtless the word 'public" has at least two distinguishable meanings, but they are closely enough related to suggest the propriety of including in the tables electricity generated by a municipal enterprise exclusively for the use of the municipality. There are exceptions to this, however, since some public buildings in the larger municipalities have their own isolated plants. The fact that these are operated by the municipality INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY is not regarded as a sufficient reason for attempting to include the quantities thus generated in the totals of this inquiry. These isolated plants of municipalities are, or course, quantitatively unimportant.
As implied, a municipality that has an electrical department for the operation of its street lighting system, etc., but purchases the energy used in street lighting and similar operations, is regarded merely as a consumer of electricity and is not separately counted.
The quantity appears in both the generated output and the quantity supplied to consumers as shown by the public utility selling to the municipality. Definition and determination of the quantity consumed.-Reference has been made to the possibility of the absence of accurate data of quantity generated owing to lack of attention to definition or owing to absence of metering. The qualification of totals of quantities generated on such account is practically unimportant. As regards the determination of quantities and totals consumed, the situation is less satisfactory, yet not so bad as it might be.
There is an unfortunate ambiguity as regards the meaning of the word "consumption." In economics "consumption" should mean destruction of goods or services in the process of obtaining from them the uses and enjoyments for which they are produced. Destruction by accident or wasteful destruction, unaccompanied by contribution to satisfaction, is not economic consumption. In the industrial and commercial world, however, and especially from the point of view of sellers, the decisive consideration in connection with consumption is the reduction of existing stocks. Uneconomic destruction serves the purpose of making it necessary to build up supplies by further production just as much as does economic consumption. Furthermore, it is not always possible in compiling business statistics to make a distinction between loss of usefulness and power to satisfy through use and enjoyment and loss due to accident or waste. Often the only way to get at something like the quantity of economic consumption is to take figures of objective destruction, perhaps applying to them some ratio to determine an allowance for the portion lost or wasted in transit and otherwise between the process of production and the process of consumption.
Consumed means used up in actual service, or in the process of contributing to satisfaction, at the place where the service is performed, in the form of light, heat, locomotion, or other motor impulse.
The service may, however, be intermediate or productive, rather than rendered to the ultimate consumer, as in the extensive power and heat uses of electricity in industry.
It happens that electricity supply is one of the few industries where quantities consumed are to a great extent measured close to the point of consumption and where there is thus afforded an objective measure, to a large extent, of the quantity consumed. This must be said with a qualification, however, since, although in the case of most consumption of electricity it is metered on the consumer's premises, there is a small element of loss after it is metered.
As stated, the major portion of electricity supplied by publicutility corporations for general consumption is metered on the consumer's premises. This is not true of all the electric energy supplied by public utilities, however. It is not true of street lighting, INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY which is commonly estimated on hours' use of lamps or perhaps metered at substations. In the case of a street railway using electric energy, moreover, the energy is not metered at the point of consumption, that is, on the car, but presumably at the station or substation.
If metered at the generating station, especially, very considerable conversion and distribution losses occur before the energy reaches the point of consumption; that is, where it propels the car.
Parallel with the ruling out of industrial generation for own industrial use, only such consumption by industries is included in the total consumed as is purchased from public utility companies. It will be noted that the stub of appendix Table I in the lower part (relating to consumption) ignores any data for industrial consumption of energy generated by the industrial plant itself, through taking for generating industrial companies merely the quantity supplied to others.
As the stub of this table indicates, moreover, company use of electricity, both by central stations and by railroads, is deducted.
A practical justification of this in the case of such public utilities is the possibility that, in some cases, though contrary to instructions, electricity used in generating stations has not been deducted before showing station output. For the rest, general principle justifies the exclusion of company use on the basis of regarding such quantities as a part of cost of production. Analogously a company producing gasoline and fuel oil may use some of the fuel oil under its own boilers, in which case it would be proper to include as its output only the fuel oil which could be sold.
An opposite rule, however, has been applied in the case of municipalities, because in general the municipalities have combined their self-service, which is of a public character, with the service of consumers. This suggests that the municipality is not manufacturing electricity in the same sense that a private company does. But practical reasons for treating municipal use for street lighting and the lighting and service of public buildings and public works as a part of consumption, irrespective of whether furnished by the municipal plant itself or not, are, first, that from most such use the general public cooperatively obtains a direct benefit, and, second, that it is consistent to include it in all cases where it must in many cases be included.
Hence, where a municipality generates electricity merely for its own use and private citizens obtain their supply from a utility corporation, the quantity generated and consumed by the municipality is included in the totals of the table. In other words, use by a municipality itself which the general public also directly enjoys to a large extent (e. g., street lighting) is regarded as public use and put along with use by the public, while company use by a private enterprise is not regarded as public use.

The use of these consumption totals as terms in the ratios of imported to consumed quantities for the United States and for the individual States calls for cautionary remark. For thoroughly satisfactory statistical comparison the two terms of a ratio should be on the same basis and entirely comparable; and in showing the relation of part to whole, the 100 per cent term should be no more nor less than the sum of all the parts. But the imports include losses between the State boundary and points of consumption which are already deducted in the figures for quantities consumed. The impropriety of this situation is illustrated by the possibility that the ratio of imports to consumed may exceed 100 per cent, even with no exports to dispose of part of the imports or an equivalent quantity.

This hypothetical extreme case indicates an inherent weakness in the ratio, which, however, does not affect its comparability with like ratios. The ratio of exports to generated, it should be noted, is not such a "like" ratio, but, on the contrary, because of losses between points of generation and export contains an opposite weakness, such that it would be impossible, apart from counterbalancing imports, for the ratio of exports to generated to be quite equal to 100 per cent, with no imports, and with nothing consumed within the State. But it is true that, in general, the distances traversed are greater between points of crossing and of consumption than between points of generation and of crossing, and also that the voltages drop in the former case, so that the imports-to-consumed ratios are more affected by this defect in their constitution than the exportsto-generated ratios by the similar impossibility of putting the two terms of the ratios on the same basis as regards losses.

Recrossings of State boundaries.-Duplication is largely a matter of definition and purpose. Power that crosses two State lines and is counted at both, or that crosses and recrosses the same boundary, may by definition be so labeled. If the transmission line follows the course of a river, and in going from one side to the other with reference to topographical conditions crosses the boundary line a number of times, it may be alleged that this is no more a movement between States, at least no more a movement in interstate commerce, than would be the carriage of physical commodities by a railroad along its line uninterruptedly where the line happens to cross a river boundary several times between the point of origin and the point of destination of the freight. This argument is valid. In conformity with instructions of the Senate resolution, however, the tables show electric energy crossing State lines counted at each crossing, no matter if, in some cases, there is a crossing and recrossing of the same boundary, or the crossing of boundaries between two States in succession, even without there being any power taken off the line between the two boundaries.

"Wholesale and retail" in relation to interstate commerce. -One reason why a classification by voltage of quantities transmitted across State boundaries is given is because of its relation to a distinction that has been made from a legal point of view between what is referred to as "wholesale" and as "retail" interstate movement. The National Electric Light Association bulletin showing interstate movement for the calendar year 1928 attempts to separate quantities moving interstate into these two classes. The distinction need not be discussed here, unless merely to say that it is not fundamentally very significant, since the situation by which a company operates in two States and transmits energy into one, instead of generating it there, where it retails, can easily be so rearranged by the holding company that is presumably in the background that the generating company transmits to a distinct company in the second State, this second company doing the retailing, with the effect that, what was formerly retail movement subject to the jurisdiction of the State authorities, according to the legal classification under discussion, becomes wholesale movement subject only to Federal regulation.
No attempt has been made in this report to make a separation of the total movement of electric energy between States along this line.
If anyone considers making such a separation, it is possible to do so, at least to the extent of achieving a fairly good estimate, on the basis of the voltage data shown in appendix Table III combined with the information regarding the companies at the two ends of the various interstate lines shown in List IV. The latter gives, in addition to the name of the operating company at each end of the interstate movement, the name of the controlling or holding company, a fact of specific significance in relation to the possibilities of the situation by which retail interstate movement can be converted into wholesale interstate movement at the wish of those in control.

Whether energy disposed of to a public-utility company in the State to which sent is sent to the latter company or first to the sending company itself, at least where the sending company operates the transmission lines and transformers, is the form the question often takes. A company that buys directly off the transmission line from another State, however, might be considered as engaged in interstate commerce, regardless of the distance of the point of its purchase from the State line. How a distinction is to be made between the interstate line and connected intrastate lines, however, arises to confuse the issue. The stepping down or INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY the stepping up of the voltage might perhaps be regarded as defining the terminus of the interstate line; but if so, the fact that the energy, or most of the energy, purchased by a company came across a State line might not be decisive. If the source of the energy is decisive, then a question arises as to what is the source when the line from which energy is taken is connected with lines crossing the State boundary but is also tapped for consumption and fed by further generators between the crossing and the point where the energy in question is taken off. The distinction between interstate and intrastate commerce appears to be largely a question of degree and can not always be easily made. One thing is generally admitted, however, namely, that the separation of sending and receiving company operations at the State line does not affect the status of either buying or selling company as regards involvement in interstate commerce.
In this case the entire amount of energy purchased is sold and goes directly across the State line with no intervening reenforcement or depletion. However great may be the difficulty of drawing the line between what is interstate and what is not interstate commerce in electricity, that difficulty arises beyond the State boundary itself.
It is nevertheless a fact that legal advisors of utility companies have sometimes apparently assumed that a contract effecting legal transfer at the crossing would keep the companies out of interstate commerce.
This report, however, is not concerned with the legal question as to just what is and what is not interstate commerce. Its purpose is merely to show the quantity of the interstate movement of electric energy, together with such accompanying descriptive data as may be significant of the nature of the movement, its importance, and what companies are concerned. Whether the companies listed as directly or indirectly concerned in the movement are involved in interstate commerce it is not proposed to decide.
On the ground of this deliberate lack of concern with the legal question as to what is interstate commerce, it may be inferred that the present report attaches but little importance to making a distinction between wholesale interstate transfers and retail interstate transfers, with the implied difference between the two as regards the degree of Federal authority. This matter is referred to above at page 42.

Section 5. Prospects of Further Increase in the Interstate Ratio.
Indicated increase in 1929 and possible normal rate of growth.-There is no available figure for interstate movement during the previous year directly comparable with the results obtained by the present inquiry. The comparison made in the subsection at page 8 above may be taken to indicate that the National Electric Light Association figure for 1929 is fairly complete, and the competence of its statistical staff may be taken to mean that its own figures for 1928 and 1929 are on the same basis. Hence it may be assumed that the increase obtained by it from 10.90 to 11.84 per cent of the total generated is substantially correct for figures compiled on a broader and more adequate basis than that of the National Electric Light Association.
The kilowatt-hour increase is, of course, much more conspicuous, because of the very rapid increase in generation and consumption in 1929.
If the increase in the interstate movement ratio in 1929 over 1928 was nearly 1 point on a base of 10.90 per cent, it might be inferred that the interstate movement will in 10 years be twice as important as it is now. But there can be no assurance that the 1928-29 change is typical and will remain so for the next 10 years. The opening of the Conowingo plant in March, 1928, may seem to contribute an important nontypical element to the 1929 increase, since the latter year includes 12 months' operation as compared with 10 months partial operation in 1928. The increase for 1929 in energy generated at Conowingo was 393,000,000 kilowatt-hours. The State Line Generating Co. was put into operation in Indiana about the middle.
of the year 1929 and contributed 505,000,000 kilowatt-hours to the total generated. Presumably practically all of this, as well as the increase in the quantity generated at Conowingo went out on interstate electric lines. The increase in exports shown by the National Electric Light Association was 1,936,000,000 kilowatt-hours. Considerably less than half of the increase was, therefore, accounted for by these two stations. Such irregularly large contributions, moreover, are legitimate elements in the general average increase from year to year, and the only question is what part of the contribution will wash down in the general average. Certainly not all will. The INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY fact is, there are bound to be irregular elements in the increase of any particular year, and only the availability of records for a long series of years makes it possible to pass judgment upon a figure for one year as fairly representative of, or larger or smaller than, an average that may be expected to repeat itself.
In the present state of statistical information some other method of deciding whether a continued large increase in the ratio of interstate power may be expected is needed. Hence the data and considerations presented in the following subsections.
Reserve capacity of existing interstate electric lines.-The questionnaire (see Exhibit VIII) asks, with reference to lines crossing State boundaries, for "Full capacity of line as at present equipped" and for "Maximum ultimate capacity for line as designed." In connection with the first of these two questions, rated capacity is defined as "Rated kilovolt-amperes of transformers controlling the line." No attempt has been made, by follow ups, etc., to obtain comprehensive answers to these questions. Therefore the results commented on below are based on partial returns, or sample data. In compiling, furthermore, no attempt was made to sift out duplication due to reports being made by the different companies at the two ends of the line.
For 23 per cent of the lines thus tabulated, either no report is made or only one item is reported, so that no comparison is possible. For 41 per cent, present capacity and maximum capacity are reported as the same, indicating perhaps chiefly a tendency to undue economy of thought, especially as to the meaning of design, though in some cases growth of use may have already taken up any element of capacity provided for in plans beyond immediate need. It is not certain how the word "designed" may have been taken by those reporting for the remaining 36 per cent of the lines here tabulated; and it would scarcely have been practicable to define exactly how it should be taken.
For the 36 per cent of instances where comparison is possible, maximum ultimate capacity is 68 per cent greater than full present capacity. There is no reason to suppose that the situation is greatly different, as regards such reserve capacity, for the lines for which similar data are not made available. But the fact that the average present capacity for this group is smaller than that of others for which present capacity is reported-about 7,500 as compared with 10,000—may well mean that the reserve is nearer 50 per cent than 68 per cent.
Provision of a 50 per cent reserve indicates pragmatic expectation of growth in the quantity of electricity to be moved interstate. The increase in the interstate ratio, however, is a different matter.
Projected additional interstate electric lines. In the report form used there is included the question: "Have you any plans or projects under consideration or in hand involving the sending or receiving of electric energy across a State line at additional points within the coming year?" The unidirectional flow from favored situations for generation to consuming districts may be expected to be much more important than reciprocal exchanges; and some of it will take the form of exports from one section of a State that in another section imports; and some of energy passing entirely through a State.
Although electricity can be transmitted over hundreds of miles practically instantaneously, the movement is not accomplished without considerable expense or loss. Hence there is an economic limitation, though an elastic one, upon the distance which it may be expected electricity will be moved from an advantageous point for generation to a consuming district, even though the generation be cheap by reason of abundant water power or other favoring condition.

The Background: A Network of Corporate as Well as Physical Interconnections Companies operating in two or more States without interstate physical connection, and the significance of this situation.-List VI is supplementary to List IV and shows interstate companies in a different sense from that in which those in List IV may be regarded as interstate. These are companies operating in more than one State but with no connecting electric line between them crossing a State boundary. The list is presumably substantially complete, but the condition to which it relates is a changing one and rather accidental as regards its constitution than of permanent interest.
The separate incorporation of companies operating in different States is sometimes necessary in order to conform to the law of the State in which the company may be operating. Aside from such a case for separation of operations by State lines, it is the general practice of the larger holding-company systems to have one or more separate operating companies for the business conducted in each separate State, whether in order to facilitate conforming to regulations of State commissions or for other reasons. Indeed, several important holding-company men have stated that State lines constitute practically the only reason for the permanent existence of a large number of separate companies in a holding-company group. Hence INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY the situation by which a company operates in two or more States without interconnection is probably transitional or local and may be largely due to the absence of well-developed regulation of public utilities in the States concerned.
List VI is naturally rather brief. It consists in the main of rather small companies whose units are sometimes in the intermediate states of transition from independent operation to absorption by the larger systems. An important power company with operations in two States that are susceptible of unification will tend to interconnect them for the sake of efficiency and economy. But if the needed unity of operation can be sufficiently provided with separate operating companies in the several States, the development of separate operating companies is to be expected.
The fact that, in addition to the electrical companies that are in some sense interstate by reason of their sending or receiving electric energy across a State line, there are a considerable number which operate in two or more States with no physical connection between them has a certain implication which is worth explicit development.
As stated above, the principal abiding reason for the separate corporate existence of numerous operating subsidiaries appears to be the need of conforming to the different laws and administrative institutions of different States. It is therefore an accident of the particular situation that a given company operates in more than one State, since substantially the same condition in other cases presents itself in the form of the existence of separate operating companies for the several States, which are controlled by the same holding company instead of being combined in one operating company.
this situation is the gist of the holding company scheme. Such a holding company is thus as much an interstate phenomenon as is physical interconnection across State lines; and, in relation to public administration, it is at least as important a problem as is the interstate movement of electricity.
Groups of interconnected stations. It was proposed to present in this report a list of groups of interconnected electrical companies showing the extent to which the electric energy supplied by one company might be in part derived from energy generated in various places, some rather remote from the place of consumption, or perhaps merely relayed from rather remote points.
The attempt to show interconnected groups is interesting, and a few years ago it might have resulted in what could be called a list.
At present, interconnections are so general that it would be impossible to tell where to stop in defining such a group. At present, power is transmitted by continuous lines such that, so far as physical connections are the decisive factor, it might readily pass from Boston to Minneapolis or from Boston to the Gulf States. Of course, such movement would involve many changes in voltage, and actual operating conditions could not by any possibility be made consistent with the claim that specific kilowatt-hours at present move from one extreme point to another extreme point of the interconnected network.
Convenient evidence of general interconnection is afforded by the Annalist map of high-tension electric lines, published annually. The latest issue shows all States east of the Mississippi continuously interconnected by lines of 30,000 volts or more, with an apparent break near the western boundary of Maine, another extending through INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY the middle of the States of Mississippi and Tennessee and cutting off the southwest corner of Kentucky, then extending northwest across Missouri and similarly approximately through the middle of Iowa and Minnesota to the Canadian boundary. The Provinces of Ontario and Quebec are also tied in with this group of States. The connections referred to extend clear across the States, and not merely across the boundary, except in the case of Maine, as described above, and with a qualification as to southern Delaware. This map does not profess to show lines below 30,000 volts and in this respect does not give a complete picture of interconnections. West of the States referred to the connections are not similarly continuous, though the Pacific Coast States are tied together and the group ties in with with Nevada, Idaho, and Montana.
As an illustration of interconnection the Connecticut Valley Power Exchange a pool whose existence and methods are interesting evidence of the economic nexus between different stations and companies affords a good concrete example.
Four companies are directly concerned in the exchange, namely: The Hartford Electric Light Co. and its subsidiary, the Connecticut Power Co.; the Connecticut Light Power Co.; the Turners Falls Power Electric Co.; and the Central Hudson Gas Electric Co.
These mutually independent companies cooperate with the object at all times of so using their combined generating capacity as to produce at minimum cost the quantity of energy necessary to meet the needs of the participating companies. The central object is the most efficient use of water-power plants owned and operated by the different companies, which are supplemented, of course, by certain large and efficient steam plants and other less modern and less efficient steam plants as well. To this end, the exchange functions as a reservoir into which energy flows from the different companies and is dispatched and distributed to a large extent according to orders of the manager of the exchange, or his assistants. The manager's office schedules the production of the different water-power and steam units in accordance with conditions as to stream flow and anticipated load throughout the area covered by the exchange. Hydro plants reporting abundant water are scheduled for full production, those having shortages are scheduled for whatever their water supply will carry, and the remaining load is assigned to the steam plants in the order of their relative efficiencies up to the estimated needs of the pool area. The operation of the exchange necessitates constant adjustment and rearrangement of the load with varying stream flow and load conditions.
As regards points of origin of power entering the exchange, it is to be noted that the four companies immediately concerned are in turn interconnected with other companies. Theoretically, therefore, the sources of power entering the exchange are far more extensive than the plants of the four companies. For instance, the Central Hudson Corporation is connected to the south with New Jersey and Pennsylvania companies, and to the north with the Adirondack company group. It is possible that power generated in New Jersey or Pennsylvania has been imported by way of the Poughkeepsie and Falls Village line of the Central Hudson Corporation into Connecticut and distributed over the Connecticut Light Power Co. lines. Similarly, the Turners Falls Co. is interconnected to the west with New York power companies that are in turn interconnected as far west as INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY Buffalo and northward to power sources along the St. Lawrence River. To the North, the Turners Falls Co. has connections with Vermont and New Hampshire power sources, and to the east, through the New England Power Co., interconnections extend to the Edison Electric Co. of Boston and the Narragansett Co. in Rhode Island. While the physical movement of power, for instance, from the Narragansett or Boston Edison Co. to Hartford may not actually take place, it is at least theoretically possible. The same may be said of the inward or outward movement of energy to or from the exchange in any other direction.
The above two paragraphs are stated substantially in the words of an officer of one of the members of the exchange. Illustrations of the extent of interconnection given should not be taken as defining its limits, but rather as showing what connections operating officers believe to be of some practical interest. In general, physical interconnections appear to be more common between companies of the same system; at least the data of List IV suggests as much.

EXHIBIT IX CERTAIN CORRESPONDENCE BETWEEN THE MANAGING DIRECTOR OF THE NATIONAL ELECTRIC LIGHT ASSOCIATION AND THE CHIEF ECONOMIST OF THE FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION The following letters and memorandum are here reproduced because of the light they throw on the plan of the tables contained in this report and in order to include expressions of opinion of the National Electric Light Association upon this INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY subject where those using the tables may have full opportunity to take account of such opinions and viewpoints: NATIONAL ELECTRIC LIGHT ASSOCIATION, New York, November 1, 1930.
Dr. GEORGE P. WATKINS, Chief Statistician, Economic Division, Federal Trade Commission, Washington, D. C.
DEAR DOCTOR WATKINS: Since you were here on Thursday to discuss the report on interstate power, I have given further thought to some of the points we discussed.
It seems to me that the inclusion in your totals of electric-power transactions of some industrial organizations, particularly the Aluminum Co. of America, will give a misconception as to the quantity of electric power provided by public utilities which crosses State lines.
Since the quantity of power reported by the Aluminum Co. of America, for example, as crossing State lines is very large, but nearly all is used by the company in its own operations, the company merely generating in one State and transmitting to its own plants in the other and selling only a negligible percentage to the public, it would appear as if this company should not be included in that sector of the information covering public-utility operations.
This Another point that I have in mind relates to the basis you suggested of comparing total imported power against power sold to ultimate consumers.
basis of comparison completely overlooks the losses of energy sustained on imported power in transmission and distribution between the State line and the ultimate consumer. It also overlooks corresponding losses of energy incurred between power houses generating within the State and the ultimate consumers; that is, in effecting delivery. Since these losses, on the average, amount to about 16 per cent, a large degree of error results if imported power is compared against only that finally delivered to the consumer. In all of its considerations, the utility company and the regulatory commission must take into account these losses of energy which from the very nature of electric transmissions are incurred.
It would be more accurate if you were to give the complete picture in your tabulations and specifically to include 1. Power imported into the State.
2. Power generated within the State.
3. Power lost in distribution or used internally by the company.
4. Sales to ultimate consumers.
In arriving at percentages, it is evident that consideration must be given to these losses. A true comparison can be made only by relating the total imported power after lost power is deducted with the total actually sold to ultimate consumers.
I respectfully request that you give these suggestions your most considered attention.
With respect to the tabularized information by States, as I understand it, these are the over-all figures including not only transfers across State lines by electric utility companies, but those, as well, made by steam railroads, electric railways, manufacturing plants, and that eliminations have not been made_of duplications arising from repeated State line crossings of the same power. Because of this, your figures for the individual States will have wide variations from those issued by us, which are for electric power utilities only.
If for any reason it is impracticable or difficult for you to make the above deductions for the individual States, reducing them down to State transfers by utility companies as you have already done on the national totals, I believe a clear statement should be made in connection with each table indicating that these deductions have not been made. This, in order that those interested in the specific State figures may not be confused and given an overexaggerated picture of the interstate movement as it relates to public utilities.
In our conference, Major Bennion made a point which it seems to me will be of interest to the Senate in getting a correct picture of the interstate power situation, and which may properly be included in your report. That is, it may be pointed out that approximately two-thirds of all the power crossing State lines from electric utility sources comes from a relatively few large electric generating plants located at or near the boundary lines, constructed in whole or in part to serve a market in an adjoining State. The Conowingo hydroelectric plant and the new State line plant in Chicago are examples of this.
Yours very truly, PAUL S. CLAPP, Managing Director.

MEMORANDUM FOR THE CHIEF ECONOMIST REGARDING LETTER OF NOVEMBER 1, FROM PAUL S. CLAPP, MANAGING DIRECTOR NATIONAL ELECTRIC LIGHT ASSOCIATION Herewith is submitted for your consideration a memorandum commenting on the accompanying letter from Mr. Clapp.
With reference to the inclusion of some industrial companies in the figures of the interstate power inquiry it was explained in the conference at Mr. Clapp's office last week that a company classed as industrial was included in the totals generated only to the extent that it supplied electricity for public use, especially to other companies for resale or to general consumers. This procedure is rather rough, as a matter of statistical method, but certainly does not tend to include too much industrial generation. If much emphasis be placed upon the legal definition of a public utility company, however, some other decision or method of treatment might be arrived at. In fact the interstate power inquiry, in this as in other connections, rather avoids questions involving complicated and difficult legal definitions. Therefore a company generating electricity and serving customers is regarded on economic grounds as to some degree a public utility.
The situation as regards the Aluminum Co. of America is rather special, it is true, and the treatment adopted is perhaps best indicated by the fact that no entries are made in the tables for the Aluminum Co. It is true that entries are made for the Knoxville Power Co. and for the Tallassee Power Co.
These appear to be separately organized corporations having for their principal purpose the generation of electric energy. Most of this, it is true, they sell to the allied Aluminum Co. of America. A very considerable amount, however, is reported as sold by the Tallassee Power Co. to other companies for resale. On the basis of the separate organization of these electric power companies and the very appropriate statements in their returns showing sales to other companies for resale and sales to general consumers, they are classed with public utilities. In so classifying them the inquiry offers no decision as to their legal character.
The report happens to give the specific quantities transmitted across the North Carolina-Tennessee State line by these companies. The quantities generated and those distributed to other companies for resale, it is true, appear only in combined form in the general table. The only modification of this treatment that would seem appropriate to the undersigned is possibly the statement of the quantities generated, etc., within the State of North Carolina by these two companies together with such other information as would make it possible accurately 1 This conference dealt with the comparison of National Electric Light Association figures for interstate movement in 1929 with the results obtained by the interstate power inquiry.

INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY and completely to eliminate them from the State totals affected by their inclusion.
Whether it is desirable to do this, however, is a question, since the report does not in general give quantities for companies as such.
The third paragraph of Mr. Clapp's letter refers to the Aluminum Co. in a way that would be appropriate if the power companies were organized merely as departments of the industrial company. The fact is that they are organized and report as power companies selling to other companies for resale and to general consumers, public and private, including the Aluminum Co. of America. The inquiry has no report from the Aluminum Co. of America as such.
The statement in the letter that the basis of comparison of imports with consumed "completely overlooks the losses of energy sustained on imported power in transmission and distribution between the State line and the ultimate consumer" is not correct. Perhaps what is meant is that no attempt is made to determine quantitatively this factor in the comparison. It is clearly recognized that the two terms of this comparison are not on the same basis. As to the statement that the method "also overlooks corresponding losses of energy incurred between power houses generating within the State and the ultimate consumer," that is either incorrect or much misunderstood by the undersigned. The totals consumed, as compiled by the inquiry, exclude losses.
As regards the fifth paragraph of the letter, all the four items mentioned are set forth in detail for each State in the general or appendix tables.
The undersigned is not impressed by the logic of arguing the lack of comparability between imports and consumed while ignoring the similar lack of comparability between exports and generated, although it may be admitted that the element of loss constituting the discrepancy between the two terms of the ratio is greater in the imported-to-consumed than in the exported-to-generated comparison. It occurs to the undersigned, however, that the deduction of the entire loss from the State line to the consumer in making comparisons would perhaps not be indisputably correct, because the power is usually purchased by a distributing company and in such cases most of the loss would occur after its purchase within the importing State. In such a situation a question arises as to whether this latter element of loss should be deducted from the imports, even though it is conceded that measurement at the State line is not correct for such a comparison.
But this point perhaps merely suggests the futility of attempting to bring in legal conceptions of " commerce," etc., where the first thing to consider is the appropriateness of the statistical ratios arrived at. From the statistical point of view it may not matter very much that the imports-to-consumed ratio is in general too large by reason of the lack of comparability between the two terms.
Statistically it is important that the ratios be on a basis comparable with one another rather than they be absolutely exact, if that is ever to be expected. The imports ratios for the various states are comparable with one another, though of course not comparable with the exports ratios, which are in turn comparable with one another. Furthermore, the attempt to put the two terms of both these sets of ratios on a comparable basis (and it should of course be done for both if for either) would make all the ratios to an undesirably large extent estimated instead of based on objective returns.
It is a fact that the general tables of the report include the data for steam railroads, electric railways, manufacturing plants, etc., but these various elements can be eliminated from the individual State totals with the aid of the detail shown in the various tables themselves or else with the aid of separately compiled figures shown by States in various text tables. Doubtless there will be wide variations from the National Electric Light Association's figures for the individual States, but it is believed that the basis of compilation conforms to the implications of the Senate resolution and therefore should stand, of course as accompanied by the supplementary information making it possible to modify this basis as may be desired by persons making an intelligent use of the information. It is believed, also, that the situation is clearly indicated in the tables and in various other connections and that anyone who misuses the ratios is irresponsibly careless. It would be rather impractical at this stage to make the deductions individually for the various States in the tables.
The possible implication of Mr. Clapp's letter that the ratio of interstate movement is exaggerated by reason of the inclusiveness of the tables is not correct, since the taking out of railroads, for instance, would remove proportionatley more generated energy from the totals than it would interstate-movement energy from the corresponding totals for the other end of the ratio. The same is true of municipalities. There are no important industrial interstate lines that are included except in the case of the Aluminum Co.'s subsidiaries, which are INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY not regarded as industrial, so that in general the effect of narrowing the basis of the tables would be to increase the ratios of interstate movement.
With reference to the further point made by Major Bennion, that so large a proportion of the total electric energy crossing State lines comes from sources close to the boundary, that fact is of interest and it would be appropriate for the National Electric Light Association to remind the public and the Senate of this situation as occasion arises. It would not be possible for the inquiry to deal with this situation comprehensively, at least not at this stage of developments, and a fragmentary presentation of the facts is undesirable. In fact, there are difficulties in determining the source from which most of the power crossing State lines comes that have made it impossible to do all that might be taken to be suggested or required by the Senate resolution along this or similar lines. By way of general comment, also, does the fact that there are power stations frequently located just across the line from their market signify merely that the interstate movement is unduly exaggerated by such situations? Would it not be highly regretable if power stations were to be located with regard to political boundaries between the States, rather than with regard to economic considerations which should ignore such boundaries? If there has been a tendency to pay attention to questions of jurisdiction in such matters, it is perhaps less important that the interstate ratio may thus have been made smaller than it otherwise would be than that the utility companies have attached more importance to avoiding the possibility of Federal jurisdiction than to obtaining the utmost economies of plant location.
In brief, it is difficult to change the form of the tables in the interstate power report at this stage, or to extend them in a way to show further detail, and it is believed that legitimate needs of students of the data for the presentation of alternative figures making it possible to put the comparisons on a different basis have been very well anticipated. Such has been the purpose and plan in the organization of the data. It would be almost impossible to anticipate every need, it is true, but those implied in Mr. Clapp's letter, it is believed, have all been anticipated.
NOVEMBER 7, 1930.
G. P. WATKINS, Chief Statistician.

To the Senate of the United States: As directed in Senate Resolution 151, Seventy-first Congress, first session, the commission submits herein a report of progress in the conduct of the interstate power inquiry thereby initiated.
The matter of substantive interest herein reported is the indication of the tentative figures so far compiled that a much larger amount, and a considerably larger percentage, of power generated in the United States moved across State lines in 1929 than in 1928, the comparison being with figures compiled and published for 1928 by a private organization, the National Electric Light Association. The commission is not yet prepared, however, to state in definite figures either the increase or the proportion in question. The data dealt with are discussed in the following paragraphs.
On or before January 2, 1930, questionnaires calling for comprehensive information regarding quantities of electric energy generated INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY and consumed during the calendar year 1929, as well as quantities crossing State lines, were mailed to operating electrical companies, including electric railways and steam railways reported to operate electrified mileage. No attempt was made to cover private electric plants of industrial and other companies except such as may sell energy to others and thus become, in a limited sense, public utilities.
The form of report calls for data for the several States separately, so classified by source and disposition of energy as to provide for compiling the quantity consumed, as well as the quantity generated, in each State.
The two principal parts of the statistical work to be done in connection with the inquiry have rather different characteristics. The data for interstate movement relate to a limited number of companies, and full information should be obtained from every company ascertained to be involved directly in such interstate transmission or distribution. As regards the compilation of total energy generated and consumed in each State, on the other hand, effort needs to be directed almost entirely to securing the comprehensiveness of the totals. The totals to be obtained are of interest to the inquiry only for comparison with quantities of electric energy passing State lines. The number of companies involved is, of course, much greater than in the case of interstate movement, and includes many small companies without statistical personnel. To them the compilation, even of the very simple information required, may seem rather difficult. Hence the achievement of comprehensiveness in these basic totals is naturally a slow process and could not be expected to be realized in the short period of time now elapsed since the questionnaires were sent out.
Information regarding interstate movement, as already stated, is needed from fewer companies, perhaps from only about one-tenth of the companies generating or distributing electricity, and these are, on the whole, the larger companies, usually having permanent engineering and statistical staffs. Although the compilation and return of data for interstate lines is more complicated than that of data for the sources and disposition of energy generated and consumed, the work is not time consuming except so far as, in some cases, rather elaborate methods of estimation may be required where the energy_crossing a State line is not metered somewhere near the line. Hence an attempt has been made to compile fairly comprehensive data for interstate movement, at least for central stations, in time for the present report. Tentative totals of electric energy exported have been made by States. They are apparently fairly complete, except for a few States, although completed only by the use of some figures that are provisional and some that are of uncertain interpretation.
The National Electric Light Association has compiled and published data of electric energy crossing State lines during the calendar year 1928, and the significance of the tentative results obtained so far in the present inquiry may best be brought out by comparison with these 1928 figures. In such a comparison it is necessary to use 1929 totals for central stations only, both because it is not yet possible to check up completely the details, and because of uncertainty as regards the completeness of the list of companies covered. Such totals for interstate movement as have been obtained are not directly presented as such, but only by way of comparisons based upon them. It is believed that the quantitative relations stated will hold regardless of INTERSTATE MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY the changes that checking and the obtaining of further information will bring about in the absolute figures for interstate movement at present reached.

To the Senate of the United States: As directed in Senate Resolution 151, Seventy-first Congress, first session, the commission submits herein a report of the progress in the conduct of the interstate power inquiry thereby initiated.
According to the tabulation so far made for 1929, total interstate, including international, movement of electric energy, as measured in terms of exports across State boundaries, for continental United States excluding Alaska, amounts to approximately 14,500,000,000 kilowatt-hours.
This figure is not complete and final, since a few minor returns have not yet been reported and some adjustments will doubtless be found necessary in the final check-up. Figures reported by sending and receiving companies often do not agree and the identity of the other party to a reported movement is not always clear.
Figures for total electricity generated and consumed in each State and in the United States as a whole, also compiled as a part of the work of the inquiry, are not yet ready for use, although in the final stages of preparation. In the absence of such figures compiled with direct reference to the computation of ratios the comparison implied in such ratios can not be made on an entirely satisfactory basis.
The totals of this inquiry include municipal plants, street railways, electrified steam railroads, and also companies classed as industrial, for such quantities as are sold to the public or to public utility companies. For comparison of the interstate movement with the National Electric Light Association total for private central stations in 1929 these various elements should not be included.
On the other hand, the total for electric energy generated compiled by the power resources division of the Geological Survey is in certain respects more inclusive than is appropriate for comparison with the above figures of exports. The putting of the interstate-movement figure on a basis entirely comparable with either of these requires considerable detailed critical analysis. The movement across a State boundary may be from a private central station to a municipality, or from a municipality to a central station, or a railroad may be involved at either end.
The figure of exports above given is approximately 15 per cent of the total kilowatt-hours reported by the Geological Survey as generated in the United States in 1929, namely, 97,352,385,000 kilowatthours. It is approximately 16 per cent of the National Electric Light Association's figure of 91,249,900,000 kilowatt-hours generated by private central stations. The ratio will not be greatly affected by the difference between a purely private-central-station basis of comparison (for both ends, of course) and the more comprehensive basis, including railways but not including generation by industrial plants for their own use. An analysis of the various elements entering into the interstate movement with reference to seeing how the situation is affected by the basis of comparison will be undertaken in the final report.
With reference to the difference between the above ratios and the 10.90 per cent figure obtained by the National Electric Light Association for private central stations in 1928, it should be noted that the difference is not due merely to growth from 1928 to 1929. The comparison, after allowance is made for differences of basis, is very much affected by the probability that fuller reports of the data of interinterstate MOVEMENT OF ELECTRIC ENERGY state movement have been obtained for 1929 than were obtained by the National Electric Light Association in 1928. There are reasons why one may believe that the total given above for movement across State boundaries is an understatement, rather than an overstatement, especially because of the tendency to underestimate, or even disregard altogether in some cases, back flow over an electric line where the major movement is in one direction and where meters, if any, are not installed at appropriate points or are not of the type that shows kilowatt-hours moving in both directions. On the other hand, the fact that energy recrossing a State line several times or crossing two boundaries is counted at each crossing may tend to swell somewhat the figure for interstate movement by including something that is at least not particularly significant.
The ratios for interstate movement, of course, vary greatly from State to State. In the case of as many as eight States, it appears that the exports are more than half the total amount of energy generated in the State. Likewise, in the case of as many as four States (two included in the above eight), it appears that imports exceed the amount of energy generated in the State. Specific ratios for individual States will be given in the final report.

FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION, Washington, September 19, 1930.
To the Senate of the United States: As directed in Senate Resolution 151, Seventy-first Congress, first session, the commission submits herein a report of progress in the conduct of the interstate power inquiry thereby initiated.
The final report on interstate power is practically complete and will be submitted to the Senate in a short time. The disorganization consequent on the fire occurring in the temporary building occupied by the Federal Trade Commission on August 30 and upon the moving and salvaging of damaged material has delayed somewhat the final stages of the work. The fire also destroyed some portions of the detailed appendix tables which were in a room subjected to severe devastation, so that some of the details may have to be presented in somewhat fragmentary form. The text and text tables were completed in rough draft on the day of the fire and were not damaged, so that all final figures and conclusions are unaffected.
Total interstate movement of electric energy outward across State boundaries, or exports, during the calendar year 1929, amounted to 14,505,190,623 kilowatt-hours. The similar inward movement, or imports, across State boundaries amounted to 15,906,132,127 kilowatt-hours. The difference between these two figures is mainly accounted for by large importations across the international boundary from Canada. These quantities appear in the imports, of course, but not in the exports of the 49 States (including the District of Columbia). Except for this movement across international boundaries to and from Canada and Mexico, the exports and the imports of the States are, of course, identical.

On the basis of the above figures the ratio of outward movement to electric energy generated was 15.31 per cent. Similarly, the ratio of inward movement to electric energy consumed was 19.65 per cent.
The above totals include quantities generated and consumed and the interstate movement of railroads (electrified operations of steam railroads as well as electric street railways being included). The interstate movement for railroads as compiled is smaller relatively to the total of such movement than is the quantity generated or the quantity consumed by railroads in comparison with the corresponding gross totals of electricity generated and consumed. The subtraction of interstate movement for railroads from both terms of the above ratios, therefore, makes them somewhat larger.
In the above totals of outward and inward movement across State boundaries are, of course, included some quantities that cross boundaries twice. For reasons of statistical technique, among others, it was deemed best to compile objective facts as to boundary crossings of electric lines and movements of electric energy thereat. But such a compilation counts twice a quantity moving out of a State and then back into it again, and also twice a quantity moving over a boundary between two States and across the second State and another boundary into a third State. With regard to such duplications, it is not possible definitely and completely to ascertain their quantity, because it frequently or generally happens that between the two crossings the line is tapped for consumption and fed with additional energy, in which case only a theoretical assumption with regard to the origin of the energy crossing at the later intersection of the line makes it possible to compute the element of duplication, and then the reasonable theory or assumption to adopt is that the energy crossing comes from the nearest available generating source. Total duplications of both the above-described classes amounted to 1,680,746,671 kilowatt-hours for exports and 1,680,633,431 kilowatt-hours for imports, the difference being due to a slight element of duplication at the Canadian boundary.
After deducting these duplications from exports and imports, respectively, the ratio of exports to total generated becomes 13.54 per cent and of imports to total consumed 17.57 per cent.
Certain details of quantities and ratios for individual States will be presented in the final report. These are of great interest from the point of view of the inquiry, but their significance can best be apprehended when made available in their entirety for the 49 States (including the District of Columbia) covered by this inquiry.
