CHAPTER II. 

THE WOMAN WAITS 

As the boy at the rail sights the beckoning shores of old New York, a feeling of thanksgiving pervaded his being, a feeling of relief. It was all past-the dancing, the drinking, the lovely women stiffling him with their perfumed attentions. The boy hadn't known there were so many lovely women in the world -and all quite eager for masculine flattery and attention. He was anxious to be ashore-home-four long years since he had seen his lovely mother and his dainty little sister. As the salt spray cooled his face, in which the eager blood of youth was racing madly in anticipation of home, the boy leaned far over the rail and whispered a goodbye to Paris-to the frivolous side of life-and a good day to New York, and he again whispered a prayer to his God to keep him clean and worthy-to preserve his youthful dreams and help him to make them come true. 

While at home, the home whither Howard Norton was bound, a woman waits. For most every man in the world some woman waits, and the caliber of the woman oft times spells success or failure for the man.

Margaret Norton, Howard's mother, regardless of how her world or her God viewed her status, she considered herself a success. A cynic might have, a few years ago, in attempting to describe her, referred to Margaret as a "climber" who had "arrived", but in this modern democratic age the social god oft times allowed certain qualities to outweigh the ancient requisite of blue blood. There were no "climbers" in this modern age; if one had the money, if their rating in 

Bradstreet's was O. K. they just "arrived" and that's that red bloods with cash went "up" and bluebloods without cash-"down." It was one of the ruthless rules established during the late war and whether it pleased or displeased the majority, with few exceptions the rule applied.

Margaret Norton was a perfect hostess, according to certain standards. Her liqueurs were all bonded, or, according to a certain connoisseur, they were "deucedly clever counterfeits"; her cars and aeroplanes were the last word in modern beauty and mechanism. She was beautiful and talented, loved youth and all it embodied, and she sympathized perfectly with modern rebellion at youthful restraint. Her magnificent home was the favorite rendevous of the ultra smart set. Was it any wonder then that her

popularity was increasing and that certain bluebloods of the old school resented Margaret's invasion of their once cloistered domain? 

Margaret Norton came from red blooded ancestors. 

Her husband Phillip Norton, she had driven in the relentless harness of an ambition undauntable-like a beast of burden-money-make money—it was her God-money-it was the eternal magic key that worked the combination to the social realm-money. 

She had driven him on and on-day and night. Phillip Norton had been a mechanic, living to serve God and his fellowman, his family a precious asset, Margaret's word his law.

About the time war burst like a treacherous bomb on a peaceful world, Norton had perfected some patents that started his fortune. Several valuable war devices followed and then the "Street". On and on Margaret had driven him-day and night he clamored about the boards-then something snapped. The human capacity is limited. 

The night Norton had been stricken-he lay quietly in his bed—a feeling of peace hovered over him.

Mildred, his little girl, was a woman in a library famous for her own work, while his son, Howard, so stalwart and handsome, imparts to canvas a picture so real, so worthy, it could not do otherwise than startle the world.

CHAPTER V. 

IN NATURE'S GARDEN 

In the mountainous region of old Mexico nestles a picturesque village torn by two factions. Alvez Nunzio, gambler de luxe, and atheist as well, with his lottery, other gambling devices and dance hall, is the avowed leader, while Father Merton, an American, with God and a decided minority of the population on his side, strives to reclaim the village. Situated in close proximity to the city, the village is a rendevous for sports of all races, who congregate to gamble and partake of Nunzio's hospitality. This despite the rumor of a smouldering volcano that continues to menace the safety of the population.

But for one thing no one would suspect that Nunzio possesses a heart. He has a daughter, Jean Marie, as beautiful and pure as a flower. Jean Marie's Mother, an American, at the death of her Father, had married Nunzio, a half Mexican, for the protection he was able to offer her, and before she was cognizant of his true character. When Jean Marie's Mother died in the city, she had secured a promise from Father Merton, a life

long friend of her family, to watch over the little human flower. Thus it was that when Nunzio had repaired with his lottery to the village, the Padre had followed and established a little church.

Marie, her father. She loves him, she knows that beneath the rough lawless exterior of the man there is a heart. As the girl tripped lightly down the path, the morning sunlight danced among the red gold of her hair, across the gleaming white throat; the Summer breeze rustled the leaves by the roadside, but with it came no warning voice signalling "Dangerous curve ahead" in Life's complicated highway-so, with laughing eyes and happy heart, a girl goes forth to pit her frail strength against the searing sands of Time, the binding cords of Destiny.

As Jean Marie nears the church she is startled by a commotion in the valley. Peter, a little mountain waif, is being used rather badly by some older boys, and Jean Marie takes them to task. "Aren't you boys. earnestly the boys ashamed", she asks so sweetly and hang their head, as the girl drops the white roses which scatter about her feet, kneeling to the ground and holding Peter close to her. Unobserved the young artist reaches his destination.

CHAPTER VI. 

ART-AS GOD INTENDED IT 

Several days have passed since the boys were established in the village. Sponsored by the Padre they had been royally received by the entire village. Howard's sensitive soul received a shock when the Padre had pointed out Nunzio as Jean Marie's father. "It just can't be possible," the younger man had said to the Padre. An angel with a father like that, and then the Padre had told him the story of Jean Marie and her father.

The days were stretching into weeks. The artistic soul of Howard Norton hungered for the sight of Jean Marie. Only on one occasion had he seen her except in the choir of the church, and that was one afternoon when he had been down near the church ruminating on its artistic possibilities; perhaps he would make a painting of it. The setting sun and the late Summer foliage made a gorgeous background for the little structure with its bronze cross nestling so peacefully in the quiet picturesque valley. 

As Howard drank in the beauties of the peaceful

The people of the valley, having become accustomed to its menacing antics no longer feared its possibilities, but to Howard Norton, in his artistic mood, it seemed an evil influence working against God's infinite wonders, even as Nunzio labored in opposition to the good Padre, but the reverie is rudely interrupted.

CHAPTER VII. 

THE CALL OF THE WORLD

Marie and asked her how long before they could be married. He hadn't quite figured out what he would do with a wife in case his queenly mother refused to accept Jean Marie, which of course he figured she would not do-how could any one see her and not love her. CHAPTER VIII. 

A DANGEROUS FLIRTATION 

It was the last evening aboard. By midnight they would dock, and Howard had eluded the boisterous crowd of merrymakers and gone on deck. CHAPTER X.

Then an angel in white singing—a girl, in startling contrast to the surroundings, was leaning on the railing of an upper balcony. The house was darkened while a spotlight played about the mysterious girl, producing rainbow hues. She was singing "Marble

Halls" from "The Bohemian Girl", then came light lilting tunes suitable to the occasion and lastly a modern thing "The Easter Parade". 

As she bowed her way out to a deafening applause, one "butter and egg" man from the big state across the border started for the balcony, another yelled "bring that baby to my table"; then Nunzio's voice, booming, commanding: "This lady" (he did not say "my daughter" but "this lady") has a message for you. You, my friends and patrons, will listen". It was rather a command than otherwise, and was typical of the man-always he had commanded, even his guests. 

The low sweet voice of Jean Marie floated thru the air thick with smoke and the fumes of much liquor, but deadly quiet: "I came by invitation to your celebration", the girl was saying. "I now want you to come to mine. This is Easter morning. In the little church down the valley we're celebrating with early mass, and I'm asking you to come. Will you please?" The girl held her hands aloft in mute supplication, then bowed humbly and was gone.

Nunzio had promised something out of the ordinary for the re-opening of his elaborate Club, but this climax was positively uncanny. The crowd of merry-

makers were stupefied. They sat for a moment with the feeling that it was a figment of their over-taxed brains, that they would wake up presently and find that the angel on the balcony had only been a dream.

Then bedlam reigned. Everyone talked at once, each taking a different view of the surprise finale, but all were agreed on one thing. The girl was a thoroughbred-she hadn't preached to them, she had sung to them in their language-a good sport-the highest ace in the deck, and they really should show their appreciation.

A little later the faces of Jean Marie and the Padre beamed-the little church was filled to overflowing. 

Some of them were a bit dense, many slept in their seats, but they came, almost to a man, and the heart of 

Jean Marie sang. Always, however, her face was turned anxiously toward the door-her father-he had not come-or-maybe he had slipped in unseen.

The Padre spoke to his strange congregation. 

Never had man spoken more eloquently or with more human feeling. The people listened in awe and, when they departed, many dollars, factors of the Gilded 

Palace, had been left behind.

After the services Jean Marie stationed herself near the door; she searched each face eagerly. When the last person had departed the girl bowed her head.

Again Heredity and Environment, in the guise of enemies, had reared their heads. The leopard, indeed, does not change his spots overnight. Alvez Nunzio had sent his friends and patrons, but he, himself, had not come.

CHAPTER XI. 

THE LURE OF INDIAN SUMMER 

Looking into the pale listless face of her son, Margaret Norton began to wonder if she had made a mistake. "To the mountains" Howard's physician had said and, hardly realizing the trend of his footsteps, Indian Summer finds him back at the little mountain valley church. At the spot where he and Jean Marie came often, he had fingered the foliage lovingly. It was Indian Summer again, and he had not come for her according to promise-he had come only to tell her goodbye. The sun was setting in the west-his sun had set too, Fate had robbed him of life's most priceless possession. He entered the little church, kneeling at the altar he poured out his soul in mute appeal to He who alone knows and understands. 

That day Nunzio had gone to the City. Pedro, watching his movements, visits the cottage during his absence. Seeing herself trapped, Jean Marie rushes out madly into the night. On and on she races, at last seeking refuge in the darkened church. In the darkness, Howard is unable to see Jean Marie's face,

but gleans the fact that a woman is in distress. As she rushes toward the altar for protection, Pedro's flashlight from the window illumines her face, and Howard strains Jean Marie to his heart in an agony of love. She no longer wonders why no word had come from him in the long months of waiting, she only knows that it is Indian Summer, and of course he had come to keep his troth. 

She no longer doubts or is afraid. She doesn't even remember to tell him what had frightened her. She knows only that she is in his arms again-nothing else matters.

Pedro intercepts Nunzio on his way back from the city, and tells him that Jean Marie is in the darkened church in the arms of the handsome American. Pedro spreads the news and a crowd congregates at the church, a ray of moonlight falls thru the window on 

Jean Marie and Howard seated on the altar, her head pillowed on his shoulder, like a tired, bruised little homing pigeon that had found its haven of rest and protection after having been tossed relentlessly with the tide.

Nunzio advances on Howard, his menacing dirk flashing, but the Padre lights the church and bars the way. The Padre looks pleadingly at Howard. Jean

Marie's reputation and the faith of her followers are at stake, surely he would speak. And Howard, poor miserable soul, what could he say that he was a married man? He looks at Jean Marie, the white flower drooping under the weight of his love, what difference did lies make, God would understand and forgive-sohe says the only thing that would excuse Jean Marie in the eyes of the people gaping, in curiosity, and those who believed in her. "I have come to claim my affianced wife", and the Padre gives thanks for the magic words.

The crowd, sponsored by Nunzio, suggests a marriage. Nunzio eyes Howard suspiciously, as he writhes under the suggestion, but he cannot protest, as Nunzio's eyes seem to penetrate his innermost thoughts. 

The Padre prepares for the ceremony. "Until death do us part", Howard Norton repeated, and then stood aghast at the enormity of his crime. "Father in Heaven," he breathed under his breath, "forgive me and help me".

In the Nunzio cottage Jean Marie packs her things with a smile to accompany her husband. She breathed a prayer to her God also to make her worthy of her husband's love. He was so good, so wonderful, it all seemed too good to be true. She felt so little and in-

significant. She would be worthy of him, build herself up intellectually to his standard, she would study-she had to make him proud of her-make him happy always she loved him so.

Howard had gone in search of the Padre. He would make a clean breast of it all and act as the Padre directed-it was a terrible task he had set for himself, but it was the only thing to do-the only honorable thing and he owed that to Jean Marie at least-he could give her the continued protection of the Padre regardless of what the verdict of the Padre would mean to him. He would tell him, on his knees, the whole story-the loss of his picture, his consequent illness, and how he had married the Countess believing it to be Jean Marie-how that after marrying the Countess he had not been able to break with her, due to her position and the resultant effect on the happiness of the Countess. Some how, some way, the boy believed 

God and the Padre would conspire to breach the gap between the girl he loved better than life and his own contemptible self, for the further he tried to reason, the more complicated the future seemed. 

Howard listens in a dazed sort of way to the words of the Padre's housekeeper who answers his knock. "The Padre had been called by cablegram to the bedside

of his sister in London. He barely had time to catch the train after leaving the church. He had left a hurried blessing for the bride and groom, promising to look them up immediately on his return. 

In the taxi Jean Marie was waiting for Howard who had gone in search of the Padre. "Gone! Merciful 

God", Howard asks himself, as he stumbles blindly toward the taxi, "what is to be done with Jean Marie, she isn't his wife, never was, and only God knew if she ever would be."

Mechanically Howard returns to the taxi-he was in a daze. He felt the soft lips of Jean Marie on his, the warm blood surged back into his heart-he loved her, God, how he loved her he had to move heaven and earth to protect her. When the Padre returned he must find his little white flower white still, and with a happy smile-and to that task I shall dedicate my heart and soul. 

Howard had purchased tickets for New York-he would have to go back and fight clean and fair. He knew the Countess was not vicious or naturally cruel, she had just ruled the social world until she felt that her power was invulnerable. When she could be made to realize surely she would understand-she must-it was the only way out.

In their stateroom that night Jean Marie was ravishingly beautiful in her simple negligee of shell pink with her glorious curls falling about her shoulders.

Howard sat rigidly erect trying to think of something except the loveliness of the girl. He tried to think of her in the role of a little girl and he her noble protector. Again, she was the wife of his friend whom he had sworn to protect during his absence, etc., etc., but ever and anon the trail led back to the beginning, she was his in spite of laws and conventions, he loved her, she loved him-surely in the eyes of God nothing else matters but that, "but it does matter, old man, he whispered to his tortured heart, it means everythingto her to you for if I violate the Padre's confidence in me, I shall always hate myself, and self contempt is about the meanest state in the world," and so he turned out the light to shut out her maddening beauty, the lure of her soft warm arms and lips that were an open invitation. He knelt beside her-the beautiful, treacherous moon sent its rays thru the window to compete with the artificial light he had scorned, and she was more desirable than ever. "Jean Marie", he breathed her name so faintly, so reverently, it was like a prayer. "Will you listen, my darling, and try to understand, remembering that I

love you more than my life?" And then he explained to her that they would have to be separated for a short time. He said many things she could not understand, did not care to, she knew only that she loved him and would trust him with her very life. "We shall have to wait for our Paradise, Jean Marie, but it will be more heavenly for the waiting, my darling, and you will help me, won't you, Jean Marie, for he knew that without her help he was setting himself an impossible task-man is only as strong as his will-and he was afraid to attempt the battle without enlisting her aid, but with her to help him fight life's queer battles no task would be quite impossible. 

Thus began their strange honeymoon. Arriving in 

New York Howard establishes Jean Marie in a picturesque country bungalow with a reliable old couple in the guise of caretakers, and the battle for his freedom began so that he could make Jean Marie his legal wife and right the wrong before the Padre came back. 

The Countess had heard his plea for freedom with beating heart and tightly drawn lips. She had heard him say, "Olga, I love her, love her with every ounce of my life's blood, I must be free. If you had ever loved any one that way you could understand.”

But Olga did love, she loved him, loved him more

than she had ever thought it possible to love any man, loved him with a love great enough to give him his freedom so that he could be happy. She had told him she would give him his freedom, but she had not told him how desperately she loved him, she had not lost all her pride, she was still a Queen-Queen of the Social Realm, but every ounce of her dignity was brought to bear that she might not fall on her knees and beg Howard Norton not to leave her to give her still another chance. 

In one thing Howard Norton was mistaken, he should have carried her to see Jean Marie. Olga had taken it for granted that Jean Marie was waiting in Mexico, and when she found out later that she was in New York, it was a blow to her pride as well as her heart. She would show Howard Norton how easy it was to cast her aside-even bringing the little 'Mexican savage, as Margaret Norton characterized Jean Marie, to New York-living with her under her very nose while he was pleading for a divorce. 

It had been Margaret who so enlightened Olga and it was she who instigated a diabolical plot to break up the romance that meant her son's very existence. 

Howard had Pedro to thank for Mrs. Norton's part in the complicated triangle. Pedro had followed Jean

Marie and Howard to the City and had learned the truth about Howard's double life. He reports to Howard's Mother, who pays him well for the information. 

Mrs. Norton in turn reports fully to Olga and suggests that she assert her rights. It was humiliating to them both, they agreed, to have Howard openly living with the brazen creature while he prated of her innocence she was just a clever little adventuress, and they intended to outwit her if possible. 

Pedro had been seen by Jean Marie hiding in the grounds. She was nervous, unstrung, she fingered her rosary-she felt like a helpless animal driven to bay. 

She was a woman, honorably married to the man of her choice, entitled to the rights and privileges of a wife, and here she was literally hidden from the world. 

He was using her love for him to further his own schemes, she was due an explanation for his mysterious conduct surely he couldn't longer refuse to take her into his confidence. There seemed no explanation of anything, and now here was this unprincipled Mexican who had followed her her very life was in jeopardy and she had no idea where to look for her husband. She wanted to rouse the caretaker but she hesitated for fear of further explanations. 

At last Howard arrived. When Jean Marie saw

him she forgot everything except that she loved him. 

She clung to him in an agony of love and despair. She wouldn't let him leave-she didn't understand, didn't care-nothing mattered except that she loved him. 

Wild words, but the girl was desperate-she had gone thru with so many long days and nights of waitingwaiting-waiting for she knew not what. "Of course, Dear, I shall stay with you. Now run along to bed", Howard suggested, "and I shall remain here like a good sentinel, shan't close my eyes, and when morning comes this Mexican spy shall answer to me." "You-you dare tell me to run along, just as if I were a little girl, I'm tired of it I tell you, I won't stand it another minute, I am your wife-and youyou don't-even-want me", the girl finished with downcast eyes. Where was her pride, the puritanical teachings of the Padre-her sacred modesty? All-all had been laid on the altar of her love, but maybe she was making a mistake, maybe she had better go back to the valley, to the little church-home. She told 

Howard as much, but he was on his knees, he was pleading with her, "Jean Marie", he was saying, “my darling, you are right-there has been a mistake, I shall tell you everything in a few days-why, my darl-

ing, the Padre will be coming back, you will wait until he comes so that I may explain to him first. Please, Dear, I love you so," and she kissed him again, and told him nothing in all the world mattered except that he love her always-always. "Nothing matters except love" Howard repeated, "Nothing" the girl echoed.

Far into the night Howard held Jean Marie in his arms he forgot everything but her, his duty to his love the Padre-even his God-her clinging arms and soft lips had robbed him of his senses.

CHAPTER XII. 

THE TRANSGRESSOR'S DEVIOUS TRAIL 

The morning light sends its first rays across the living room of the bungalow. The man bows his head in an agony of regret. He cursed his cowardice for not telling the whole truth. 

In her bedroom Jean Marie sleeps like a child. 

That night as Howard Norton fought his battle and lost to the Conqueror, Love, Olga, his wife, was giving a gay party. There was wine, rare and old, many songs, laughter and music, extending far into the morning hours. Margaret Norton had suggested as a culmination of the festivities that they visit the country bungalow, thinking it a clever plan to humilate Jean Marie, establish Howard's guilt and thereby balk his plan for divorce. 

Mrs. Norton arrives at the bungalow shortly before the Countess and makes her way, unobserved, to a side window, which happens to be the window of Jean Marie's room. The woman looks in upon the sleeping beauty. The first ray of dawn caresses the girl's angelic countenance. The woman holds her breath in

awe. She does not need to be told that Jean Marie is guilty of no wrong. Whatever the circumstances, the fault lay elsewhere, no sin attached itself to her. 

In the living room Howard raises his haggard face to the morning light-he tries to smoke, read, anything to soothe his troubled conscience. She was sleeping, she believed she was his wife. He had betrayed the 

Padre's confidence, he had compromised her-his honor-all of it-to his own selfish desires. It had been heavenly, holding her in his arms to the battle cry "nothing in all the world matters except love", and now the aftermath. He could pay without protest, it had been worth it all, but what of her? If only the

Padre had not been called away, but why did he need the Padre, why had he not been man enough to rule his own destiny-with Paradise just ahead, he should have waited-why sacrifice her?

To all this reasoning there seemed no aswer. He was turning the leaves of a book-seemingly hunting an answer to his problem, feverishly, with staring eyes and trembling lips he turns page after page. At lastsomething has arrested his attention—the title of 

Oscar Wilde's weird poem "MAN KILLS THE THING 

HE LOVES". He had not killed, in reality, the thing

he loved, but to her with all her spiritual theories of life and love, it would be worse than death. 

Olga Norton and her party arrive at the entrance of the spacious grounds. As they leave their cars and prepare to walk up the long drive, Howard, still wrestling with his problem, sees them, and the full realization of what it will mean to Jean Marie comes to him. 

Pedro, slinking in the background, wears a leering smile, and Howard knows whom he has to thank for the present state of affairs. 

He clinched his hands, he would wring the fellow's neck-it was enough that he had dared raise his eyes to Jean Marie, but to bring this tragedy upon her on top of the indignity he himself had heaped upon her defenseless head, but Pedro's punishment could wait, what was to be done now? 

Howard mumbles feverishly, "Man kills the thing he loves". She, with all the saintly ideas instilled into her by the Padre, and the inborn innocence of her, she would never understand, never forgive him-she does not believe in divorce, and even his legal freedom would not breach the gulf stretching between them. "Death to her was preferable to dishonor", the words of the Padre come to him with an awful significance. "Man kills the thing he loves". Thru the stillness of

the night something had seemed to whisper that such happiness could not endure, that the ethereal glory of it did not belong beyond the confines of heaven, when she knew she would turn from him, disgust, loathing, would supplant the lovelight in her eyes. "Death to her was preferable to dishonor", the words persisted as the boisterous crowd make their way up the long drive. The man takes his little automatic from the drawer, the one he had given Jean Marie for her protection. Well, he was using it after all for her protection. "Man kills the thing he loves".

He was no longer a man, just a weak desperate individual hounded beyond endurance. He had sinned against the one precious thing life held for him, and he-they-must pay the price-the eternal price of perfection, for that perfect state was not an earthly quantity and he who makes the mistake of thinking so must pay. Jean Marie would be at peace with God and he, whatever came to him in the hereafter he deserved.

Howard stands by Jean Marie's bedside. She opens her eyes. Mrs. Norton looks thru the window. She smiles, she realizes now, as Howard's eyes rest on

Jean Marie, why the social world with its hollow glamor meant nothing to the boy, she realizes also that

the combined efforts of the two in the world of art would reveal its hidden possibilities. She regrets her interference with her son's life and resolves that she in the future would let him live his own life in his own way.

The boisterous crowd was at the door now clamoring for admittance. A male voice was heard singing "A love nest for two". Jean Marie looks to Howard for explanation, he speaks, "Jean Marie, for all things there is a reason. If you do not understand, my darling, you will know this: I love you-I love you, and it is the only thing I could do to atone for a great wrong."

There is no mistaking his meaning, the look in his eyes, but she knows he loves her, and even the sight of the automatic in his hand does not spur her to any plea for mercy, but in God's kingdom there is no room for a murderer, this she knew, and she would make a supreme effort to save her love from a murderer's fate.

As the automatic creeps up and into the lacy folds of her negligee, the girl raises a majestic hand and directs the man's attention to a picture of the Saviour with his cross and crown of thorns. 

Outside the window Mrs. Norton clutches her agonized heart in frozen horror. "Merciful God," she whispers, "he is murdering the ministering angel."

She wants to cry out, but her tongue is paralyzed with horror she wants to go for aid, stay his hand, but she is rooted to the spot, her limbs refuse to move. In the brief interval that followed the woman strains her ear and catches the sound "I shall go with you, dear," and suffers the tortures of the damned, for she knows that her son's act of madness is directly attributable to her own actions-that she, not he, will be guilty of this double tragedy. Her social career had not been worth the price.

Howard gazes in fascination at the picture of Jesus. "Man Kills the Thing he Loves". Man? What manner of man, Howard asks himself, a madman-perhaps a fool, but a man, a real redblooded man-fights for the woman of his heart, he shields her even with his life. 

If Jesus bore such a cross, why should he flinch at what lay before him. If Jean Marie turned from him today-if she denounced his shameful weakness, his cowardice, tomorow he would move the universe to restore himself to her favor. In an agony of thankfulness, he loosens his hold on the automatic, which slides to the floor, and he kneels at the feet of the woman he loves more than his life-more than the world. 

Howard Norton's Mother, outside the window,

kneels also, and pours out her soul in thanksgiving to the God whom she had forgotten.

The loud knocking at the door calls the man back to his senses and to the task before him. The girl strokes his hair as he kneels at her feet. She wanted so much to help him, console him in some way, as she seemed to realize that it was she who had brought him to his present desperate state, but she would ask no question, for she was sure that would only make it harder for him. 

One beseeching look, a frenzied kiss, and he was gone, locking the door behind him. He doesn't know just how but somehow, someway he must protect herthat pack of scandalmongers must not see her-caveman tactics might be necessary, but protect her he must.

Howard opens the outer door and demands an explanation for the intrusion. Olga cowers for a moment before the fire in his eyes, then remembering what she termed Howard's "brazen duplicity", she raises her head defiantly and with a sarcastic laugh brushes past him and bids her friends enter, but Howard bars the way. She shouts in tones that extend to Jean 

Marie's ears, "I am your wife, how dare you forbid me entrance?"

Jean Marie's despair is pitiful to see, as she waits for what seemed an eternity for Howard's answer, which finally comes, slowly, deliberately, "You, Madame, may enter, but your friends I do not recognize". "His wife," Jean Marie repeats, the tragedy of a life time mirrored in her eyes. "If she is his wife, who am I" she asks herself despairingly. 

Howard had admonished Jean Marie to wait there in her room until he returned, but for once his wishes were disregarded. She slips into her coat, she tries the door-locked! Well, there is a window, she would make her exit that way, but get outside she must, she needed air, she felt her knees tremble beneath her frail weight. Outside the window she faces the half drunken crowd, who fall back, awe stricken, ashamed, in the face of the girl's innocence and despair. To 

Olga alone she speaks, "You are his wife?" The woman nods assent, even she is ashamed, it was hardly the hour of triumph she had planned.

As Jean Marie's world crumples at her feet, she raises her eyes to Howard. His soul is crushed, but he cannot deny it.

Jean Marie rushes out and on-anywhere to get away. The crowd falls back and make their way out. 

Before the picture of our Saviour, the picture that

had stayed his desperate hand but a few moments earlier, Howard Norton raised his haggard eyes as if seeking a solution to the awful problem facing him. 

Jean Marie had been publicly disgraced, branded like any common woman of the streets and had gone forth into the night, God only knew where, and he he was powerless. His love-the best of which he was capable, had done that to her, and he would have died to have saved her, he would have killed to have kept her love, beautiful, trusting, he could see the tortured eyes as she looked to him for explanation. Again that desperate, trapped feeling crept over his being, again the urge to kill. There lay the little automatic beckoning. It would only serve to further complicate the unraveling of the tangled threads, he whispered to his tortured heart. I must pull myself together, be a man, he muttered shakily, but the ambition to be a man was not strong enough to offset the odds against him-no man could do the things he had done. He wanted desperately to end it all, it would be easy. 

From the doorway Olga and Mrs. Norton had been watching, each realizing their responsibility for Howard Norton's present state. They saw him reach for the automatic, saw him raise it to his heart, heard

him whisper, with his eyes on the likeness of our Saviour "Forgive" and then Olga screamed "Wait".

A little later Olga was kneeling by Howard's side, telling him a story that meant life itself to Howard and Jean Marie. I loved you so, she murmured, with tears glistening in her eyes, I played a desperate, despicable game, and it is because I love you so I am going to right the wrong I have done, and she read to him, in a tear choked voice, a letter from a Detective

Agency telling her that they had at last succeeded in locating her husband who had been given up for dead. 

He had been injured in an airplane accident, and was in a French Institute for Incurables, where he apparently was attempting to hide himself from the world in which he had formerly enjoyed unlimited prestige. "So you see, Howard," she is your wife you can go to her, paint her picture, be happy". "And you”, Howard, inquired, " you will forget me and be happy?" "I shall never forget, my dear, so long as life shall endure I shall remember. It is that memory that will take me out of the world that gave me so much and yet cheated me of the only treasure I fought to retain -love-that will take me back to France to reclaim my husband. I know him well enough to know that he hid himself away in order to avoid handicapping me

in my mad social career. "Forget you, Howard Norton?-not while life shall endure, for you have taught me that there is something to live for besides oneself". 

The woman's beautiful face assumed an angelic expression, as she lifted her eyes to the picture of our 

Saviour. 

She was heaping coals of veritable fire on the head of the boy. "Olga, is there anything I can do to help?" "I would give my life to atone, it has been a terrible mix-up all the way round", Howard asked in a choked voice. "Yes," Olga answered, "I think I shall endow a

Home for Incurables, I shall find work to do, and I want you to come and see if I do it well, and I may be able to tell you then that I have succeeded." So- 

Howard Norton felt a long lingering kiss on his lips and the Countess had passed from his life. 

Because of the Countess' living husband, Jean Marie is Howard's wife in the sight of God and man. Howard races in quest of Jean Marie, but she is nowhere in sight "gone", Howard cries frantically.

CHAPTER XIII. 

A HAVEN FOR THE WANDERER 

After Jean Marie had heard the Countess say that she was Howard's wife, and he had refused to deny it, she had stumbled blindly away from the scene of her degradation, not realizing, or caring, where only to put as much distance between herself and the humiliating scene as possible was the dominating thought that propelled her weary limbs in the coveted direction when physical rebellion threatened collapse. 

The girl shivered in spite of the luxurious seal coat that covered her negligee. The coat had been a Christmas present from Howard. It was the first fur coat she had ever owned and she had been inordinately proud of it. That she had it was a stroke of Fate because when she had slipped into the coat, it had been the first thing that came within her reach.

Altho it was April the early morning breeze was cold, in addition to which a fine mist began to fall. 

While the girl shivered, and was plainly on the verge of collapse, she evidently felt nothing, for she stumbled blindly along the road, her throat bared to the wind,

only one button of her coat fastened. 

The frail little mules on her feet were wholly inadequate for walking, they careened dizzily from side to side, occasionally one of them came off, once she fell to her knees.

After that she took off the slippers and walked barefoot.

Finally, after following many unfrequented trails, she came to a suburban residence park. It was evidently a new subdivision for only three houses were visible.

The street on which the girl found herself was deserted except for a modest cottage with a fence around it and some old fashioned flowers in the front yard. 

There was a roomy comfortable looking lawn chair in the yard that looked extremely inviting to the weary wanderer, but she passed the gate slowly, wistfully. 

A little further along she dropped down on the curb, exhausted. She massaged her bruised and aching feet. 

The frivilous little pair of pink slippers, with their ruffles of lace, which she had continued to carry in her hand, lay on the grass to mock the misery of the breaking heart.

The mist of rain had ceased and the rose streaked eastern sky gave promise of a clear day. A bird in some foliage nearby began a plaintive little song; a

big gray cat sat on the steps of the cottage washing its face with its paw, the only visible evidence that the world was waking up.

The soft earth, with its covering of fresh green grass lured the girl's senses.

She made a pillow of the big soft collar of her coat, snuggled down within its protective folds, leaned against a big tree that ornamented the subdivision and, from sheer exhaustion, soon lost herself in merciful slumber.

She dreamed she was back home; she saw the Padre, her Father. Tears of relief glistened on her cheek. 

A thin, drabbled gray kitten, which possibly had been foisted on the neighborhood by a passing motorist, who wished to be rid of it, snuggled close to the girl's warm coat and, perhaps, dreamed also of homethat the warm furry coat was its mother. 

Down the street came a milkman, in a small white truck, making his morning rounds. He was young and athletic in appearance, as he had a right to be, for he was in reality one of the many students who worked during spare hours to pay their tuition thru the local institutions of higher learning. 

He spied the girl by the curb. "Huh", he ejaculated, "another dame went joy riding without her skates", and started to drive on, but his attention was arrested

by the pathetic droop of the young shoulders; the collar of the fur coat had dropped away from the girl's face; her beauty was startling. 

"Another version of Beauty and the Beast I guess,' he muttered, and drawn by an irresistible force, the boy descended to investigate at close range. 

The girl was breathing easily, no evidence of an accident or foul play. He prepared to move on, "no affair of mine", he said, but he was fascinated by the appearance of the sleeping girl, her beauty and apparent helplessness. One pink toe protruded from the shelter of the massive coat, the nail torn, blood visible, in addition to which a teardrop glistened on the soft white cheek. "Some boy's sister I guess", he addressed himself, "and, if she were my sister, I'd like to lay my hands on the man", for, as is the world's way, the miseries of womankind are invariably construed to the iniquities of man. 

The boy seated himself on the curb. He'd wait a little while anyway, he decided, maybe she'd wake up. 

He continued to wait—the girl continued to sleep. "Well, I'm almost to the end of my route" he mused -"the dames who usually make such a fuss if I am a few minutes late, have been served." 

So he continued to wait, smoking many cigarettes

and cursing the confounded luck for being chickenhearted enough to want to get mixed up in other peoples' affairs. The girl, for all her apparent innocence and beauty, might be a gangster's moll (appearances were often deceiving these days) and he might get shot for his pains, but somehow he couldn't leave the spot until he had spoken to the girl.

As the sun continued to mount in the eastern sky, the boy decided he had better awaken the girl. He was already late for classes, and had personally declared a holiday, but if he were too late reporting from his route, it might not work so well, and he needed the job. 

He started to whistle "Good Morning to You", a childish little song he used to sing with his class every morning in elementary school. At first he whistled softly, then a little louder and a little louder until he had reached a threatening crescendo, but the girl did not stir.

A woman came to the window of the cottage in her nightgown. "Such carryings on as the young folks do have these days," she muttered, "even the milkman goes on a petting party before breakfast. Looks like the girl's drunk." The woman closed the window with a bang. "She might call the police", the boy decided. He

was forced to admit that things did look suspicious, and he didn't appreciate the idea of being haled into Court when he was guilty of nothing more than a human attempt to succor a soul in distress. "Good morning", he spoke in a sharp tone, "any milk today?" This latter with a twinkle in his eye.

As the girl opened her eyes, she was startled by the young man in white suit and cap bending over her. 

Her head was hot, her joints were so sore she cried in pain when she attempted to move.

She decided she must be in a hospital-may be there had been an accident, and the young man in white was probably an intern, but he was smiling, she couldn't be hurt badly.

As the boy reassured her, she sat up and pulled her coat closer to hide the lacy negligee. "You see", the boy continued, "I am near the end of my route, and, as this is such a deserted neighborhood, it occurred to me that I might give you a lift on your way home.” "Home", the girl echoed, tears springing to her eyes, I-you-no-" she faltered, "thank you just the same."

Sensing that he was on the wrong trail, he attempted another suggestion. "Then, let me take you to the

Y.W.C.A. or some protective agency until you get in

touch with relatives," but for all his well directed importunity, the girl remained adamant.

There was really nothing he could do, she insisted, thinking of her drabbled and torn negligee, her feet bare except for a pair of pink boudoir slippers. How could she face any one? It was better to just die and have it over with, "but one didn't die," she thought bitterly, "when it was convenient to do so." "But, see here," the boy assured her, "you can't remain here, that woman who has been watching from the window is suspicious, she may call the police", whereupon a look of terror came into the lovely eyes, she clasped her hands in abject fear. "I have it", the boy cried at last, "my Mother, she's the most understanding person in the world, let me take you to her, she will help you decide what is to be done. "Mom, does a lot of missionary work anyway, it's her pet hobby", the boy continued enthusiastically. "Would you", the girl asked gratefully, and added "all appearances to the contrary, will you please try to believe that my present predicament was occasioned thru no offense of mine, and" she added, her voice trailing to a whisper, "I shall find a way to repay you." "I'm sure you've done no wrong," the young man

answered. "As for repaying me, your very evident gratitude has already done that." "And now", he continued, let's be on our way. You can ride with me to the end of my route and then we'll decide what to do."

As the girl rose from the ground, the kitten stretched itself also with a meow of protest at being disturbed. 

Neither had discovered the cat before. It looked so hungry and forlorn the boy produced a bottle of cream from the truck, removed the lid, tilted the bottle so that the cat's tongue could reach the cream and, together, they watched in amusement as the almost famished creature lapped the refreshing liquid. 

As the cream disappeared slowly because of its inaccessibility, the boy suggested that they take the cat along and feed it at their leisure. 

The girl was afraid the boy's mother might object, and voiced her opinions, but that the idea met with her approval was evinced by the tired little smile that illumined her countenance. She picked up the homeless creature, held it close to her, as she whispered "two of a kind-wanderers on the face of the earth." 

And so the trek began, the lovely girl with the touseled curls and sealskin coat, hugging to her a thin bedraggled gray kitten, and the neat white uniformed young man at the wheel of the little white dairy truck.

They talked about the cat and other trivialities, he being careful to keep the conversation from veering into a personal channel, as she was so obviously distressed at any mention of things that might involve any accounting of her strange predicament.

When the last bottle of milk had been delivered, they planned the trip home. He would be forced to leave the truck at the plant, he explained. His home was on the other side of town, he rode the subway. 

She could, the girl decided, drape her negligee about her waist and hide it with the coat, she could even go without a hat, but she could not, she acknowledged in despair, ride the subway in a pair of pink lacy boudoir slippers. "What a nuisance to you", she said at last, "may be you had better go ahead, I'll manage some way", she finished miserably, but he had other plans. "There's an old colored woman, one of my customers, who lives on a back street. Aunt Phoebe is an old 

Southern negro and knows her place. Suppose you stay there with her while I go for Mom and a pair of shoes. That would be better than getting a taxi. I live in such a nosey neighborhood, it might be embarrassing to you anyway to go home with me. He had visions of many necks craned to get a glimpse of the lovely girl in the sealskin coat, and many nods of ap-

prehension as the neighbor women drew their own narrow conclusions. "You are so kind", the girl answered, in a voice choked with emotion, "I'll repay you some way". "Forget it, Sister, forget it", the boy answered, reverting to common vernacular in an effort to cover his confusion and embarrassment, as the girl, prompted by sincere gratitude, laid her little hand on his arm and looked into his eyes with an expression of candor and confidence.

It occurred to him that she was not only beautiful, but was in a class entirely to herself-must have dropped from another planet-certainly he had never seen a girl quite like her before. 

Then he addressed himself seriously "better slow up, old man, she's wearing a sealskin and a diamond that would choke a calf-and-you're only the milkman”, he finished with a contemptuous smile.

He did not remind himself that the most formidable consideration was the platinum band of orange blossoms, slightly overshadowed by the huge solitaire, that circled her third finger, but he was fully cognizant of its modest existence. 

Aunt Phoebe received the girl with a beaming countenance and no questions asked. She found a crisp bill in her hand while the young man whispered

"make her comfortable, Aunt Phoebe, and give her some breakfast and I'll give you another bill like that when I come back". "Sho will, Honey," she replied, and went about her duties, as the young man tucked one pink slipper in his pocket and remarked with a flourish, "I'm off with 

Cinderella's slipper".

While it was an effort, the girl smiled bravely and answered with mock seriousness, "aye, your Highness, and may the luck of the gods attend you".

Jean Marie would not allow Aunt Phoebe to prepare breakfast, only a cup of tea she insisted, which she drank piping hot, after which she curled up on the couch in the spotless sitting room and, in spite of Aunt 

Phoebe's well-directed efforts, cried herself to sleep. "Po lil lamb" Aunt Phoebe whispered, as she rearranged the pillows and wondered how that sweet child got in such a scrape. "Man at the bottom uv it, I guess", she wisely soliloquized.

It was long past the luncheon hour before Aunt 

Phoebe awakened Jean Marie. She hesitated to disturb her, but vowed if the young man came back and she hadn't "fed this chile, he'd be madder'n a hornet." 

Aunt Phoebe brought the tea tray to the sitting room. There was home baked ham, a tasty green salad and dainty tea biscuits.

Altho the girl made a valiant effort she was unable to eat. She did manage to eat a small portion of the salad and drink the tea, sinking back into the cushions with a profound sigh. How fortunate that she had fallen into friendly hands, not a cent of money, only the clothes on her back. She dared not communicate with her father. As long as he did not hear from her he could not be certain of her fate; when he knew, he would find Howard Norton-and-", she shuddered and burst into tears again, while Aunt Phoebe trotted about so obviously distressed that she choked back the tears, patted Aunt Phoebe's hand and promised not to cry any more.

The old colored woman, in an effort to distract the girl's thoughts, called her attention to the kitten sleeping on a cushion in the chair. She had given it a bath while Jean Marie had been sleeping, and had tied a pink ribbon on its neck. It no longer looked like a cat that had come from the gutter.

Jean Marie was pleased and stroked the kitten's fur with an air of proud possession while she praised 

Aunt Phoebe's efforts. 

Aunt Phoebe brought the afternoon paper to Jean

Marie and with it a bunch of little spring wildflowers. 

The girl buried her face in them and irrigated the bright blossoms with her tears; they reminded her of

her strolls with Howard Norton among the wildflowers of Mexico.

A little later Jean Marie's friend of the morning arrived with a gentle, sweet-faced woman of middle age, whom he introduced as "Mom", Mrs. Mary Blake.

Then he laughingly suggested that Mom introduce him, as he had neglected to attend to the little detail of an introduction by name. He had been wondering about the girl's name-surely it would have to be a beautiful one. "Harvey", his mother answered with a smile, “don't be absurd". 

Jean Marie said simply, "my name is Jean", and held out a little hand that trembled slightly, as she looked wistfully at Mrs. Blake, a little uncertain as to the reception she might expect.

With maternal instinct the woman drew the girl into the shelter of her arms and kissed her gently on the cheek as she said, “I am happy to be of any possible assistance to you, my Dear, and I want you to feel that you are among friends." "Thank you, Mrs. Blake", the girl answered in a tearchoked voice, it was wonderful of you to come." "And Mom", Harvey interrupted the earnest dialogue, "how do you like this rare feline specimen"? "Jumping jelly fish", the boy exclaimed as he dis-

covered the kitten's clean, fluffy coat and pink ribbon, "how come?" "Aunt Phoebe did it", the girl replied, "isn't she efficient tho, doesn't look like a tramp now, does he"? "All he needs now, Harvey continued, "is a pair of boots, to join the celebrities of feline history. He really should have a name tho," and, because the day was Friday and one that neither of them would ever forget, the kitten, like Robinson Crusoe's man, was duly christened "Friday".

The time was slipping away, so Mrs. Blake produced a pair of black patent sandals, saying in a businesslike tone, "if they don't fit, my dear, we can exchange them tomorrow."

As Jean Marie slipped her dainty feet into them, Mrs. Blake continued, "I wanted to get a more conservative model for street wear, but Harvey was sure these were more suitable for you" and, she said with a twinkle in her eye as she surveyed the girl's foot, "I am forced to admit that he does know something of feminine raiment after all". "They are lovely, Mrs. Blake," the girl answered, "just what I probably would have selected myself; both of you have excellent taste. How can I ever repay such kindness?" 

As the girl's eyes threatend to fill with tears, Mrs.

Blake bustled about arranging for the trip home. 

After the goodbyes had been said and Aunt Phoebe's hand closed on the promised bill, Harvey deposited "Friday" in Mrs. Blake's arms. His mother smiled indulgently, as she said "this time it is a cat Harvey brings to me. Once he brought me a pup with the mange, then a pigeon with a broken leg." "And now a girl with a broken heart", Jean Marie said in tones meant only for Mrs. Blake's ears. "My Dear", the woman replied, "it has been said that the heart of youth is fragile but it mends easily" and she patted the girl's hand in maternal fashion. 

It was decided that for the present Jean Marie should remain with the Blake family, which consisted of Harvey and his mother, a widow. To avoid any embarrassment to Jean, Mrs. Blake had introduced her as her niece and as such she was accepted. 

Jean Marie had told them to call her Jean, neglecting to supply another name. When another name was needed Mary Blake very tactfully supplied her own, and in time Jean Marie answered readily to the name of Jean Blake.

Jean had told Mrs. Blake that she was married, that she loved her husband with all her heart, but circumstances, which she could not explain separated them. 

Mrs. Blake accepted Jean as she was and asked no

questions, being convinced in her own mind that Jean was innocent of any wrong doing. And so the days sped on, filled so full for Jean, she prayed that time and work would bring forgetfulness.

She had insisted on sharing her portion of the household expenses, as well as the work about the modest apartment. She had sold her seal coat, being careful to remove the label so that she might not be traced. 

She had disposed also of the diamond. 

These things were gifts from the man she loved with every fibre of her heart, regardless of the thing he had done to her, but she parted with them without a pangshe still had her wedding ring-that would always be hers, for she knew that in the sight of God it was right for her to keep it.

The coat and ring, she reasoned, really did not belong to her, they were too valuable, and she thought of the proceeds from their sale in terms of a loan. 

Some day she would reimburse Howard Norton for the amount they had cost him.

In the meantime, she gave the money to Mrs. Blake, who protested vigorously, saying "Jean, the added expense is almost nil and you more than pay your way, you do so many things for me," but the girl replied, "please, Aunt Mary, you don't know what your kindness has done for me and, unless you take the money,

I cannot stay." "All right, my dear, we shall not argue. I'll start a savings account for you with a portion of it." So that was all settled.

Mrs. Blake took a few dollars each week as Jean's contribution to household expenses and the remainder she deposited in the bank to Jean's credit, and the bank account continued to grow for Jean's hands were never idle. 

She did gorgeous hand work, having learned the art under the most skillful workers of Mexico. 

While the girl shrank from personal contact, she and

Mrs. Blake did explore the shops in search of new designs and rare materials. 

Thru the merchants from whom she purchased materials, Jean had gotten commissions from several wealthy women of the city, who appreciated the rare value of Jean's handiwork and were willing to pay generously for it.

She was making considerable money, and could have made much more, but Mary Blake, acting in the capacity of a self appointed business manager for Jean, had refused several orders, fearing for the girl's health.

While Jean was the essence of amiability and apparently worked with feverish abandon, yet the keen

feminine eyes of Mary Blake traced the tears of a breaking heart. Once she had said, "Jean, can't you tell me, Dear, there may be something I can do", but the girl evaded the issue and smiled bravely. 

Another thing to interrupt the smooth running machinery of the Blake household was Mary Blake's recent anxiety also for her son's welfare. 

In their mutual interest in the Blake household Jean and Harvey were brought into close contact. Sometimes they planned happy surprises for Mrs. Blake.

Occasionally they pooled their earnings to purchase some coveted article for the comfort and convenience of the home.

At first there was nothing in their relationship to disturb the equanimity of the mother. 

On Jean's part their relationship was plainly one of platonic beauty, the natural outgrowth of constant association, a beautiful friendship, founded on admiration and gratitude.

But on Harvey's part of late there seemed sometimes to be a strained attitude, he was either punctilious to an obvious degree or gruff, taciturn, unapproachable. 

Either Jean did not notice, or refused to associate Harvey's periodical changes in character with herself, but the mother realized it was time for action. Harvey was unhappy.

It had all been a mistake taking a girl like Jean into her home, she could have watched over her and befriended her in other ways, but to associate her constantly with her son, under the same roof, was indeed a mistake; the heart of youth is too vulnerable.

One day, as the boy's eyes followed Jean about the apartment, Mrs. Blake spoke in an undertone, "Harvey my son, when you were a very little boy, you often times fretted for things beyond your reach. You're a big boy now, Dear, but I am afraid you haven't yet learned that some things are still forbidden." 

The boy waited until Jean was beyond earshot and replied, "what's the idea, Mom?" But the color mounted to his cheeks, he knew she was referring to Jean. "Jean", the woman answered, "you must not forget that she is bound by the laws of God and man." "Oh, Mom, forget it" the boy answered. "Jean's a trump, but you know I never cared for girls". 

He smiled bravely, but it was a puny effort, his lips were drawn too tight, his nails cut into the palms of his hands. 

But Mary Blake did not forget, she must think of some way, the two must be separated. Time would, perhaps, be merciful and teach Harvey the wisdom of avoiding forbidden paths.

Then Fate took a hand and decided things for Mary Blake.

The Villa de Matel, a magnificient training school for nuns, which had been endowed by some of the city's civic and social leaders, was nearing completion. The buildings were quite imposing and occupied a tract of land near the city, which had been beautifully landscaped. Those responsible for its erection might well be proud of the effort.

An artist of repute, Peter O'Day had been commissioned to paint a life size picture of a nun to decorate the massive hall and the artist was now engaged in looking for a suitable model. 

On a certain Sunday morning, while Mrs. Blake and 

Jean were preparing to attend the little church around the corner, as was their usual custom, in another part of the city a dialogue was taking place between an artist and his friend that was to have a tremendous affect on Jean Marie Nunzio's life and was to decide a very perplexing problem for Mary Blake. 

The scene is Peter O'Day's Art Studio. Peter's friend was saying, "you might have a look at her anyway, Pete, even if you couldn't, or wouldn't, paint her picture, you will thank your stars always for permitting you to feast eyes on the ethereal beauty of the gods" he finished with a melodramatic flourish.

"Gee, how you do rave", the artist answered, "another affair of the heart-eh?" "Wrong again", the man answered, "I don't even know her myself-besides, she's not my type-I want one of the hotcha variety to smoke a cigarette with papa and smack her lips over my favorite vintage. 

You want a saint to put on canvas, Pete, I'm trying to help you, old man, take it or leave it," he added, "but be assured I have no personal interest in the matter aside from the satisfaction of helping a fine artist and a good fellow." 

O'Day replied "thanks, old man, I'm sure your motive is above reproach, and I appreciate the interest. 

May be we'd better have a look at this rare specimen of womanhood." "I dare say, however," the artist continued, "that while she appears so saintly in a church pew on Sunday, that on Monday we might run across her in a "speak", the type of a girl you like, for the histrionic talents of some of the moderns are to be considered." "May be so" the man replied, "I'm not going to be responsible for any girl's morals, as feminine standards seem to be keeping pace with the changing times, but really, Pete, as an artist you should see her." "My Dear", Mary Blake was saying to Jean on this particular morning, you look lovely, like a rare picture

ready for its frame. May be your expression, may be that charming new frock, with its puritanical style, but whatever it is makes me wish I were an artist." 

Jean acknowledged the compliment with her usual modesty.

It had not been altogether coincidence that had prompted Mary Blake's words at a time when one of the country's foremost artists was waiting to get a glimpse of Jean for the purpose of possibly transferring her beauty to canvas, for many times Mary Blake had looked at Jean and, with the idea in mind that beauty has the inconsistency to fade, and with an unselfish concern for posterity, deplored the fact that the artistic world, and the general public, were being cheated each day that Jean's rare beauty illuminated the humble little apartment of the Blake's. It further occurred to her that Harvey would have been less than human not to love this incomparable girl. She had been blind not to have thought of it before it was too late. "We'll park the big boat", Peter O'Day was saying to his friend in reference to his luxurious limousine, "and rent a flivver that won't attract attention", but the imposing figure of Peter O'Day commanded attention regardless of his mode of transportation. 

As he and his friend, a little later, stepped to the

curb from the modest little car that O'Day, himself, was driving, all eyes were turned in their direction. 

A few recognized the artist. 

He returned their salutations in his usual dignified manner, but was plainly annoyed. No mission was too delicate that the world did not clamor to share, if it concerned Fame's favorites, and Peter O'Day was certainly one of Fame's favored few. 

As the artist stood by the curb lighting a cigarette, his friend nudged him. O'Day turned, slightly bored, then he stared rudely.

Under the intense scrutiny of his gaze, Jean dropped her eyes, color suffusing her cheeks.

Mary Blake was used to seeing people stare at Jean, but O'Day's manner was too pronounced. She drew herself up to her full height, as she slipped a protecting arm about the girl. 

The artist, pale and shaking, whispered to his friend, "God, old man, my picture in the flesh."

Inside the church, from a commanding vantage point, the artist watched in fascination the play of the girl's mobile countenance, and whispered to himself, "that for which I comb the globe, I find here at home -in this humble little church".

After that there was no stopping O'Day. It took quite a bit of diplomatic maneuvering, but a week later

Jean Marie was posing in the flowing robes of a nun within the Studio of Peter O'Day, one of the world's most celebrated artists.

Jean could hardly believe it herself, but Mrs. Blake's influence had been the deciding factor. Mrs. Blake had explained to Jean Marie that it was a wonderful opportunity, so much needle work was not good for her eyes, and she had further insisted that Jean should avail herself of the luxurious suite at the hotel which O'Day included in the contract while the picture was under way.

A companion was also furnished, a genteel settled lady, whom Mary Blake, herself, had selected, this being one of the stipulations Mrs. Blake had insisted upon before she would consent to Jean posing. "Such elaborate precautions may not be necessary," she told herself, "but with a girl like Jean one could not be too careful, especially with men of the world like 

Peter O'Day." "Always thinking of the girl", Mary Blake thought sadly, "but what of the man"? She had been thinking of Jean's welfare when she took her into her home for her son to break his heart over when the girl could give nothing in return. "Yes, men had hearts also, and it might not come amiss to whisper to Peter O'Day with all his worldliness that Jean was not free."

When the contract between Jean and the artist had been signed, it settled Mary Blake's problem for the time being. When the picture was finished and Jean insisted on coming home, which Mary Blake was convinced she would do, there would be time to decide then what to do to forestall such a move. 

When Mary Blake returned home the first day after seeing Jean Marie and Friday, her feline favorite, duly established in her rich hotel suite, her heart ached, not alone because Jean was passing from her life, but when she beheld the tight lips and drawn face of Harvey, her only child, she censured herself cruelly. An expression of hallowed resignation thrilled the artist's soul. The nun had renounced all things worldly for that which was holy and righteous. The artist painted as one inspired. 

Came a day when Peter O'Day's picture was finished and hung on the rich walls of the Villa de Matel; the

Margaret Norton had contributed generously to the endowment of the school. Since that morning, months ago, when she had humiliated her son, broken his heart, and sent his wife forth into the streets, alone, disgraced, the woman had done much to atone, but all the money and effort went for naught in her quest for 

Jean Marie. 

Howard had written from Mexico that he was doing God's work among Jean Marie's people. 

In her sorrow and remorse the woman had turned to philanthropy in her effort to make amends. 

She had attended the unveiling of Peter O'Day's picture at the Villa de Matel.

When she looked upon the holy picture, it was not alone the purity and beauty of the work that caused her to gasp for breath. "It is she" the woman breathed with beating heart and moist eyes.

She stood before the picture long after the others had passed on. She continued to look into the angelic face of the girl she had wronged so cruelly. Had she taken the veil? The woman held her hand to her aching heart, she repeated many prayers-then-on

bended knee and in a tear-choked voice she whispered "I must find her-for Howard-and to that task I dedicate the remainder of my life."

CHAPTER XIV. 

AT THE END OF THE TRAIL-PEACE 

After weeks and months of endless search for Jean Marie, which embraced two continents and involved a fortune, Howard goes back to Mexico. Raymond

Prescott, whose wanderings had taken him back to the mountain village, had written of the dangerous conditions existing in the valley. Anti-religious demonstrations continued to menace the safety of the law-abiding and God-fearing element there, as well as in other portions of Mexico. The Padre was in danger, he and his followers, Raymond had written, may be attacked at any minute, but the Padre will not leave his people, most of whom refuse to see the existing danger and cling pathetically to their meager holdings. 

And, that was what carried Howard back to the valley. If he were killed, he reasoned, it would be for a good cause, it would be a sacrificial offering for the sin he had committed, it was the least he could do, give his life, to atone to Jean Marie, for it would be in defense of her home and the people she loved and served; if he died in the line of duty, perhaps God, in his in-

finite mercy and capacity for understanding the vulnerability of the clay which he fashioned into human beings, might give to him a place by Jean Marie in his Kingdom.

As Howard Norton knelt at the feet of the good old 

Padre, he spoke brokenly, tears choking his voice, "Father, I have returned." Raymond Prescott had already told the Padre the tragic details of the love of Jean Marie and the young artist and Howard was spared the agony of repetition. While the Padre's soul was torn in grief, yet looking into the boy's haggard countenance, he finds it in his heart to comfort him. "My son", he speaks softly, "we can only wait and pray." "In the meantime", he continued, "there is a heart breaking emergency confronting us." The Padre cast his eyes heavenward as if seeking a solution to the tangled threads of life's pitiful problems, and continued, "Nunzio and his lawless army are seething with unrest. He is holding you and I responsible for Jean Marie's absence; he insists that we have destroyed her, and I am not sure whether you should remain or not", but the boy pleaded with him, he promised he would find some way to convince Nunzio that he was not an enemy, and it was finally agreed that

he should take Jean Marie's place and together they would labor for the good of humanity. If she is alive, she will come back, Howard reasoned, back to the home where, despite her surroundings, she had been happy in doing God's work until he had come and schooled her in the ways of man and his miseries. Far into the night, with the Padre as his Guiding Star, Howard Norton pondered over the mysterious teachings of the Bible.

Nunzio was more firm in his unbelief than ever. 

He pointed to Howard as an example-he had taken Jean Marie, the only human being in the world Nunzio had ever believed in; he destroys her-then he comes back-alone-and prates of God and his angels. He hates Howard Norton with all his soul.

Howard struggles harder than ever with his proposition and prays for the salvation of Nunzio's soul, believing if he could reclaim Nunzio for Jean Marie, it would in a measure atone for the great wrong he had done her, for Howard knows that to be the supreme blessing Jean Marie craved. If Jean Marie were alive, he would make himself worthy. If not, he would make himself worthy so that when she beckoned to him in the Great Beyond, he could go to her with a clear conscience, and the boy worked on for God.

When the Padre or Howard approached Nunzio, he laughed in contempt-he told them if God was so all powerful why didn't their prayers bring back Jean Marie.

Pedro had wended his way back to the Mexican village. He had cheated at cards and had been discovered by Nunzio, who, at the first opportunity, shot him down in cold blood. Howard alone witnessed the tragedy and hastened to the spot. He dips his finger in 

Pedro's blood, as Nunzio looks contemptously on, not without fear, however, and when Howard smears his finger, dripping wiith Pedro's blood, across Nunzio's throat and says, "you will die for this", Nunzio shrieks "blood". Howard replies, "yes, blood of atonement." "Blood of a damned cheat" Nunzio cries. "Maybe so," Howard replies, "but be it blood of saint or sinner, it is human blood, and it is the fetter that binds you to me. We have much to atone for-you and I," Howard continues, "and this is the beginning, one crooked move and I turn you over to the authorities, you are my prisoner."

Howard instructs him and Nunzio, hating Howard with all his soul but fearing the wrath of justice, obeys in a sullen mechanical way. Howard moves into the Nunzio cottage. At night he chained Nunzio.

"Rather melodramatic" he told his prisoner, "but I do not trust doors and their frail locks." Howard's attempts at conversation met with no response, Nunzio was waiting for an opportunity to dispose of Howard via the same route as he had disposed of Pedro. Howard knew it and yet he did not falter in the strange course he had chosen to pursue. By day Nunzio and 

Howard outwardly were companions, and the boy strives diligently to find a way to the human spark he believes Nunzio possesses, otherwise how could he be the father of his Jean Marie?

People marvel as Nunzio closes his dives. Nunzio's stony countenance does not look very spirituelle, but he is Howard's inseparable companion; the populace merely shake their heads, it is beyond them. 

In the Nunzio cottage Howard reads to the atheist from the Bible. He attempts conversation, dwelling on the things he knows to be of interest to his prisoner, who sits nearby with a sarcastic smile that sickens the soul of the boy.

But those strange days and nights were not without their reward. Howard had painted many pictures, some of which might have graced the walls of a King's Palace. He had painted Nunzio secured by the heavy chain which held him at night. It was a marvel-

the man's expression was a priceless asset to the young artist. This he called "The Galley Slave", and while his model gritted his teeth in rage, it must be said to his credit that the young artist with his nimble fingers and magic brush aroused an occasional glint of admiration in the the eyes of his prisoner, but this he was always particular to conceal, as did he also conceal his enjoyment of the colorful tales of life beyond the valley that Howard Norton continued to pour into his apparently indifferent ears. 

Had Howard Norton been able to glimpse the soul of the man behind his impenetrable mask, he would have experienced a thrill of encouragement; as it was he was sick at heart and discouraged beyond measure. 

He had run the gamut of his wits-he had tried persuasion and force and at last realized the hopelessness of his methods, but the hand of Providence intervened at the most critical moment.

The volcanic disturbance in the mountains assumed alarming proportions. The people talk in whispers, aroused at last to its threatening possibilities. This served to further increase the unrest among the antireligious hoodlums-they seemed to be vieing with the elements in the destruction of the village. Close scientific observation revealed that there was no im-

mediate danger from the volcanic source, but the devastating forces of man were moving with more startling precision.

Since Nunzio had become Howard's companion the lawless element lacked leadership, they were a sort of unsystematic pirate band. Had they dreamed that

Nunzio was a prisoner, they would have torn Howard Norton to shreds, but there was that in the compelling personality of Howard that demanded respect even tho they hated him.

Finally came a night when the hosts of the modern pirate band became bolder, the doors of the Gilded Palace were thrown wide and Nunzio's favorite brands of liqueurs flowed like water, and then they planned a thing so diabolical one might have been moved to wonder if man's civilization was a myth. They would burn the church, that peaceful little structure nestling so unobtrusively in the valley, and the Padre along with it, or, better still, they would "nail him to the cross" atop the church; they would fire the town and let the Padre watch the flames as they swept toward the church. Every one would be so concerned with saving the town, and later themselves they wouldn't think about the church, apart from the village, the main road of escape did not lead in that direction. Their

plans laid they proceeded with methodic deliberation. 

They would arrange so that the flames would completely circle the church, an easy matter with the help of some old lumber and the adjacent strip of woodsland surrounding the church. They would place a guard about the church to eliminate the possibility of an eleventh hour attempt at rescue.

First in the path of destruction came the Gilded Palace, Nunzio's pride. The smouldering volcano back in the hills mocked the flaming spectacle wiith its puny streaks of smoke. The high winds carry the flames and the people begin to realize the hopelessness of further attempt to combat the flames, and move on to safety. Howard fears for the Padre's safety, but is assured by Peter, (the little mountain waif whom Jean Marie had championed on Howard's first day in the village) that the Padre is further along the line; in this however Peter was mistaken, he was held a prisoner, securely fastened by a rope to the cross atop the church, while the flames in their fury were sweeping on, even then eating their way thru the strip of woods that separated the church from the town. 

Howard takes command, comforting and assisting in every way possible. An old Mexican couple, whose route of escape lies by way of the church, laborously

plods toward safety. The man is a cripple and the woman half carries, half drags her precious burden.

At last exhausted from the arduous task she leaves the man, admonishing him to wait at the church while she goes ahead to secure aid.

As the man waited, and watched the treacherous flames eating their way to the beloved little church, the voice of the Padre comes to him, but he is powerless; had he been capable of reaching the prisoner, the guard nearby would have stayed his hand. 

At last the woman reaches Howard and pleads in broken English with the Senor to save her husband. 

Howard decides to free Nunzio and go alone, realizing the seriousness of his mission. To Howard's amazement, Nunzio declares his intention of accompanying him. At least Nunzio is no coward. Howard cannot believe that he is the man that so feared death that he became his prisoner.

Howard reassures the frantic little woman and the two set out for the church. They reach the church and Howard helped the little woman to safety, not a minute too soon, for as Howard and Nunzio enter the church the flames, fanned into an inferno, cut off their exit. Howard shoulders his burden with Herculean ease only to realize the helplessness of escape. The

three men eye each other and as the realization is forced upon them that they are doomed, the voice of the Padre comes to them above the roar of the flames. 

They dash frantically to the bell tower and see the almost limp figure of the Padre dangling from the cross. With a mad cry Howard prepares to climb out on the roof and go to the rescue, but the restraining hand of Nunzio stops him. Howard looks into the man's face, at his powerful physique. In the mad rush Howard had lost his revolver. He attempted to read Nunzio's mask like countenance, but it told him nothing. If this is his revenge, Howard decided, he has surely chosen a weird setting. The boy waited, and then Nunzio spoke, "Senor, I bring him to you". 

Dared he trust him, how easily he could dash the Padre into the seething flames below, but one look into Nunzio's eyes and the boy would have trusted him with the life of a dozen Padres, there was that there that spoke his sincerity in silence more powerful than the man's conception of English could afford. 

As Nunzio carries the Padre bodily to the brief safety that the inside of the church affords, Howard kneels beside him, the boy blamed himself for the present state of affairs, why had he not done something,

he should have secured outside aid, it was a situation too dangerous for the Padre and he to have attempted. 

Howard takes out his little Bible and reads "I am the Resurrection and the Life, etc.", while the cripple a devout Christian, prays fervently, his eyes fixed on the likeness of our Saviour. Nunzio looks into the composed face of Howard Norton so glorified by the nearness of Death, for it was Death, there was no mistaking that, a dash thru the roaring inferno without would have meant instant death. Could it be possible that there was a God, Nunzio asked himself. 

As Howard's eyes turn pleadingly to Nunzio, he turns away. He believes that any display of feeling at this time would be cowardly, he wasn't afraid to die, it was the end of things he told himself fiercely, men of his type usually died with their boots on, he would be game to the end. He looks sullenly into Howard's eyes, then grinned sarcastically, as he mocked “your 

God not helping you much, eh?"

The soul of Howard Norton writhed—he knows the man is worth saving-he had demonstrated his metal by his willingness to follow into the doomed building. 

In a moment they would be cast into eternity, and Nunzio had denounced his God,-he, Howard, was powerless to save him-to do this one thing to atone

to his Jean Marie, his darling whom he had in a moment of weakness evidently destroyed. 

Out of the bitterness of the boy's soul, feeling that his methods were all wrong, he cries, "God I've done my best and failed, won't you please help me?" 

Outside a roaring inferno of fire and smoke completely surrounded the church-then, as if in answer to Howard's earnest prayer, a wind, seemingly coming from the Heavens, beat the flames low on the ground; the flames circle the tall outer pillars of the little church, they char the framing, the intense heat shatters the windows-then the flames pass on, leaving only the little church in their wake. 

God had answered the boy's prayer, but in a way so miraculous, they are dazed. Nunzio seems rooted to the spot. The cripple with an understanding of 

English has crawled to the organ, his poor crooked feet search for the pedals, and the organ peals "Just as I Am", and the clear bell-like voice of Howard Norton sings the words of the famous hymn.

As the full realization comes to Nunzio that God had given him back his life to prove the wisdom of Howard's teachings, the man bows his head. The last note dies away, the organist slumps forward, exhausted from his effort. Nunzio kneels at the altar,

his eyes search the likeness of the Saviour; he raises his face, squares his shoulders, and says, "Just as I am, God, just as I am."

Sunday morning one week later, all is quiet in the valley again. Even the volcano in the background had been tamed. The little church, the miracle church as it became known through the valley, was jammed with people. Nunzio, gambler, atheist and bad man of the valley, had joined the ranks of the Padre, given his heart to his Saviour, and the people looked on in awe. Many of them, Nunzio's former patrons, had come out of curiosity, but left the church enlightened. 

Still the congregation is not complete. Jean Marie and Mrs. Norton enter. The girl crosses herself as she sees her father kneeling at the altar. Nunzio takes her in his arms and thanks God for the further manifestation of his miraculous power. "Jean Marie," he whispers, I'll never doubt God again. He then hands 

Jean Marie into Howard's keeping. "My wife," the boy breathes reverently, as he turns to his Mother. "I brought her back to you, my son, it was the least I could do to atone", the woman spoke simply, and Howard kisses her as he whispers, "God bless, and thank you, Mother." "Alvez Nunzio commends himself to the mercies of

the law regarding Pedro's untimely death, and is advised that there would probably be only a technical trial, then freedom, as Pedro had proved to be a gang leader of the most vicious type, whose activities were the plague of two continents."

That night as Jean Marie and her husband took leave of the devastated valley, with Mrs. Norton, and prepared for their belated honeymoon, Alvez Nunzio whispered to his daughter, "we are going to another place, a little further up the valley, away from the threatening influence of the volcano, and we are going to build another town, a bigger and better church also for the Padre, but our hearts will make a shrine of our little miracle church." The girl smiled her happiness, even as her eyes overflowed with grateful tears, as she whispered "My father".

Jean Marie and Howard visit Duval in Paris and Howard presents him with another picture of Jean Marie, a more finished work of art than the first. The papers far and near herald the glory of Howard Norton's picture 'While Angels Listen", and Countess 

Baronoff and Mrs. Norton, from their homes, smilethey are at peace with God and the world.
