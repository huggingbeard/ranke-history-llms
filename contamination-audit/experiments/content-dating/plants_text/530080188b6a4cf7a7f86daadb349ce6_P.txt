AUTHOR'S NOTE The general scheme has been to make the work practical instead of academic. For this reason, as well as for the purposes of affording a precedent where there is no reported case, and of illustrating the application of the principle in everyday practice, Law Journal decisions have been cited. These decisions are taken almost entirely from the period between November, 1930, and May, 1931, when the material was in the course of preparation.
In order to make the book still more useful as a ready manual, annotations and forms have been run in the body of the text, instead of as footnotes and appendix respectively. (Bertschy, 14 N. Y. W'kly Dig., Kings Co., Dec. J. Nor is it necessary that the services required are prospective. Even if they have already been rendered, if it appears that the wife will receive no further assistance unless they are paid for and that her situation or condition demands it, an award will be made.
The only specification in the statute which authorizes such an allowance is the provision for the support of the wife and children in Section 1169 of the Civil Practice Act, which would seem to be included in the word "alimony" NEW YORK LAW OF ALIMONY in the caption. There is nothing in the reported cases to justify the allowance, other than the circumstance that the remedy is uniformly granted.
The only similarity of the allowance to counsel fees or any other expense or disbursement, aside from the incidental procedure, is that it is conditioned upon the wife's inability to pay for the services herself. The moving papers must therefore disclose the financial circumstances of the wife in the greatest particular.
The application may be made by way of modification of the order granting or denying alimony or counsel fees (Elder vs Rosenwasser, 238 N. Y. 427, 1924).
Special allowances are generally either for medical or legal services. That does not mean, of course, that only physicians and lawyers may benefit by them. They may be, for example, for hospital treatment and nurses, or for bail premiums respectively.
Medical services The allowance may be general, as to defray doctors' and hospital bills (Lechner, Appellate Division, 2nd Department, January, 1931) or specific, as to pay the expenses of the wife's confinement (Rosen, L. J., Kings Co., Jan. 23, 1931; Mathat, id., April 21, 1931; Balabanich, id., Queens Co., April 8, 1931), or to pay the expenses for the medical care of the wife necessitated by her pregnancy (Liss, id., Kings Co., Feb. 19, 1931). When the confinement is impending, the court may allow the sum in anticipation of it (Stoelzel, id., Kings Co., Jan. 31, 1931) or may defer the application until it is imminent (Hoffman, id., N. Y. Co., Feb. 11, 1931).
An allowance for medical services may be made simultaneously with one for counsel fees and expenses (Briggs, id., Kings Co., April 6, 1931) or on the order for alimony and COUNSEL FEES AND EXPENSES counsel fees (De Candio, id., Nassau Co., March 23, 1931).
The allowance may be made payable in weekly instalments, as one for the children's medical expense (Schwartz, id., Bronx Co., April 9, 1931).
The husband may be permitted to offer proof that the bill is excessive (Gorsuch, id., Nassau Co., March 23, 1931).
The order is reviewable. The Appellate Division may make the award if Special Term refuses it (Lechner, supra).
Legal services Before a special allowance may be had for services rendered to the wife in a matter outside the action, it must appear that the husband is responsible for them as necessaries. That is, no recovery may be had in this summary method unless an action could lie civilly, by the third party directly against the husband. It would not terminate if he were in the wrong until the final decree making her an allowance had taken its place. . . . interlocutory judgment . . . If the Court of Chancery could exercise its discretion in view of such moral considerations as the condition of such a complainant would suggest, it would seem not unreasonable to refuse to enforce a settlement in favor of a woman, who, though still in law a wife, was living in flagrant and profligate disregard of the obligations which formed the inducement to the settlement. . . . Not so. The Court of Chancery will interfere at the instance of the wife, and enforce the specific execution of marriage articles, although the wife is living separate from the husband in a state of adultery; and so, THE JUDGMENT also, to compel the performance of articles binding the husband to her maintenance on an agreement to separate although she then be living in adultery." (pp. 669, 672, italics ours) The analogy between the situations which prevail as to divorce and separation judgments is further pointed out in the observation that the independent state which the wife obtains by being divorced is not the only consideration opposed to a modification. Conceding that her misconduct would neither be countenanced nor condoned and that the natural tendency would be to reduce or vacate the allowance, the court proceeds:- "But this would plainly be extreme, for more than one reason. Possibly her unprotected and destitute condition may be itself the cause of her decline from virtue; and if not so, still she is not to be left to degradation as her only resource." (p. 673) In any event, it would seem that the wife's subsequent misconduct is not such a "change of circumstances" as to bring it within the cases which have made the change the condition to modifying the alimony or striking it out.
Pecuniary circumstances have been determining factors, not moral character. In this connection, the other case referred to may be taken up. In Goodsell, 82 A.D. 65, 81 Sup. 806, 1903, the court denied the husband's application for a re-hearing on the question of alimony following a divorce judgment in favor of the wife, on the ground that her adultery alleged to have been committed before the trial was no longer a matter which could be taken up in determining either the propriety or amount of the allowance. It was conceded, however, that ordinarily the wife's behaviour is a proper consideration in determining the amount.

NEW YORK LAW OF ALIMONY ""The circumstances of the parties,' as used in these statutory provisions, with reference to the original decree, is quite comprehensive, and not only to the pecuniary circumstances and the requirements of the parties in view of the manner in which they had lived, but also authorized and required a consideration of the conduct of each; and, if the wife was to some extent responsible for the misconduct of the husband, or in fault, although this did not justify his acts, yet it might be considered in determining the amount of alimony that she should receive." (p. 808) See also Peckford, 1 Paige Ch. 274, 1828, holding that the wife's indiscretions and other misbehaviour are proper considerations in determining the amount of alimony to be allowed; and Burr, 7 Hill, 207, 1843, p. 218, that the husband's extreme ill-treatment of the wife may serve to make the allowance larger than would otherwise be made. The principle is somewhat analogous to the one which justifies a larger award of alimony so as to include re-imbursement to the wife for expenses incurred by her as a result of the husband's misconduct, despite the fact that alimony is not determined in the same way as damages for torts (Comstock, 49 Misc. 599, 99 Sup. 1057, 1906).
The wife, therefore, exposed herself to the hazard of having the husband's disqualification removed by bringing the contempt motion. Ordinarily, if she did nothing and the husband moved for a reduction, it would have to be denied for his arrears, regardless whether the default was justifiable or not. This feature of the situation has now been eliminated by a drastic change in the attitude of the courts. Motions to reduce are being granted not only where the default is excused (Friedman, L. J., Queens Co., NEW YORK LAW OF ALIMONY March 31, 1931), but where it is condemned by an adjudication in contempt, if the husband shows that he cannot further comply with the order or judgment (Wilson, id., N. Y. Co., Jan. 2, 1931; Alleyne, id., Jan. 7, 1931; Tauber, id., Bronx Co., March 11, 1931; Ribler, id., March 28, 1931; Minnix, id., Kings Co., May 6, 1931), and while serving a sentence for contempt (Bail, id., N. Y. Co., Bronx Co., Nov. 13, 1930; Visic, id., N. Y. Co., Dec. 30, 1930; Leal, id., Kings Co., Jan. 2, 1931).
In conclusion, it may be said that the determining considerations on the motion to reduce are the same whether it is made alone or counter to the contempt motion: except, that if it is made alone, the court may not go into such equitable circumstances as would defeat a motion to punish for impropriety of contempt, viz., the wife's laches, waiver, bad faith, etc., the reason being that they are strictly collateral to the issue.

Leave to purge may be granted even where the husband fails to oppose the motion (Mcdonough, L. J., N. Y. Co., Nov. 28, 1930; Apotheker, id., Feb. 4, 1931), or the order NEW YORK LAW OF ALIMONY may provide for settlement on notice to him (Smith, id., Bronx Co., March 14, 1931).
It should be noted that the purge provision is virtually a modification of the order or judgment granting the allowance, in so far as the manner of paying the amount found to be due is concerned.
The order may not provide that commitment issue on the husband's failure to pay current alimony. The reason is that such a course would be a substitute for a regular contempt proceeding, to which the husband is entitled by the statute. It becomes important, therefore, to determine whether payments which are made following the entry of the order must be applied by the wife towards the arrears. It would seem that she is not obliged to do so, and that remittances forwarded without any accompanying specification by the husband will not serve to pay the fine. If the husband desires to confine the payments to the accrual as determined by the order he must describe them as such. The situation is comparable to that which obtains when the husband makes payments of alimony in a manner other than that provided in the order or judgment (VB, IA2, ante). Here, as there, the wife has the choice of treating the instalments as being paid for a purpose different from the observance of the order; and, exactly as in those cases, the amounts and intervals of the payments may indicate their purpose so clearly as to charge the wife with both knowledge of that purpose and the acceptance of them as such.
Separate purge provisions may be made for counsel fees (Bennett, L. J., N. Y. Co., Dec. 9, 1930; Cook, id., Kings Co., April 25, 1931).
