CHAPTER I 

The Need for Inventions in America The first English settlers in 

America brought with them knowledge of the arts and manufactures of their own country. The 

British king expected them to manufacture the things that England lacked. Foreign artisans were sent with the colonists to set up industries with which the English were unfamiliar. In 1608, eight Poles and Germans were sent to Virginia to make pitch, tar, glass, mills, and soap-ashes. It was the design of the mother country to send over to Virginia "Husbandmen, Gardners, Brewers, Bakers, Sawyers, Carpenters, Joyners, Shipwrights, Boatwrights, Plowwrights, Millwrights, Masons, Turners, Smiths of all sorts, Coopers of all sorts, Weavers, Tanners, Potters, Fowlers, Fish-hookmakers, Netmakers, Shoemakers, Ropemakers, Tilemakers, Edge-tool-makers, Brickmakers, Bricklayers, Dressers of 

Hempe and Flax, Lime-burners, Leather-Dressers, Men skillful in vines, Men for Ironworks, Men skillful in Mines."

In adapting such arts as they knew to a new country, these pioneers developed rare ability. As needs arose, better methods of doing things were invented. In some fields, Americans made inventions before their brothers in 

England ever thought of them.

With a new land to conquer, America's colonists were faced with a great amount of work. Lack of man-power and an overabundance of work turned them to invention. 

Unfortunately, no attempt to gather the facts about industry and invention was made before 1810. Therefore, many interesting inventions of minor importance have doubtless been lost.

In taking up the story of colonial inventions, we must remember some of the things that had not yet been invented. Most of the great inventions that have profoundly affected industry and the social, moral, and political life of nations, had not then been discovered and applied. The power of steam and electricity were barely suspected.

The cotton-gin and the machines for the manufacture of cloth had not yet been thought of. Shipbuilding was but a rude art. Medicine was in its infancy. The use of hard coal in iron manufacture was yet undreamed of. In short, whatever comforts and conveniences we have today, were almost non-existent at the time of the colonists' arrival in the New World.

Colonial invention, however, contributed to man's comfort and opened the way for a great flowering of inventive genius in the 

Nineteenth Century. (1)

CHAPTER II 

Joseph Jenks, the Father of American Invention

In 1643, John Winthrop, the younger, returned to Massachusetts after an absence of two years in England. He brought back with him workmen and machinery with which to set up ironworks at Lynn and Braintree. The master-mechanic was Joseph Jenks (Jenckes), a most ingenious man, from Hammersmith, England. Jenks was fortyone years old-young enough to give many years of service to the colony, and old enough to bring valuable experience to it. It is a generally recognized fact that Joseph Jenks started the first successful ironworks in America. He was the first builder of machinery in this country, and the first man to receive a patent for an invention in America. In fact, he was the man who conceived the idea of a colonial patent law. Such a law had been granted by Act of Parliament in 

England in 1625, and Jenks felt the need of a similar law in the colony of Massachusetts Bay. It has been said that one of the reasons Jenks came to America was the hope that he could carry out some of his new ideas and secure patents for them.

In 1646 the General Court resolved that:

In answer to the peticion of Joseph Jenckes, for liberty to make experience of his abilityes and inventions for ye making of Engines for mills to go with water for ye more speedy dispatch of work than formerly, and mills for ye making of Sithes and other Edged tooles, with a new Invented Sawe-Mill, that things may be afforded cheaper than formerly, and that for fowerteen yeeres without disturbance by any others setting up the like inventions, so that his study and cost may not be in vayne or lost; this peticion is granted so as power is still left to restrain ye exportation of such manufactures, and to moderate ye prizes [prices] thereof if occasion so require. 

On January 20, 1647, he purchased the right to manufacture scythes and other edge-tools at the ironworks. In 1655, he was granted another patent for an improved grass-scythe "for the more speedy cutting of grass, for seven years."

The new thing about this scythe was that the blade was thinner and longer, and was thickened on the back by a welded bar of iron, much as in the modern scythe. 

The old English scythe was very clumsy and slow, short and thick like the bush or stub scythe. To this day no important change has been made in the grass-scythe.

He should be remembered because he was the first founder who worked in iron and brass in our country. He made the first models and the first castings of many domestic tools and implements. A small pot which would hold about a quart is said to have been the first article that Jenks made of cast-iron. In 1652, Massachusetts decided to coin money, a thing very irritating to the British king; but England was far away, and the colony needed money. After the first issue, the date was never changed on the coins, in order that it might appear that there was only one issue.

In October, 1652, Joseph Jenks cast the dies at the ironworks. The issue, struck from the Jenks dies, consisted of threepences, sixpences, and shillings. These coins were of sterling silver, by weight "two pence in the shilling of less valew than the English coyne." 

They were stamped with the word "Masatusets, with a pine tree on one side; on the other side, "New England, Anno 1652," and the number of pence in Roman numerals. The design for the coins was suggested by the wife of Joseph Jenks II, the daughter-in-law of Joseph Jenks who cast the dies. The younger

Mrs. Jenks is known to history because she was brought before the Court of Rhode Island for wearing silver lace on her dress.

There is a story that upon a visit to England, Sir Thomas Temple showed some of the money to 

Charles II. The King inquired what kind of a tree was on the coins. Sir Thomas quickly replied that it was a royal oak tree, the tree that had saved His Majesty's life. The King was either amused or convinced, for he only called the colonists "a set of honest dogs" and listened quietly to Temple's defense of his colonial subjects.

Two years after casting the dies for the pine-tree shillings and pence, at the request of the selectmen of Boston, Jenks built a fireengine. This was the first “Ingine to carry water in case of fire" ever built in America. In Europe there were few such engines. Paris did not have one for fifty years after Jenks built the Boston engine.

CHAPTER III  1. Early Iron Manufacture 

Joseph Jenks did not establish the first ironworks in America, but he did establish the first successful ironworks. He was also the first man to make things out of iron in the New World. However, as early as 1619, no less than one hundred fifty ironworkers were sent to Virginia to set up three ironworks. 

They were to make bars of iron, only, which could be sent to England to be made up into manufactured articles. The colonists were expected to buy back their own iron in the form of manufactured goods; but the first ironworks in Virginia had scarcely opened when the Indians fell upon the settlement, destroying the ironworks and glass-house, and killing nearly all the settlers.

The next movement for the manufacture of iron came in New 

England. Iron was found there in the peat-bogs and ponds in great quantities. It was called "bog iron." 

The Massachusetts Bay colonists needed iron for shipbuilding, mills, dwelling-houses, tools, household articles, and firearms. Thomas Dexter, Robert 

Bridges, and others formed a plan to introduce iron manufacture into 

New England. John Winthrop the younger, who was deeply interested in iron manufacture, was already in England trying to raise money for such a project. In 1643, Bridges took some samples of ore to England to show to certain wealthy men whom he hoped to interest in mining in the new country. The ore turned the trick. Winthrop and Bridges formed a company called the "Company of Undertakers for the Iron-works." At various places ironworks were set up and met with varying success. 

The investors seemed to have their ups and downs, with plenty of downs.

There seemed to be few improvements in the manufacture of iron during the remainder of the Seventeenth Century. This may have been due to the struggle to get ironworks started. One of the very first improvements invented was the casting of iron in sand rather than in clay molds. Jeremy Florio, a clever Englishman, introduced this art, which was promoted by 

Joseph Mallinson, early in the 

Eighteenth Century. In 1739 the 

General Court of Massachusetts gave Mallinson two hundred acres of unimproved land for his services in the manufacture of hollow-ware in sand moulds. In speaking of cast-iron hollow-ware, it is interesting to note that the first cast-iron teakettle was made in Plympton, Massachusetts, between 1760 and (5)

2. Hugh Orr; Edge-tools and Cannon

In 1741, the town of Bridgewater received a young Scotchman, Hugh Orr, who made mechanical history. He was an expert gunsmith and locksmith. He applied to a scythe-maker for work and proved his mechanical ability by making a keen razor out of the handle of an old skillet. Needless to say, he was quickly employed.

Later, Orr set up his own shop and built the first trip-hammer in the colonies.

He began to experiment with making scythes, and it was due to his efforts that an axe factory and a scythe factory were eventually set up in Rhode Island and Connecticut. For years Orr was the only edge-tool maker in that part of the country. So great was his skill that men would travel twenty miles to have an adze, an augur, or an axe tempered by him. 

About 1748 Orr made five hundred stand of arms for the Province of Massachusetts Bay. These were stored in Castle William (Boston), but nearly all of them were later carried away by British soldiers. They are said to be the first muskets ever made in this country. When the Revolutionary 

War began, Orr was in sympathy with the colonists. A "French gentleman" was his partner. Orr and his partner adopted a new way of making cannon, which had just been invented in Europe. Up to this time a cannon was cast with a cylinder-like cavity a little less than the desired calibre, and this was afterward bored out to the required size. Such a cannon was likely to be spongy in that part where strength and smoothness were most needed. Orr adopted the new method of casting the gun of solid metal, then cutting out and smoothing the cylinder with a boring-iron and cutter. His brass cannon from three to fortytwo-pounders, besides a vast amount of cannonballs, were of enormous value to the Revolutionary Army. 

After the Revolution, Orr resumed the manufacture of edgetools, and helped to establish industries of various kinds in the new states. He was a staunch advocate of the machine as a substitute for the human hand. As early as 1753 he invented a machine for cleaning flax. His activities with machinery for making cloth will be mentioned in another chapter. 

Hugh Orr had ten children. One son, Robert, followed his father's trade. He made scythes with a trip-hammer and introduced the

iron shovel into Massachusetts. 

Robert Orr made his shovels light for the troops in the Revolutionary Army, changing the shovels considerably from the English models. 

John Ames and his son Oliver carried on the making of these shovels in Easton near Boston.

Later Oliver devoted his time to improving the iron shovel, making it less durable but less heavy to handle. He said that it was easier to replace shovels than human muscles. 

3. The Nail-Machine 

Before the nail-machine was invented, nails were made by hand.

Iron bars were cut into rods at a slitting-mill, and men cut and hammered these rods into shape on small forges. Nearly every chimney-corner had its little forge where the men of the family hammered out nails in bad weather and in the long evenings of winter. 

Farmers made nails for their own use, exchanging their surplus for bar-iron or iron rods for nail-making. At Bridgewater, near Boston, in 1786, Ezekiel Reed invented a machine to cut tacks and nails. 

His son Jesse later invented a machine to cut and head tacks with one operation at the rate of sixty thousand per day. 

In 1792, Jacob Perkins invented a machine that could cut and head two hundred nails a day. Such a machine could be used with either water-power, horse-power, or steampower. The nails were considered better than English nails and they sold twenty per cent cheaper.

Many of Perkins's inventions won for him gold and silver medals from the Society of Arts in London. 

Possibly no man did more to raise English respect for American inventiveness than did Jacob Perkins. 

The nail-machine was a great saver of time and labor. 

It was modified by Thomas Odiorne of Malden, Massachusetts, to cut brads. The English thought very highly of the nail-machine. It was soon adopted abroad. The new machine enabled the country to supply an enormous demand for tacks and nails at home, and it made possible, also, the shipping of vast quantities to foreign countries. The nail-machine was one of the sights of the day. It was so popular that a song was written about it. The first stanza ran as follows: 

"Sure, have you seen, a nail machine 'Tis all the people's wonder, O, It thumps away, both night and day

In the same year that Jeremiah made the tacks for his wool-cards, the second Continental Congress offered fifty pounds sterling for the first twenty-five dozen domestic pins equal to those imported from

During the Revolution Wilkinson made pins and needles out of wire drawn by himself. Imported wire and tools were almost impossible to obtain during the War. Jeremiah went to drawing wire by horse-power. He made his own tools, dies, and plates. Needles were so dear that Jeremiah's wife bought a spinning-wheel with three of her husband's darning-needles.

During this scarcity of provisions and supplies, Jeremiah invented a corn-mill for the manufacture of corn-syrup. He ground cornstalks in his new mill and pressed them in a cider-mill for the sweet juice.

The juice was then boiled into syrup. 

David Wilkinson invented machine to cut screws. He had worked in his father's factory since he was a mere lad. His father was 

Oziel Wilkinson, whose iron-factory at Pawtucket, Rhode Island, was considered the hub of the New England iron-manufacturing business. Oziel was not the father of 

Jeremiah, but probably an uncle.

In 1786, Oziel Wilkinson had begun to manufacture iron screws for oil presses and clothiers' presses. The cutting and finishing of these screws always interested David, and he worked on this problem for several years. In 1798, he took out a patent for a new machine to cut screw threads, which used the sliderest. This was the first screwcutting machine invented in the

United States, though an Englishman must be given credit for the basic invention. 

In 1788-89, David and his father helped Samuel Slater to make the first successful machines in America for the manufacture of cotton. 

Oziel spent the last years of his life in the cotton manufacturing business with Slater. 

David perfected a mill to bore cannon with water-power. The boring-tool was stationary, and the cannon revolved against it.

In the panic of 1819 David lost all his money. In 1848, the United 

States government granted him ten thousand dollars as a reward for his slide-rest invention. That invention had been adopted by the government in the manufacture of fire-arms. The slide-rest is an attachment for a lathe, or other machine tool, which holds the tool or cutter firmly while in motion,

usually feeding by means of screws. 

As David Wilkinson & Company, David and his brother Daniel went into business for themselves in Pawtucket for the manufacture of iron products. In nearly every state along the Atlantic seaboard the manufactured goods of David 

Wilkinson & Company were sold. 

5. Some Curious Cannon and

Other Inventions 

William Denning, a Pennsylvania blacksmith, tried to serve his country by inventing and making wrought-iron cannon of curious design. Only two of these wroughtiron cannon are known to exist today. One was captured by the 

British at the Battle of Brandy wine; they were so captivated by it that they afterward took it to the Tower of London. It was considered a relic of American skill and patriotism. The other, an unfinished specimen, was placed in the Philadelphia Arsenal. These singular cannon were made of wrought-iron staves and hooped with wrought-iron bands like a barrel. There were four layers of staves "breaking joint, all of which were firmly bound together, and then boxed and breeched like other cannon.

Denning claimed that he made the "only successful attempt. . in the world to manufacture wrought-iron cannon." He completed two small ones in Middlesex, Pennsylvania, and started. another one at Mount Holly. He never finished the third one, because he could get no one to help him. No workman was willing to work in the intense heat required. 

Denning claimed that the heat was so intense that it melted the lead buttons on his coat. The British offered a stated annual payment besides a large sum of money to anyone who would show them how to make the wrought-iron cannon; but Denning preferred to remain a poor and obscure blacksmith rather than to surrender his secret to the enemy of his country. Ironically enough, his country did not give him any help until near the close of his long life. He died at the age of ninety-four in 1830. 

Samuel Wheeler, of Philadelphia, also made wrought-iron cannon during the Revolutionary War. 

They were "of a new construction invented by him." His cannon were made without hoops and were lighter than brass cannon. They had a longer range and were more accurate. Napoleon I took the 

Wheeler cannon as a model, and had cannon for his flying artillery manufactured after that pattern.

In 1777, a still more interesting specimen of field artillery was used by the Indians at Fort Henry. One evening a large party of Indians advanced in the dusk within sixty yards of the fort, with a cannon made of a hollow maple log. 6. David Rittenhouse and his

Scientific Inventions One of the important men of science in the colonial period was 

David Rittenhouse, instrumentmaker, astronomer, and mathematician. David was a greatgrandson of William Rittenhouse, who established the first paper mill in America. Young David had very limited opportunities for obtaining an education. He had remarkable mechanical ability, however. Because he had so little with which to work, he early formed the habit of making what he needed from whatever lay at hand.

One of the most important things that David Rittenhouse accomplished, was the construction of apparatus for the observation of the transit of Venus.

The transit of Venus is the passing of the star Venus across the sun's disk. At certain times the planet can pass between us and the sun, appearing as a black dot on the sun which can be seen through a telescope. The last two transits of Venus took place in 1874 and 1882. The next two will not occur until 2004 and 2012. You will notice that the transits are eight years apart, and then follows a long period of more than a hundred years. They run regularly with the short interval followed by the long interval. 

In 1768, Rittenhouse presented to the American Philosophical Society at Philadelphia his calculations on the transit of Venus, which was to take place the next year. The 

Society considered his calculations so accurate that they appointed committees to prepare for and to observe the approaching event.

They selected the State House 

Square, "Norriton" (the Rittenhouse farm), and Cape Henlopen as the observation sites. 

Thomas Penn, the legislature of

Pennsylvania, and public institutions provided the funds for the apparatus required for the three observation points. On his own farm, David built the observatory and the equipment for his observations. In this he included a transit telescope, the first telescope ever built in America. He also made an equal-altitude instrument and an excellent clock. The great English scientist, Newcomb, considered Rittenhouse's observations of the transit of Venus among the best ever made up to that time. With his report on the transit of Venus, Rittenhouse made for himself a worldwide reputation. 

The year before Rittenhouse made the above calculations, he designed an orrery, for which he was celebrated at that time. This orrery represented the motions of the solar system and showed the eclipses of both sun and moon, and other phenomena, for a period of five thousand years forward, or five thousand years backward, in time. Thomas Jefferson, who a little later invented a copying machine and a scientific mouldboard for a plow, said of this orrery: "He has not indeed made a world; but he has by imitation approached nearer its maker than any man who has lived from the creation to this day." Before the Revolution broke out, there was a plan under way to build a public observatory, with David Rittenhouse at its head. The plan had already been approved in England by Lord 

North and a man by the name of 

Maskelyne, the royal astronomer.

8. Early Steel-making

Colonial steel-making began with 

Joseph Higby in 1728. was a clever blacksmith of Granby,

Connecticut. He claimed to have "found out and obtained a curious art," "with great pains and cost."

He further stated that he was "the very first" to convert common 66

iron into good steel." For these reasons he asked for himself and

Joseph Dewey of Hebron the exclusive right to practice "the business or trade of steelmaking" for twenty years. The conservative 

Connecticut Court granted the right for ten years, provided that Higby and Dewey would improve the art to reasonable perfection within two years.

Higby became well known by his "Granby coppers." In 1737 and 1739 he struck coins from the 

Simsbury copper. The "coppers" were in great demand by goldbeaters for an alloy. The coins bore various designs, some a broadaxe, with the motto, "I cut my way through." Others bore a sledge-hammer under a crown; whether this was a symbol of the high-handed authority of the British Crown or not is not known.

Eli Whitney and His New

Method of Manufacture 

Eli Whitney, having despaired of profiting from an earlier invention, turned his attention to the manufacture of guns. In 1798, he secured a contract with the United States government to furnish ten thousand stand of muskets to be finished in two years. Whitney had an original idea about manufacturing that he wanted to try out. 

He thought it possible to make the same parts of different guns as much like each other as the successive prints of a photograph. 

Military men men in England and France laughed at the idea. Even the officials at Washington were uneasy when they promised Whitney $134,000, with not a single finished gun in sight. Whitney, of course, was well-known as the inventor of the cotton-gin, and his countrymen had faith in his ability to carry out his ideas. 

Ten men of New Haven went on his bond and furnished the money for the factory. Thomas

Jefferson at that time was Vice- 

President of the United States.

These Whitney deftly put together, and there was a complete musket! He continued the demonstration until ten complete guns, instead of ten piles of parts, lay on the table. The experts were convinced. Immediately after this demonstration, Whitney's time was extended and more money was advanced.

Whitney had undoubtedly reduced an extremely complicated process to a few simple operations.

The machines were so simple that a man with little or no experience in gun-making could tend them. 

Expert gunsmiths were no longer required to manufacture guns. The machine took the place of the skilled human hand. Whitney worked very hard. He was up every morning at daybreak to begin the day's work. He said that he could not be away an hour without being needed for some reason. 

Every night before he went to bed, he put everything in readiness for the next day. It was Whitney's factory-he was the blood and heart and brain of it all.

In 1742 Franklin made an original and important invention. It was called the "Pennsylvania fireplace," and later became known as the Franklin stove. It came into a world that was almost stoveless. The Dutch and Germans had brought stoves from the Old World, but the English heated their homes only with fireplaces. These old fireplaces were more romantic than practical. A great deal of the heat went up the chimney. Cold chills played up and down one's back while one's face was scorching. 

The only thing that the English colonists had that even resembled the stove was the small tin footstove that was sometimes carried to church by a lady to warm her feet. This little foot-stove was filled with coals to provide a little comfort in the unheated church. 

Franklin's Pennsylvania fireplace retained the heat that had formerly gone up the chimney. It warmed the air in an air box, and sent the warmed air into the room through shutters in the stove. Heat radiated from the closed top and sides, as well as from the open front. 

It did not smoke; it saved fuel, and took away none of the pleasures of an open fire. The Franklin stoves you see in antique shops often are not the original Pennsylvania fireplace at all. The original 

Franklin stove was set into the fireplace. It was not set out into the room and connected with a flue, as were the later models, improved by manufacturers. writing of his stove, Franklin said:

Franklin said: But

We enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours; and this we should do freely and generously. 

This shows Franklin's life philosophy. He was thrifty and he was a money-maker, but he was no miser. He always thought in terms of benefit to his fellow-men.

If Benjamin Franklin had not devoted so much time to public service, he might have given the electric light to the world. His early experiments in electricity made him a famous figure in the scientific world. The story of how he brought down electricity from the clouds with a silken kite tied to a wet string is too well known to be repeated here. Franklin discovered that electricity is scattered through most substances, and that nature is always ready to restore its state of balance. He developed the theory of positive and negative electricity. He and his friends played with this strange newlydiscovered force like a group of frolicking boys. They made a cork spider, and with electricity made it move in a most life-like manner. They relighted candles that had just been blown out; they gave shocks by kissing or touching other persons; they charged glasses of wine that shocked the drinkers when their lips touched the glasses. 

They set gunpowder and alcohol afire with an electric current that had already passed through water. 

They experimented with the Leyden jar (invented in Holland) and made an electrical battery. With this they killed a turkey, roasted it on a spit turned by electricity, and enjoyed a feast. 

The improvements that Franklin made in the Leyden jar itself were valuable. He used ground-up lead instead of water in the jar. He made a foil-coated pane of glass called the Franklin pane.

This was used long afterward in wireless telegraphy and in the treatment of disease with electricity. He made various electrical machines, which were of scientific value rather than of practical use.

After his famous experiment with the kite, Franklin invented the lightning-rod. This was an iron rod which was placed six or eight feet above the highest part of the building it was designed to protect. The rod was tapered gradually to a very fine point, which was made of gilt to prevent rust. The lower end of the rod entered the ground and went down two or three feet, where the earth would be moist. When under the surface, it was turned so as to go

This arrangement of the rod prevented damage to the foundation of the building. The electricity was supposed to be caught with the fine point and conducted to the earth where it would be made harmless. An English traveler wrote in 1759-1760 that "no house was ever struck, where they were fixed."

Whenever Franklin had a moment to spare, he amused himself by inventing or discovering something. He laid different colors of cloth on the snow and observed how fast the snow melted under each. In this way he hoped to discover which colors are the best conductors of heat. He proved that wind cools only by the loss of surface moisture. He was in favor of the fresh-air bath, when fresh air was considered dangerous by most people.

When he became bored by longwinded speeches in the Assembly, he invented a mathematical game called "magic squares." For an

Italian friend, who was a musician, he invented the "Armonica," a string of glasses tuned to the ascending scale and played with wet fingers. By building and experimenting with a toy canal and toy ships, he discovered that light vessels moved faster in the canal than did heavy ones. In 1769, he charted the Gulf Stream. In 1773 he discovered vitamins, though he did not call them by that name.

He proved the reviving power of the sun's rays by placing flies, that had been drowned in wine, in the sunshine. The apparently lifeless flies, warmed by the sun, came back to life and went buzzing about their business as usual.

It would take too long to name all the inventions and discoveries of this unusual man. However, we must note one more invention the bifocal spectacles. In his old age, he grew tired of changing spectacles for far sight and near sight. He simply had "the glasses cut, and half of each kind placed in the same circle." In that way the the proper glasses were always ready. Besides many mechanical inventions, Franklin established the hospital and the circulating library.

1. Ship-building

When the Puritans came to New 

England, they were faced with new conditions. They had come to make permanent homes but they had to adapt themselves to a new country. One of the first things that tested their ability was the building of boats. At first these boats were built on Indian models. 

The birch-bark canoe was light and could easily be carried from one stream to another; but it was tricky to handle. Many a good 

Puritan lost both his dignity and his tall hat in a ducking while learning to manage a birch-bark canoe. The hollowed-out log canoe was heavier, but easier to manage. 

The first boat ever built in this country for commercial use was the Virginia. It was built at the mouth of the Kennebec River in Maine in the fall of 1607. She was a queer little boat, provided with both sails and oars. The hull had a part of a deck enough to protect the cargo.

When the wind was light, the crew enjoyed an awning, but when the wind blew hard, they must take in the sails and face the gale. Such a ship was built by men who intended to use her for fishing, coastwise trade with the Indians, and overseas trade. It is known that she made at least one trip to England and back to the Chesapeake Bay.

There were two things that brought about a change in shipbuilding in the New World. In the first place, money was scarce.

Ships were built with hardly a shilling changing hands. Workmen were paid with goods. The neighbors who went together to build a ship traded farm produce, fish, or lumber to the merchant for iron.

The merchant sent such products to England for such things as iron and sailcloth. Instead of produce, the merchant was sometimes given a share in the boat. Only by the strictest economy and careful management could a boat be set afloat.

In those days European ships had great cabins piled high at the stern end. These were ornamented with colored paints and carvings.

The New Englanders were forced to build their ships for practical purposes. They found afterward that they had unknowingly invented a new and better kind of ship. The tall cabins made the 

European ship top heavy and they strained the hulls. Moreover, they held the ship back in a stiff wind. 

The American ship was stronger, faster, easier to handle, and of greater loading capacity. 

The second thing that forced the 

New Englanders to invent a new kind of ship was a matter of geography. The American ship must (19)

Colonial Inventions be prepared to meet winds from any direction. There was neither trade wind nor monsoon to depend upon in fishing or coasting voyages; therefore, the ship must be rigged to sail against unfavorable winds.

In England, ships with deep hulls were built for deep-water ports. 

In America the water was more shallow along the coast. The hull must be widened rather than deepened for more cargo-space. The broadened beam gave strength to the boat. A wide vessel could carry wide sails, all the while standing "up like a church" in a heavy wind. A wide hull of light draft and with wide sails spread upon stout spars made a fast ship.

The short distances between harbors called for small ships and small crews. That meant more captains, men trained to take responsibility. The short passage made the crew willing to take a risk in bad weather. Sailing in bad weather made the crew conscious of faults in the building of the ship. 

Whenever a ship failed to do what was expected of her, the inventive minds of her builders found some way of improving her.

American sailors were high-priced, compared with English sailors. 

Money was scarce. It is easy to see why American ships were rigged in a way to have as small a crew as possible. That meant that the rig of the sloop (something like a present-day yacht) was the most popular for the first one hundred years in the colonies. Sloops could go on long voyages carrying forty tons of cargo with no more than four men.

4. Inland Travel by Water 

For inland waters, boats suited to the needs of the colonists were

Colonial Inventions invented. The keel-boat was an improved batteau or canoe with long oars and a rudder. It was so named because it had a heavy timber about four inches wide and four inches thick along the full length of its bottom. This was to protect the boat when it hit anything in the river. The keel-boat was usually from forty to seventy-five feet long and from seven to nine feet wide, carrying a mast and sails.

One steersman and two men at the sweeps (long oars) could easily take it down-stream. Coming upstream, it must be moved by poles, like the pole-boat. Four, six, eight, or twenty men (according to the size of the boat) were armed with long hickory, or ash poles, the ends of the poles tipped with heavy wrought-iron spikes. An equal number of men stood on each side of the boat near the bow. Facing the stern of the boat, each man planted his spike in the floor of the river at an angle. With the poles against their shoulders, and bodies bent forward, the men "walked" the boat against the current. Thus they pushed the boat forward about a boat's length, as they paced the narrow walking-boards attached to the sides of the boat. 

When the two men in front reached the stern of the boat, they held the distance gained, while the others ran back to the bow and dug their spiked poles into the river-bed again. This was a hard way to travel, but men travelled long distances in this way. They were familiar with hardship, and knew no easier way to travel. 

The Durham boat was a canoeshaped keel-boat. It was invented and built at Durham, Pennsylvania, about 1750. It was designed to carry iron and farm produce to market. This boat was about sixty feet long, eight feet wide, and two feet deep. It drew only twenty inches of water and could carry fifteen tons of freight. It carried two sails and was manned by a crew of five. One man steered, while four pushed the boat forward by setting poles against the bottom of the stream.

About 1687, the first horsecoaches appeared in the streets of Boston. The pious ones among the colonists scorned them and refused to ride in such works of Satan. 

The new coaches were little used outside of the towns, for there were no bridges and roads were very poor. When a stream was too deep for a carriage to ford, the coach was set into two canoes side by side, two wheels in each canoe, and ferried across the stream. The horses were unhitched and made to swim across the stream.

There were three types of colonial coaches. One was like the heavy carriage used in England.

The other two were suited to conditions in a new country; each of these was drawn by one horse. The first one was called a chair; the second a chaise. For a hundred years these vehicles changed very little, except to grow more ornate. They were built by local blacksmiths and wheelwrights, who took pride in their workmanship. Just before the

Revolution, they were painted in brilliant hues. Bodies of red and blue and brown, with trimmings and panels of harmonizing colors. 

Brightly painted wheels gave color and dash to the streets of the colonial towns. In many villages no one owned a carriage of any kind. It is said that when Governor

In winter, the Northern colonists rode in sleighs. The idea of the 

American sleigh came from Canada; it was in general use by 1700. 

The Canadians called the sleigh a "cariole," but the New Englanders preferred a more common name.

A sleigh drawn by two horses was called a "pung;" drawn by one horse, it was called a "pod." Into such a conveyance the farmer packed his wife and children and the produce of his farm. He trotted alongside, guiding the horse, on the way to market. To the side of the pung or pod was tied the family lunch-a generous chunk of frozen bean porridge. From the Indians the Americans adopted snowshoes and dog-sleds for travelling.

Outside the towns, crude homemade barrows, carts, and wagons slowly developed as the need for them arose. A horse-barrow was a large wheel-barrow drawn by horses. Its three wheels were often made of solid rounds from a tree trunk. The first four-wheeled wagons were very small with flat beds and perfectly straight bodylines. Sometimes a wagon was partly or wholly covered by a homespun piece of "linsey-woolsey" to protect the mother and children as they rode along, while the father walked and led the horse. The twowheeled cart, copied from European models, was used for nearly two centuries as a means of transport from farm to village.

Soon after 1700, a new kind of conveyance appeared-the freight wagon. Between Perth Amboy and 

Burlington, New Jersey, these wagons carried passengers and freight whenever a load could be procured.

The wagons were painted in gaudy colors and had tops of canvas stretched over wooden bows. The large wheels were shod with tires of hard wood or thin iron, six to ten inches wide. The freight wagon was the germ of regu- larly scheduled transportation. By 

1771, the speed of these wagons had been increased so much that the trip from New York to Philadelphia could be made in a day and

The wagon was made with straight sides and a long tunnel-like covering of "linsey-woolsey." Inside were three or four backless wooden benches placed cross-wise in the wagon-bed. To reach the back seat, the passenger had to climb over the seats in front. After a time, the wagon began to turn into a coach. The wagon-top was flattened and leather curtains took the place of the homespun cover. 

Backs for the benches were the next improvement, made of boards or strips of leather. The body of the wagon slowly increased in height and width. The crowning improvement came with the introduction of springs of wrought-iron or leather. 

As the occasional freight wagon slowly evolved into a regular stagecoach line, the packhorse men began to oppose the building of better roads. Each wagon-road meant one less "tote-road" or "horseway." Good wagon-roads meant that the pack-train would disappear and that the packhorse men would have to go out of business. We have seen the same thing happen in our time, when the good roads and automobiles ruined the business of the small-town merchants; though it must be said that the merchants did not fight the good roads and motor-cars. They did not realize what the combination would do to their business, until after it was well established. 

The wagon-road led to an invention called the "corduroy road."

It was made by placing small logs side by side across the road. Over the logs there was placed a covering of dirt two or three inches deep. 

When the rains washed the dirt away travellers received a good jouncing as they bounced along over the bare logs. The corduroy road soon shook a wagon to pieces, and people began to think mudholes might not be any worse than corduroy roads.

About the time that the stagecoaches came into general use, the 

Conestoga wagon made its appearance. The Conestoga wagon was distinctly an American invention. for overland travel. It appeared in

Pennsylvania about 1750 and remained in use for long overland trips for a hundred years. It may have appeared first in the Conestoga valley; or it may have been named for the heavy draught horses that drew it, horses that were bred in the Conestoga Valley. 

The Conestoga wagon was a huge, lumbering affair, very heavily built. It was higher in front and in the back than in the middle, like a boat. This was to keep the contents of the wagon from spilling out when going up or down hill.

The body of the wagon was built deep to hold as much as possible. 

It was topped with a grayish-white cover of heavy home-woven cloth.

1. Some Early Experiments in 

Steamboat-building The steamboat did not originate in the mind of an American, but Americans contributed to its development. In France as early as 1641 a "madman" was clapped into jail because he believed that ships could be driven by steam. In 1695 and 1698, Denis Papin of France and Thomas Savery of England had the same idea. In 1736, Jonathan Hulls of England drew the plans for a steam-tug, but there is no record that Hulls ever tried such a boat on the water. 

The first American´ inventor to experiment with a steamboat was 

William Henry of Lancaster, Pennsylvania. Henry was an expert gunsmith. His rifles were so accurate in performance that the Henry rifles were famous throughout the colonies. He became very wealthy by forming a partnership with Joseph Simon, a Jew. Henry furnished the mechanical genius and

Simon furnished the capital. The combination created wealth for both partners. During the Indian wars, Henry was the chief armorer to the troops. In his laboratory he did a great deal of research in gunmaking and made some experiments with steam. 

In 1761, William Henry went to

England on business. There he met 

James Watt, "the father of the steam-engine." Watt explained to

Henry the working principles of his steam-engine. Henry was so impressed by the engine that he began to build a steamboat as soon as he came back home. By 1763 the boat was ready. On Conestoga 

Creek at Lancaster, William Henry launched the first steamboat in 

America. The boat was too heavy for its displacement and sank; and that was the end of William Henry's steamboat. 

Some time later, Henry presented another steamboat design to the American Philosophical Society at Philadelphia, but nothing came of it. Both John Fitch and 

Robert Fulton visited in Henry's home. Steamboats must have been discussed on these visits. Fitch and Fulton were destined to carry on the work that Henry began. 

As a steamboat builder, Henry was not a success; but as a citizen, he was very valuable to his country. He held many offices of public trust. Among his successful inventions were a steam heating-system, machines for his gunworks, the rag carpet, and a screw augur. He also invented a "sentinel register." This (32)

was a device that opened and closed a flue-damper in a furnace by using the expanding force of warm air. 

As early as 1773, another American was experimenting with steam.

He saw that this vast new country needed many hands to develop its riches. At that time there were fewer men in all the colonies than there are now in one of our large cities. Oliver Evans thought of steam as something that could be used on both land and water. He kept thinking of how steam expands, and how that expanding force might be harnessed. Why not drive machines with that force?

Steam might do the work of hundreds of hands; thereupon Evans decided to invent a steam-engine to generate power. The steamengine already invented was a lowpressure engine. James Watt, the 

English inventor, thought that the high-pressure engine was too dangerous because of the grave risk of explosion. Evans believed in the high-pressure engine sincerely. Independently of James Watt, he produced the first high-pressure steam-engine. He richly deserved the name of "the Watt of America."

In the building of a steam-dredge to clean the city docks of Philadelphia, Evans invented a steamboat as well as the first steam carriage or locomotive in America. 

In 1803, he built this strange mechanical boat-wagon in his shop a mile and a half away from the Schuylkill River. Being a builder of steam-engines, he found it easy to plant one of his engines in the boat, set the boat on wheels, and run it

2. John Fitch 

In the year 1785, John Fitch had the idea of making a vehicle that would move on land by the force of steam. After a few days, he discarded that idea as impractical. 

What could such land-carriages do without good roads to travel on? Roads were few, and the few were very poor. At that time, the rivers were the highways of America. Naturally, Fitch's mind turned to boats. Why not use steam to push boats through the water?

John Fitch was a peculiar person, a man of varied experience. He had been a gunsmith, a watch- and clock-repairer, a map-maker, a surveyor, an explorer of backwoods country, and a captive of the Indians. He had been born into a 

Connecticut Puritan home. His father had worked him too hard and had given him little education and affection. With money earned from the sale of a few bushels of potatoes, John sent to New 

York by a neighbor for a geography. His father had refused to buy the geography, but John was determined to have it, for would it not tell all about the whole world?

He learned every fact in the geography, and became known as the best geographer in his community. 

John educated himself as opportunity offered. He seemed to have been born under an unlucky star, for all his life long, some other person had the luck that John Fitch deserved.

Congress for help. He had invented a boat that was especially adapted to the great rivers of the Westthose broad highways to rich new lands. The report was shelved.

1. Beginnings of Cloth Manu facture in America

In early colonial times, the implements of cloth manufacture were simple and crude. The longwheel was used for spinning thread from hemp, flax, wool, the wild silk-grass, and cotton imported from the West Indies. The Scotch- 

Irish, in the middle of the Eighteenth Century, brought the flaxwheel to America. This little wheel soon became a part of the equipment of nearly every cottage and farmhouse. Early colonial women may have used the spindle and the distaff, but little reference to it may be found. There was an old form of hand-loom used until 1670, when it was replaced by a new Dutch loom. There was a simple form of the shuttle, and hand-cards and hand-combs for preparing both animal and vegetable fibers for spinning. Every step in the making of cloth required great labor.

Each operation was done imperfectly with imperfect tools. A few "fulling-mills" existed, but most of the home-woven cloth was "fulled" at home. Much of this cloth was worn without fulling. 

The process of fulling cloth was of scrubbing, pounding, one straightening, and smoothing.¹ 

When the Spanish explorers came to America they found the natives with an abundance of cotton and cotton fabrics. It is possible that cotton is as old in America as in India. The first English settlers found little or no cotton along the Atlantic coast; but it was not long until they were bringing the "vegetable wool" from the West Indies; and then they began to plant and cultivate it. On the uplands, away from the sea, they found wild cotton growing. This was to be the great crop of the future. The cultivated cotton with the black seeds and long fibers grew only near the sea; hence, it was called sea-island cotton.

3. Eli Whitney and his Cotton-gin

Eli Whitney is known to all as the inventor of the cotton-gin that completely changed the cotton industry. By making possible the easy and rapid cleaning of the green-seed cotton, a new industry was born. Slavery was placed on a paying basis. One slave could operate the Whitney gin, thus releasing great numbers of slaves to tend vast cotton fields. A rivalry between the North and the South then sprang up that finally split our young Republic in two. Eli

Whitney had the usual boyhood of a celebrated inventor. went to school, as other boys did, but did poorly in everything but arithmetic. Always he wanted to make things with his hands. He made and repaired violins, manufactured nails, and made and repaired tools. When only a child, he took his father's watch apart and put it back together, perfectly.

4. Early Carding Machines 

In the making of cotton or woolen cloth there are five steps.

Cotton must be picked, and wool must be clipped from the sheep's back. The cotton must be ginned and the wool must be scoured.

The third step is the carding of the fibers into soft rolls. The fourth step is to spin the rolls into thread. 

The fifth step is to weave the threads into cloth. It is the third step that is to be discussed, at this point in the story of cotton. 

In the homes of town and country people the hand-cards were a necessary part of house-keeping equipment. Many of these cards were made at home, a slow and hard process. It was while making hand-cards that Jeremiah Wilkinson thought of a machine to make tacks. He needed tacks to fasten the covering of leather to the card boards.

Hand-cards were used in pairs (one in each hand) to straighten the cotton or wool fibers in preparation for spinning. For each oblong board (4 by 9 inches) a piece of leather a little smaller than the board was cut. Holes were punched thickly into the leather by hand.

Through each pair of holes a fine wire staple was pushed and bent to a proper angle. After the wire teeth were all in place, the wired leather was tacked to the board. 

Before the leather covering was fastened to the board, a short handle was fitted and tacked to the board. This handle was just long enough for holding in the hand.

It was set at a comfortable angle, flat on the side grasped by the fingers, and rounded on the side next the palm of the hand.

Indies, because he would not use the poorly cleaned cotton of the 

American planters. Amos Whittemore took away the drudgery of making cards, and made possible the manufacture of cheaper and better carding machines. Samuel

Slater brought from England in his photographic mind the cotton machinery of England and gave it to America. Each contributed to the establishment of a new and important industry in our country.

1. Sawmills and Gristmills

Sawmills were used widely in the 

American colonies about thirty years earlier than in England.

Most of England's lumber was sawed in the colonies or in Holland. 

The great forests of America naturally called for sawmills. Lumbering was one of the earliest colonial industries. A mill to saw lumber and to grind grain was one of the first needs of a new settlement. Some of the colonists brought with them from England mills already framed, ready to be set

In the 1780's, James Rumsey began to make improvements in water-wheels for sawmills and gristmills. With one of his new inventions he could turn a water-wheel with one-twentieth of the water formerly required. In dry weather, this invention would be very useful. 

For only twenty guineas (about $100) this equipment could be built large enough to raise water enough to work six saws or one gristmill. One of Rumsey's mills near Philadelphia in 1796 ground and bolted flour, ground chocolate, mustard, snuff, hair-powder, and cut and pressed tobacco by water-power. 

Rumsey made a number of valuable improvements which cut the cost and increased the value of mills. He received exclusive rights for running his mills in several states. Toward the close of the 

Eighteenth Century, he used steam to raise the water to turn the millwheel. 

Another kind of sawmill and gristmill was the tide-mill. The mill was placed at the head of a tidal basin. The incoming tide swept into the basin, where the water was stored behind a dam. 

After the tide receded, the stored water was released as a waterfall to turn the mill wheel. Some mills were operated by wind-power, and several improvements of the windmill were invented in the colonies. 

2. Oliver Evans and his Mills

Oliver Evans was the great inventor of flouring-mill machinery. 

About 1780, when he was a twentyfive-year-old bridegroom, he went into the milling business with his brothers. brothers. In this business he had the chance to use his inventive ability. Within a short time he had made a great change in the mill. He invented the elevator, the conveyor, the hopperboy, the drill, and the descender. 

Every movement of the corn and meal, of the wheat and flour, from one part of the mill to another, from one machine to another, was performed by machinery. From the time the grain was emptied from the farmer's bag until the flour or meal was ready to pack, the labor was performed entirely by machinery driven by a waterwheel. Evans's machinery not only made a great saving in labor and cost, but it made better flour. 

After his machinery was installed in the famous Ellicott Mills near Baltimore, the savings were enormous. These mills manufactured 

325 barrels of flour daily. In labor alone, they saved $4,875 in one year. They gained fifty cents a barrel in the increased amount of manufactured flour. The gain in all amounted to $32,500 in one year.

In Pennsylvania, Along the Brandywine Creek were a number of fine mills. The

These stubborn millers refused to believe their own eyes. Evans liked to tell the story of how a party of these millers came one day to see his mills. He was in a near-by meadow making hay. He thought it would be a fine thing for them to see the mill running itself, and kept right on with his haying. The visitors found the cleaning, grinding, and bolting going on with no person in sight.

They saw that the mill could run with only one man in attendance part-time. They saw that one man could tend the mill with less effort than he could tend an old-styled mill with the help of two men and a boy. But they went home and told their fellow-millers that Oliver's machines were but a set of "rattle traps."

CHAPTER IX 

Inventions in Farm Implements Few labor-saving devices for the farmer were invented before 1800.

Colonial farm tools were little better than those used by the farmers of ancient times. Even the cart, with its two solid wooden wheels made of rounds cut from a hardwood log, was a luxury. Farmers who could not afford carts used crude drags or sleds drawn by oxen, horses, or men. The drag was simply a slender crotched tree with prongs seven or eight feet long. The trunk of the tree served as a sled-spire by which the drag or sled could be pulled over the ground. A box that would hold eight or ten bushels of grain was sometimes placed upon the drag.

The box was called a "car." The old drag plus the new box made a new invention. 

Farm tools were heavy and clumsy. They were poorly adapted to the purposes for which they were intended. Hand tools taxed the strength of men too much.

Machines that had to be pulled were too heavy for both man and beast.

The earliest colonial plow had a mouldboard that was made of a straight piece of wood. It was not heavy, but it worked with too much friction. Wood lacked the cutting power of iron. If the plow were shod with iron, the iron was sure to be rough and uneven. Το plow deeply, a second man had to bear down on the beam of the plow. Two men, using two or three horses or four or six oxen, could scratch the surface of one or two acres a day. Sometimes the mouldboard was roughly plated over with pieces of old tin, sheetiron, or sawplate. A wrought-iron plowshare was invented, but it was very clumsy. Only a very strong man could hold it in place by means of its upright handles, and it took twice the animal- or man-power to pull it that the modern plow now requires. 

The "bar-share" or "bull plough' was another form of plow used in colonial times. It had a wooden mouldboard clumsily fitted to a great pointed chunk of iron. This heavy, bungling implement was a poor tool. In the Southern and 

Middle Colonies the" 'shovel-plough" was in general use. Its beam was only a small roughly-hewn treetrunk. Into the beam was fitted a heavy stick. Upon the end of the stick was fitted a piece of iron, shaped something like a pointed shovel. Two rough handles were nailed or pinned to the sides of the beam and to the sides of the stick.

Plows were so scarce that they were used cooperatively, as we (62)

A man who owned a plow would go about from farm to farm, plowing the fields for his neighbors, who had only large and heavy hoes or mattocks with which to turn the soil. Some farmers received bounties from the town, if they would buy plows and keep them in repair. In 1636, there were only thirty plows in the whole 

Massachusetts Bay Colony. The

Pilgrims had no plows for twelve years after they came to America.

Even with iron plowshares, the colonial plows were not practical. 

The plowshare entered the ground at the wrong angle for easy cutting. 

A scientific mouldboard was invented by Thomas Jefferson in 

1793 and perfected in 1798. It was the first scientifically designed mouldboard. In 1797, Charles 

Newbold invented an iron plow.

The plowshare and mouldboard were made of one piece of castiron. It may have been less scientific than Jefferson's plow, but it was practical. It saved the labor of at least one man and one animal. 

It also turned a good furrow rapidly and easily; but the farmers refused to use Newbold's iron plow. It poisoned the soil, they said, and caused weeds to grow. They distrusted new-fangled contraptions for the cultivation of the soil. 

In 1760, George Washington invented a plow. In the spring of that year, he experimented with the "two-eyed plow" and the "duckbill plow." With the aid of his blacksmith he tried to make a plow by a new model, and partly by his own design. Finally, they gave it up. A few days later, he went to work on a plow entirely of his own invention. In a day or two, he tried it out and found that "she answered very well." new plow was a "one-eyed plow," a plow that had only one hole to receive a pin to hold it together. 

The next day, after trying out the new one-eyed plow, Washington made an exact duplicate of it, with two eyes instead of one.

About 1794, an important invention, called a cradle, appeared in the Middle Colonies. It was a broad scythe attached to a light wooden frame with four wooden fingers. The fingers corresponded in shape with the scythe, and were almost as long as the blade. With a deft turn to the left, a man could throw the grain in a swath, ready to be raked and bound into bundles. Four times as much grain could be reaped in a day with the cradle as could be cut with the sickle.
