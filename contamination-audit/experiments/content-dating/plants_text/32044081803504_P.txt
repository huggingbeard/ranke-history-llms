Socrates himself likens his use of questions to the work of the midwife³ bringing to the light of day the knowledge which lies hidden within men. Knowledge is the recollection of pre-existent truth. Now we may see the essential difference between the Socratic use of questions and the modern strictly catechetical method. A survey of medieval catechetical material shows that it would seem a gross misstatement to claim, as has been too glibly done, that Luther originated the modern catechism. 

It is undeniable that he gave to that particular form of booklet an impetus which probably none other could have given. But he had his precursors. He knew from Donatus and Bede and Alcuin how readily the form lent itself to pedagogical purposes. He saw that form adapted to religious instruction in the Bohemian Brethren's Kinderfragen. It needed but the touch of his peculiar genius to weld inextricably the form and the matter into one of the most widely distributed booklets of all times. 

The Reformers used the word catechism not as the name of a book, but as a title describing the contents of a book. The first two editions of Luther's booklet were entitled Enchiridion. It appears that Luther adopted the title of Althamer's Katechismus (1528) on later editions and this use of the word thus became standard. 

The church of the Middle Ages had ever considered it a duty to provide for the Christian education of the

young and the ignorant. For the churches of the Reformation, it became a necessity. Bulwarks along four fronts must be erected. First, against the mother church which had dominated all of life for a thousand years, holding in her hands the fate of men in time and in eternity. Little wonder that the numerically inferior Protestants found it necessary to establish their faith and practice on as firm a ground, logically and politically, as that held by the church of Aquinas!

Another enemy of the Reformed faith was the irreligion of the Renaissance. When beauty, or scholarship, or scientific accuracy takes possession of men's minds and wills, then all too often religion is rejected. Every church must define clearly the content and the grounds of its faith over against the irreligious enthusiasts.

And the religious enthusiasts constituted a third group of enemies: Ignorant innovators, fanatics, emotionally unstable men and women who sought satisfactions in a hundred aberrations. One of the cardinal convictions of the age was that right belief led to right living and wrongness of belief predicated wrongness of life. The atheist was immoral; the Anabaptist was antinomian; and every orthodoxy but one's own was under suspicion. 

A less tangible element-fear-should be mentioned last, demanding that all churchly adherents be able to justify their faith: The only half-conscious yet persistent dread of the Turkish invasions, which hung like a pall over the Eastern horizon, threatening the very downfall of civilization about the beginning of the sixteenth century; the widespread disillusionment and cynicism which followed the first glow of the Renaissance humanism and

optimism; the general anxiety and sense of insecurity caused by the wars and the deliberate campaigns of persecution throughout two centuries. These fears made the earthly foundations of life to show appalling fissures, and religious men and women clung to their faith in a changeless, timeless God.

Ein' feste Burg ist unser Gott has the ring of the elemental and the ethnic in it. It is more than a personal confession of faith. It is the cry of a whole people eager to live in the desperate staits of a shattered world. 

The symbols, therefore, of the several churches their creeds and their catechisms, or manuals for teaching the precious faith-were the most spontaneous and most inevitable products of the Reformation era.

The Puritan system of religious education was not limited to a transmissive preaching; nor was it restricted to what many would consider an unpedagogical recitation, however expressional catechizing may be; it included also personal communication and contacts between people of all ages. And these contacts between all members of the family and the officers of the church, taking place in the quiet and intimacy of the home, were the most effective means that the church might use for bringing up her young members in the knowledge and the fear of the Lord. 

It is by such contacts that character grows. It was under such influences that the justly renowned sturdiness of New England character was developed and perpetuated. All of life was shared by young and old: a full life, busy and immediately meaningful in a compact milieu; a life wherein every individual knew himself to be a living member. The close co-operation which resulted, between elder and younger, between all the forms of government, between all the varieties of interests, produced the atmosphere and the vital program of activity characteristic of the primitive, homogeneous community. Historically, such conditions always transmit and perpetuate, with but little loss, the sharply defined convictions and the elementally rugged habits which we call character. 

Dr. Richard Cabot says there are four things that "men live by": Love, Work, Play, and Worship.15 These phases of life among the New England Puritans were: Their families; their labors in agriculture, fishery, or manufacturing; their politics; and their church. These were the realms in which the social energies of these frontiersmen

The tissue of their social lives as human beings was woven in these simple patterns. In the best of their homes was mutual respect and love,—the two essentials by which personalities unfold and grow; their daily lives were so full of work that they needs must look upon all child's play as trifling, as a sinful waste of precious time;16 the business of self-government, according to English and very literal biblical traditions, furnished never-failing excitement; and their church offered education "in a howling

One of these events was the so-called "adoption" of the Westminster documents as the doctrinal "standards" of the church. In that day and age, all Christians believed that religious unbelief was the most serious problem and the greatest danger not only to the church but to all of society. The irreligious movements of thought in Europe were alarming. Bulwarks must be raised to protect the faith. But these defences should not be outside the church,-vested in the magistrature, for instance,-they should be in the hearts and minds and moral sinews of the church's leaders and pastors. Therefore, eighteen of the nineteen men assembled at Synod, in 1729, heartily concurred in passing "The Adopting Act," not as a bond of servitude to some external rule, but as a bond of union in a common faith and truth. The Records of the Synod of Philadelphia tell of the action taken September 17th 1729:6

All the ministers of this Synod now present, except one that declared himself not prepared, [here the names of eighteen ministers are given] after proposing all the scruples that any of them had to make against any articles and expressions in the Confession of Faith and Larger and Shorter Catechisms of the Assembly of Divines at Westminster, have unanimously agreed on the solution of those scruples, and in declaring the said Confession and Catechisms to be the confession of their faith [excepting only some clauses which define the civil magistrate's power to control and to enforce the exercise of religion].

3. Use of the Shorter CATECHISM 

Francis Makemie¹¹ wrote a catechism no copy of which is now known to be extant. Traveling from one sparsely settled area to another, maintaining only infrequent contacts with the families of his extended parish, he must make sure that his educational efforts reached as many as possible of his poor and often unlettered people. Thus he used, as did his contemporaries generally, the most approved method for leading minds and hearts to receive the truths of Christian salvation. The catechism was a social convention, an integral part of the Scottish heritage. 'According to the government and worship of the Church of Scotland": that is the phrase which repeatedly occurs in the record of actions of early American Presbyterianism.

Not the description of the hardships of these frontier settlers shall arrest our attention, but a few remarks will help to make clear why the religiously-minded people took the measures they did take. 

There were no towns west of the Allegheny Mountains. (Pittsburgh was not fully laid out until 1784). The small groups of cabins had but little intercourse, widely separated as they were from each other by tangled forests, untracked mountains, and unbridged streams. Occasionally, the people had to crowd together into a fort, whenever they heard of a coming attack by the Indians. 

Yet these people were socially inclined, and in spite of dangers and distances they sought help and comfort in communal tasks. It was they who created folk-lore and folk-ways peculiar to this new continent "at houseraisings, at huskings, at harvests, at flax-dressings. at wood-choppings, and at musters".45 They dealt with essentials only, with food, raiment, and shelter; with the elemental adverse forces of savages and animals, trees, and rocks, and streams; with physical prowess, disease, procreation, the traditional training of the young. Of advanced civilization and superior intelligence, here they were plunged into the most primitive environment. They therefore wasted no time on frills and finery. With knowledge of and longing for better things. these pioneers applied their rugged powers to their rude circumstances. The Home. 

In those days when, amid a poor people, there were only three or four textbooks in religion and ethics the hymn book, the Bible, the catechism, the primer these were the books upon which attention was directed; and the attention of pious folk was directed upon these books all the more intently because they were so few. With no occasions for the diffusing of attention by magazine stalls, billboards, lending libraries, daily newspapers, and the radio, the focusing of attention upon that which was at hand was all the more likely to produce deep and lasting impressions. The catechism had stood the test of time. It had (according to all the right-minded people) proved its worth. It had no rival. The Shorter Catechism had attained the rank of a classic. No good Presbyterian home would be without a copy.

As is to be expected, the ministers set the example for all their parishioners in the catechizing of their own families. We still have touching descriptions of these intimate scenes, as affection and study blended upon the hearth, of a Sabbath evening, and the priest of the household would take the very stones as a witness of his faithfulness.

The School. 

If the history of the educational policy of the Presbyterian denomination were written, it would distinguish

these several types of schools in the eighteenth century: (a) Private schools operated by individuals as a means of livelihood. (b) Schools maintained,officially by the church. (c) Charity schools instituted for the special benefit of underprivileged children. There seem to have been fewer schools of this kind among Presbyterians than of the other kinds. 

The "poor schools" are closely related to the origins of the Sunday School movement. (d) "Neighborhood schools" launched and run by voluntary associations of householders for their own children. This essentially democratic institution is as truly an original American product as is the town school of New England. It cut across traditional lines of language or religion, taking into consideration only the needs of the children. However, in the communities where the Presbyterians outnumbered other faiths, it was only natural that their principles would form part of the "curriculum".

However fragmentary the original evidence may be, yet we may safely echo the generalization of the historians: The Presbyterians did as surely use the Westminster

Shorter Catechism in their schools, as the Lutherans used theirs, and the Reformed churches and schools used the Heidelberg Catechism, and the Roman Catholic schools used the catechisms authorized by the several bishops. 

The Church Officers. 

The official representatives of the church (the teach-

ing and ruling elders) used the Shorter Catechism in several different ways. First, in the course of pastoral visitation. When the minister came calling it was no casual "dropping in". If he perforce came but rarely to visit, the occasion was a festive one, when very special preparations were made, as befitted the dignity of his office. On these occasions, he was expected to quiz the children on their catechism. 

A favorite practice was that of catechizing groups of families at one time. The parish was partitioned so that the minister (usually accompanied by one or two elders) could meet with all the families within a certain neighborhood at regular intervals. The value of this method is immediately evident: It provided the stimulation of mild rivalry; it furnished one more purposeful occasion for sociability; it perpetuated in the social esteem the objective of knowing one's catechism.

The Shorter Catechism was used in the pulpit, too. 

This was most natural, since the hearers were presumably even more familiar with it than with the Bible. From the viewpoint of the preacher, that audience is most stimulating which can receive his message with the most intelligent appreciation and responsiveness. That congregation is most depressing and distressing which has the least of these qualities. Intelligence, appreciation, response, these all predicate on the part of the congregation a certain fund of experience, a certain wealth of vocabulary, and certain habits of thinking. Though it be difficult to generalize on such a matter, yet the historian of western Pennsylvania should be given a hearing when he writes of the Presbyterian pioneers that they were strict observers

of the Sabbath, filling the time of their social intercourse on that day with no frivolous or worldly conversation, but with discussions concerning the sermons, the hymns, doctrinal subjects, experimental religion, the trials of the times. Indeed, he concludes, "we may venture to say never was there a Christian people more accustomed to religious conversation especially on the Sabbath, than were the early Western Presbyterians".57

In such an atmosphere, a preacher would have little struggle against spiritual torpor! But there were wise preachers in those days who did not have such congregations, and who therefore combined inspiration and instruction from the pulpit.

The Presbyterians of the eighteenth century firmly believed in an intelligent religion. They had no fear of a legitimate use of man's reasoning faculty. They uniformly insisted upon having an educated ministry. And a people educated in the things of religion was, they felt, always a desideratum. This is the eighteenth century's conviction in a nutshell: Knowledge safeguards against error, and early indoctrination is the best way to ensure knowledge.

The General Assembly.

In 1799, the General Assembly took action in regard to the religious instruction of the youth in all the churches.¹

Family Instruction. 

During these years, thoughtful people were deeply concerned over the apparent decrease of family religion. 

Many were the writers and preachers who voiced their distress at the neglect of that essential means of grace. 

Some rightly emphasized that it was a valuable tradition necessary for the increase of the church; some praised it for its character building effectiveness; some (Horace

[Singly and privately, each member of the family went to the home sanctuary which was called the "prayer room". Breakfast. Family worship. Meditation on the text announced for the day's preaching. Morning and afternoon worship services. At 

5 P.M., again family worship.]

An early tea then followed, to recruit the energies of the body; and no sooner was it over than all began to attend to the appropriate duties of the evening-the parents to peruse some of their favorite authors, and the children to learn the Assembly's Shorter Catechism. Between seven and eight o'clock the father commenced asking the questions, in the answering of which the mother, for the sake of example, as well as for her own improvement, joined with the children; and the whole of it was thus gone through every Sabbath evening. A few words, in the way of address from the father, usually closed this exercise; for, like Abraham, he ceased not to "command his children after him to keep the way of the Lord".

The Shorter Catechism, formerly used in almost every school in the States north of the Delaware River had been largely abandoned in the schools of Massachusetts even before the controversies of Horace Mann, who was Secretary of the Massachusetts State Board of Education from 1837 to 1849.68 Horace Mann wrote, in 1844: "In the nine eastern counties of the State, containing more than five-eights of its population, the teaching of the Assembly's Catechism and of Orthodox doctrines, had been, not entirely, but mainly discontinued, long before the existence of the Board. The Catechism had been objected to by the Orthodox Baptists themselves. In many places the discontinuance dates back, at least, to the beginning of the present century. I have met with many persons, educated in our schools, who never saw the Assembly's Catechism." 

In those areas also where Presbyterians predominated, the fact of the catechism's neglect was often noted with regret. But in the schools which they controlled, the catechism was regularly a requirement.

The Shorter Catechism in 1939. 

In the spring of 1939, three hundred and thirty questionnaires were sent to as many Presbyterian pastors, in an attempt to gather some typical statements concerning the use or the non-use of the Shorter Catechism throughout the country. The cooperation of friends in the Board of 

Christian Education was secured, who expressed their interest in the investigation and aided in choosing the Synods and Presbyteries most likely to respond to inquiry. 

One hundred and seventy-three replies were received. The figures are so small that it is impossible to consider them as representing actual conditions throughout the Presbyterian Church in the United States of America. But these replies do indicate several uses to which the catechism is still put, and a fair sampling of various reasons for not using it. The amount of repetition and overlapping in the returns seems to indicate that these are the typical, and probably the most frequent, reactions to the use of the Shorter Catechism among Presbyterians.

Probably one of the most interesting features of these returns derives from the negative responses. Here we find not merely the traditional criticisms leveled against the use of the catechism. We see aligned, face to face, two distinct ages of human experience three hundred years apart. The whole outlook on the world, life, and truth is different. Historical criticism, the theories of evolution pervading all realms of study, our conviction of knowing more accurately what human reactions are and mean, and the recent hope of "building" the Kingdom of God on earth, all have affected profoundly our current views of biblical interpretation. And, impatient with older creeds, we would write our own. For, trying to overleap three hundred years, it is not only that the words do not mean the same thing. Our minds understand the terms, but our hearts no longer respond to the vital meaningfulness of the truths. Our emphases, our hopes, our ideals sound notes unfamiliar to the ears of our forefathers. 

Yet the most important fact to be noted is, that the Shorter Catechism is still being used in the program of religious education of the Presbyterian Church in the United States of America. Many leaders in the church still find the catechism valuable as an instrument of instruction. It is compact, particularistic, highly technical: But these are traits the authors sought, and are not necessarily faults. It is still a fit guide to clear thinking and high thinking.

Which leads us to add that, where it is being used, the Shorter Catechism does not appear to dominate the field of attention but is made to serve in the subordinate capacity for which it was designed. It is rarely imposed

as a task for memorization, but is regarded as a summary of the church's traditional doctrine. In the hands of careful teachers, it is a starting point for discussion; it is means, not end; a tool and not a tyrant. 

Further, this recent investigation has confirmed that here, as in so many other realms, leadership is determinative. From several affirmative reports the clear implication is derived that the use of the Shorter Catechism would be a failure, or non-existent, without the enthusiasm either of the pastor or of some laymen. And it is right that not a book but a person should be the determining factor. For education is by influence. And Christian education is the communicating of life to life. 

A valiant battle was waged for the Shorter Catechism. 

By every historic appeal it has been advocated. Thousands of boys and girls even now are learning doctrine from it, in whole or in part. But, in spite of all efforts on its behalf, the Shorter Catechism (so the evidence says) has definitely declined from the position of almost exclusive honor that it once held.

99 

CONCLUSION 

Not only is the Shorter Catechism still valuable for teaching doctrine to adolescents and to older students, but as an example of noble thought it deserves the most careful study. If there is one thing this age needs, it is a reorientation in theology. And that demands clear thinking. 

One who knows has expressed himself vigorously to condemn the loose and turgid thinking in theology which he finds common today. Calvinism is "disproven" in a page by those who have never studied the Westminster Confession of Faith; "predestination" is ridiculed by those who have never opened Calvin's Institutes. One would have to go far to find a document which compared with the Shorter Catechism in conciseness, lucidity, and logic. 

Not only is it to be admired; it could advisedly be used as a model for renewed thinking, clear and close thinking, high and noble thinking, fearless thinking. 

The Shorter Catechism was once the classic textbook for religious education. The strength of its position was in the very fact that it stood unique. It focussed upon itself the attention of a whole people over a vast area and through a long history. It gained in prestige by the very concentration of this regard. But, since the decline of the 

Shorter Catechism, what has taken its place? With the multiplication of agencies, of programs, of courses, and of teaching helps, we have gained in quality, but we are suffering bewilderment from the quantity. There is no rallying point, as there used to be. 

Shall we seek a successor to the Shorter Catechism? 

Only another Westminster Assembly could write a book

that might satisfy us, endowing it with beauty, campactness, and prestige, and vesting it with authority. But could it? Indeed, do we need another book? We have books enough; valuable books, communicating the minds and hearts of the authors to the minds and hearts of the students. These books are and must continue to be means only.

Repeatedly, a new movement which started as a good thing has lapsed into formalism. In almost all cases, the lapse has been due to the substitution of means for ends. 

For it is difficult to keep the aim in view; and man concentrates his attention and endeavors upon the nearer tasks; and gradually he substitutes the methods for the too remote objectives. Thus it was that the catechism, revived by the Reformers to be the tool for a thorough religious education of all the people, the children and the ignorant as well as the learned, the catechism was substituted for the catechesis. Memorization became an end in itself. And when it was found that a strong Christian life did not result, there came to the assistance of the dilatory parent the revivalistic doctrine of irresistible grace, and the responsibility for leading the children to their heavenly Father was laid upon the divine shoulders. And the misuse of the catechism in Protestantism defeated the high hopes of the Reformers. 1 The Protestant church does not need to write another book and make it authoritative, "The letter killeth . . ." That is ever the danger. We have a Book, and a sufficient one, the Holy Bible. This Book, in its variety of editions and versions, is adaptable and available to all ages and to all conditions. The Bible remains 1

"the Book of Life" so long as the church seeks in it, and preaches from it, the themes of eternal reality, eternal truth, eternal life. This is the harder way. 

The easier way is to hand over to a hireling, or to an institution, or to a book, the responsibility for leading children's souls. And that way ends in delusion, intellectualism, formalism, death. 

The harder way is quite different from assigning tasks in a book. It is the contact of spirit with spirit. It means using all methods while never losing the aim from sight: -The aim of "life abundant" for each individual growing child. The harder way stresses not the methods but the using of them; less the procedure than the intelligence behind it; less the tool, the project, the book, and more "the love of God which is shed abroad in our hearts." The harder way is the way of the divine Teacher, who laid his life alongside the lives of a chosen few, trusting them. 

The harder way is the way God works, through personalities. He will not commit his message to a book only, but he commissions men and women, living souls, to touch other souls and lead them home.
