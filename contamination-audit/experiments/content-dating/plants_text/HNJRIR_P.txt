The way to the Mediterranean lies through the Strait of Gibraltar (controlled by Great Britain because it possesses the fortified Rock of Gibraltar); at the eastern end of the Mediterranean is the Suez Canal (likewise controlled by Great Britain), through which Ethiopia and the Far East communicate with southern Europe and northern Africa. Between the Strait and the Canal lies the Island of Malta, infinitesimal in size, compared with the European countries to the north and their possessions to the south.

Wherein, then, lies the importance of Malta, but a trifle larger than the District of Columbia in the United States of America?

There are three reasons for this importance. The first is that Malta, a highly fortified naval station, is a British colony; the second reason is that Malta, in the possession of Great Britain, lies between the Canal and the Strait like [ 5 ]

a sentinel guarding the way from the western entrance to the eastern exit of the Mediterranean Sea; the third reason is that the British Navy, still the best and most powerful of all navies and controlling from Malta as its base the Mediterranean, is thus in touch, as it were, with Italy, a friendly or hostile touch as Great Britain may determine.

Now, Italian vessels can not reach Ethiopia except through the Strait of Gibraltar, by way of the long route around the Cape of Good Hope to the Red Sea, or through the Suez Canal, by much the shorter route. But between the two gateways of Gibraltar and the Suez Canal stands the sentinel, Malta. 

Hence it is that the eye of the world is now turned towards the Island of Malta, and persons interested in foreign affairs are anxious to know something of the Malta of the past and of the present day. And hence 

Mr. Sumner Wood's pamphlet on Malta.

Preface 

As long ago as 1923 while I was a student at Harvard College, I picked the little known Island of Malta as being the most likely place on our globe for some future international trouble to break out; and because this tiny spot on the map of the world impressed me as being like a dormant volcano, I decided to amuse myself during life's idle hours by penning a word picture of this fortified 

English naval base.

College life and studies crowded the Malta idea into the background, and it was not until I started to practice law that I found sufficient time to devote to my hobby which I completed one year ago.

Since I had written this as a story for my own amusement, I, upon its completion, laid the manuscript away in my desk and paid little attention to Malta until the outbreak of the Italo-Ethiopian crisis when I resurrected the dusty typewritten pages and tried the account on several of my friends.

Malta continued to glow on the horizon of pending international conflict and I ventured to have it published because certainly no publisher would waste his time and money on the contents of this little pamphlet. 

I have in no way changed my manuscript nor have I attempted to bring it down to date, i. e., from September, 1934, when it was completed. With some little pride I call your attention to my prediction made more than one year ago, which you will find in the closing paragraph of this narrative. 

I apologize first to the Maltese race; next to Mr. Wil- [7]

liam Arthur Griffiths from whom I have stolen much material, and next to all who waste their time in reading my contribution to poor literature.

Now that I have anticipated criticism and taken the wind out of the sails of any destructive critics, I close this preface by warning all of my well-meaning friends not to become enthusiastic about this jumbled account because it really does not deserve any praise.

This booklet is not for sale but instead it is a gift from one who has enjoyed writing it, to my friends whom I trust will enjoy the twenty minutes that it will take them to read the thoughts which I have jotted down and thrown together on the international, historical and 

Christian aspects of the ancient island of honey.

MALTA 

Its International and Its Christian Aspects The Island of Malta, about seventeen miles long and eight miles wide (92 square miles or one-third larger than the District of Columbia-69 square miles), is fiftyeight miles due south of Sicily and about one hundred and eighty miles from the African Coast. 

Upon looking at the map one can immediately realize that its location in the most narrow part of the Mediterranean on the route from Gibraltar to Port Said makes of this piece of sunbaked land a vital strategic point from which all naval activity in this part of the world can be dominated. Its very position gives to it an international aspect. Its prosperity and the fate of its people have always been at the mercy of the nations who have ruled the sea. From the time of Tyre to the present date the fleets of mighty naval powers, the Greeks; the 

Carthaginians; the Romans; the Vandals; the Arabs, who, greatly aided by their language being closely related to the Maltese, ruled this island for about four hundred years, i. e., until about A. D. 1250; the Normans; the 

Turks; the Kingdoms of Sicily and Naples; the Austrians; the Spaniards; the English; the French and finally the English again have each in turn dominated the destiny of the little Maltese race.

Maps of Europe in A. D. 814, showing the Roman Em pire in the West at the death of Charlemagne; Europe 

A. D. 1519 showing the Holy Roman Empire at the death of Maximilian I; Europe A. D. 1811 at the height of 

Napoleon's power; Europe in 1815; and Europe at the present time, show this small island in a way which [ 9 ]

creates in one's mind an historical picture of the rôle which Malta has played in relation to Europe and the peace of the world. Maps showing Europe at different periods of time have the effect of producing a situation which can be likened to the changing designs and patterns which one sees when looking through a kaleidoscope, where different nations are represented by different colors, and in looking through the kaleidoscope at life on the Island of Malta, one would be particularly impressed by the frequency with which it during the past 2000 years has been forced to change its color. 

Malta is small in size but its influence has been far reaching. One writer once likened Malta to the Tower of Babel because during the World War there anchored in its harbors ships of many nations carrying English, Australian, Scot, Serbian, French, Hindu, Italian, Gurkha (note the vivid contrasts of human beings), Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, Montenegrin, Greek, Pathan, Cretan, Bengali, Annamite, Maori, Arab, Tonquinese, Moor, Egyptian, Bersaglieri, Tunisian, Senegalese, Congolese, Turk, Austrian and German; soldiers of war, all speaking different languages and each race of men taking back to its own corner of the world some lifelong impression of Malta's naval significance. 

Malta, as has already been pointed out, has been the scene of much trouble as well as popularity among nations wishing to control the Mediterranean Sea. It is about half way between Gibraltar (with an area of 1½ square miles) and the Suez Canal, and serves as a coaling and supply station for vessels. The old mariner speaks of Malta as the "half-way" point, and for that reason it is of great importance in the trade route between England and India (via the Suez Canal and Gibraltar). 

If one stands on the Coast of Malta, he can on a clear [ 10 ]

day with his naked eye see Africa, and at night nature brings refreshing relief from the day's heat in the form of cool breezes from snow-capped Mt. Etna only eighty miles to the north. This gives a perspective of how small the channel is between Malta and Sicily, and between 

Malta and Africa. A nation controlling Malta controls that part of the sea. It could be said without overestimating its importance that Malta strongly fortified and mined could almost separate the East from the West, so far as the Mediterranean Sea is concerned. The fact that Great Britain often has her fleet centralized in the Mediterranean Sea shows the value placed upon Malta, and later I shall point out its effect upon England's allowing Malta a constitution where England is the first "Roman Emperor" in this diocletion system of government. Malta then is of a great naval importance especially with England in relation to the Suez Canal and her direct route to India. Its international aspect is brought forth in bold relief when one ventures to predict that perhaps France might be secretly longing for a naval base near to her colonies in Africa, and Italy is not enthusiastic about having a British naval base so close to her "toe".

Many million years ago small coral insects gave to Malta its earthly beginning. A river of some unknown continent deposited clay without which no human being could survive because this layer of clay prevents the rain from sinking. Then came a layer of sand followed again by the earth-building process of the little coral insect. Mighty floods moved the earth, turning lakes into salt seas and from these many changes of the lithosphere emerged the Island of Malta. Man lived on this island long before it acquired its present shape. Prior to 5000 

B. C. mankind had reached a high state of development [ 11 ]

and left for an untold number of generations its marks on tables of stone, temples, villages, dolmens, menhirs, storage places and tombs. Scholars from all parts of the world visit this "halting place of many nations" and take home to their respective countries the naval significance of England's stubborn determination to control this strategic location.

The land under cultivation consisting of about 42,891 acres is walled up along rolling hills transforming the countryside into a series of terraces where thousands of milch-goats graze and the bulk of the population carry on the principal industry which is farming. The chief products are wheat, barley, potatoes and fruit. No other place on earth can you find such ease and contentment and I might venture to attribute their present state of happiness to the fact that ever since the advent of the Phoenicians the Maltese have been thrifty, industrious people leading a simple agricultural life with small landholdings and no large wealthy class to create social parasites by means of inheritance, that greatest of all capitalistic evils. Of course the principle of inheritance is recognized but the lack of abundant natural resources has prevented wild speculation and the selfish accumulation of wealth into the hands of a few people or corporations. 

Like the Phoenicians of old the Maltese are noted as mariners, merchants and fishermen. There are over three thousand persons employed in the fishing industry and the harbors are alive with more than one thousand fishing boats. While the men are busy farming, attending to milch-goats, fishing and working on the British naval docks, over five thousand women and children pursue the art, which can also be traced back to the Phoenicians, of making Maltese lace-a Malta product known throughout the world.. [ 12 ]

MALTA ITS INTERNATIONAL AND ITS CHRISTIAN ASPECTS 

The courtesy of the people and economic living conditions bring thousands of tourists to this little "paradise", and in this connection it may be permissible to venture the prediction that at least the male part of the tourist trade are attracted to the island by the beautiful Maltese women whose black eyes, silky hair and graceful carriage give a charm to the life of Malta which can rarely be found in any other part of the world. But 

Lord Byron apparently did not intend to compliment

Malta when he wrote that it is "a place of cursed steps", nor do the British sailors add any to its charm when they refer to it as a place of "bells, yells and smells"; however, I recently talked with an artist who had just completed a tour of fifty-one countries scattered throughout the world, and in his opinion Malta is today a "regular paradise" with a climate about the same as that of Georgia, and the people just as kind and courteous as they must have been in A. D. 60, when Saint Paul was shipwrecked on this island. The blueness of the romantic Mediterranean is not even surpassed by the beautiful color of the winding Charles River, which is a joy forever in the memory of every Harvard man. 

Valletta, its present capital with a population of over 25,000, ranks among the most important ports of call in the world. Nature's rock formation forms a part of the island's fortifications, the powerful significance of which can best be illustrated by turning back to history's rather terse account of how Jean de la Vallette, the founder of Valletta and Grand Master of the Order of the 

Knights of Malta, in 1569, with only eight thousand men defeated the Turkish forces of nearly thirty-four thousand men under Dragut. Today one can see fastened to the walls of "The Chapel of Bones" in Valletta, in a gruesome but architecturally attractive design, the bones [ 13 ]

and over two thousand human skulls of unfortunate human souls who were killed by the Turks during this sixteenth century siege of Malta and its little neighboring islands of Gozo, Comino and Cominotto. The total population in A. D. 1530 was less than 25,000, whereas today these Maltese islands have the crowded population of over 250,00 people.

In the grand harbor of Valletta one will always find a part of the British Mediterranean fleet at anchor, the international significance of which is most obvious, and perchance a little obnoxious and annoying to France: a definite hint to Italy. How sad and unfortunate it is to think that such a picturesque city should have such a bloody background, and that its present and its future have to be evaluated in relation to group murder which we dignify by that word war. 

Travelling inland about six miles west of Valletta we come to Citta Vecchia or Citta Notabile, which, at the dawn of the Christian era, was the fortified capital of the island and the seat of the Roman governor, or "Headsman of the Maltese". In 60 A. D. the island was a 

Roman "Rural District", but by 200 A. D. its capital city, which was then called Melite, was raised to the status of municipium, and ruled the island as its territory, in fact the whole island was once called "Melita". 

Prehistoric man gave to Malta a local habitat but apparently the Greeks gave to it a name, which was "Melita", meaning honey. The Arabs, who gained control of the island in the ninth century were lacking in imagination and simply called the ancient Roman capital city, Medina, which in Arabic means a town, and even today Notabile is popularly known by its Arabic name- Medina.

It may be well at this point to pause for a moment and [ 14 ]

make the mental observation that all which is contained in this writing springs from or touches and concerns a little piece of dry land, seventeen miles long and nine miles wide, only one-third larger than our Nation's capital. Your attention is directed to the fact that each paragraph has been constructed to tie Malta into the scheme of world thought. Sailors, soldiers, merchants, statesmen and scholars of every nation have learned some lesson from, or felt the influence of this strategic naval stronghold.

Edward Gibbon in his delightful history of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire refers to this spot in the great sea as the Rock of Malta, and while it is literally true he is also figuratively correct because this island in addition to braving the waves of the salty sea has for generations been battered by the rising and the declining waves of civilization. [ 15 ]

St. Paul's Shipwreck a Link Back to Christ

Upon the Rock of Gibraltar may stand the gory God of Mars, but from the Island of Malta above all strife and gruesome conflict shines out the peace giving light of Jesus Christ. No place in history and nowhere on earth can a Christian find such positive proof of the existence of Jesus as from the account of the shipwreck and teachings of St. Paul told by the people now living on this island. Down through the ages, passing from father to son for nearly eighty generations, has come the story of 

Paul's shipwreck in about 60 A. D. on this island. The harbor where he and his companions floated ashore has from time immemorial borne his name. No doubt many ships have been wrecked on this rocky coast and survivors floated into shore. All shipwrecks are emotion stirring events, remembered for years and even generations after their happening, but the shipwrecking of St. 

Paul about eighteen hundred and seventy-five years ago was so profound and soul rending that it has lived on to this very day. With your own ears you can hear living descendants of Malta Romans of 60 A. D. relate at great length how St. Paul was washed up upon their shore and lived for several months at Notabile which was the seat of the Roman governor whose residence has recently been excavated; how St. Paul cured the sick, restored sight to the blind and told their ancestors about the life of Christ. Among present Maltese people this tradition is so old and so positive that the truth of their stories. concerning the activities of St. Paul carries with it a ring of reality not easily scoffed at by the most sceptical. 

In addition we find the account of St. Paul's journey to Rome in Chapters 27 and 28 of the Acts. It is signifi- [ 16 ]

cant to note that Paul warned his centurion, before starting the voyage, that it would be one of much damage, not only to the ship but to many lives. The warning was not heeded, however, and the ship embarked for Rome. The 

Bible gives a detailed account of that tempestuous voyage. It is vivid in detail of the change of wind, the tossing of the ship, the struggle to avoid small islands, the throwing overboard of the tackling, and finally, after many days of storm, the abandonment of all hope. St.

Paul in this dark moment, although merely a prisoner aboard, cheered them with a message from God, saying that their lives would be saved but the ship lost. It was soon after this that land was sighted, and the ship, completely at the mercy of the seas, ran aground and was broken to pieces with the violence of the waves. Two hundred and seventy-six souls were cast ashore on the little island of Melita. The people there showed them "no little kindness". They kindled a fire, warmed and fed them. For three months they tarried on this island of honey, St. Paul teaching the people and performing miracles of healing the sick. In his account of the experience he repeatedly mentioned the kindness of the Maltese and their care of him. One can imagine the profound impression this man must have made upon these people. A man tossed ashore in a gale, who can heal the sick, cure the blind, is not easily forgotten.

Here in the Island of Malta not far from Rome and in the same vast Mediterranean Sea whose waters wash the shores of ancient Palestine there is a living legend which constitutes a most convincing link between us and the actual life of Jesus. It is just such virile legends as this that form the basis of all ancient history. In addition the doings of St. Paul in Malta as related in the Bible and by the Maltese dovetail together, and each in turn fits into [ 17 ]

the events narrated in the Roman history of the time as well as into archaeological excavations of today. Doubting Thomases, look to this Maltese heritage if you would see a monument to Jesus! He who gave to the world a new and vital concept of international peace. 

When you think of battleridden Malta do not lose sight of the burning light of peace which Jesus through his servant St. Paul kindled there long years ago. It is the peace of Jesus which surpasses all understanding, which answers the burning question as to why Malta should have been the focal point of so much international strife. 

Every past event is preserved to some extent by human repetition and many stories of old have lived in the human mind for generations before being reduced to writing. Paul's shipwreck was probably not recorded for nearly one hundred years and yet much of our ancient history has been handed down from generation to generation.

Let me analyze for you this tradition, because in it you will find a personal comfort, a renewed faith in Christ, and "faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." The mere fact that such a tradition persists in Malta after so many years is in itself reassuring, because often the printed page does not ring as true as an oral account of a local event. 

Forget for a moment that you are living among radios, automobiles and battleships of steel. Close your eyes to the light entering your room through panes of glass and with your eyes closed and your mind relaxed and set free from all your personal cares and troubles, let the wheels of time turn back in their flight and after a long, long journey there appears before your mind's eye a flat and rocky island. Crude huts of skins, logs and rocks are scattered about and you move unnoticed [ 18 ]

among the people. From the water's edge there comes a shout and all run down to the harbor to see. Far out on the stormy sea a tiny object comes slowly into view. 

Nearer and nearer to shore it comes. Giant waves toss it high into the air only to let it drop out of sight into deep troughs of water. The water beats against the rock throwing foam high into the air. Your clothes are wet by the rain and you stand there on the flat sandy beach not knowing that you are chilled. The distant object comes closer. "It is a ship," yells the man on your left. "Yes," says the young mother holding her baby close to her. "Look, it struck that rock," says someone.

The sea before you becomes calm for a moment and men swarming about among drifting timbers dash for shore. 

In a group, panting for breath, wringing wet, they all appear before your very eyes. For an instant all is still. You and they stand looking at one another in dreadful horror. Then some, overcome from the cold water and shock, drop to the ground, but out from the drenched little group calmly steps a man, well poised, perfect self-control. His mind is clear as he speaks and asks for firewood which when brought to him he lights and gathers his shipwrecked companions around him.

It has been months since you and your friends have talked to men coming from strange lands and during the next three months you never lose an opportunity to talk to these sturdy men or to be near to the one named Paul, he who was so composed when they landed. So like their leader, so gracious and so kind. You are at first horrified when it develops that this sage who has cured the sick and healed the blind among your midst is a prisoner being returned to Rome in the custody of a centurion. 

Yet this prisoner lifts up his voice unto the sky and prays and by his faith and his teachings about a strange for- [ 19 ]

eigner named Jesus Christ he fills your tired groping soul with a new joy, a new hope, an inspiration. Until

Paul's coming, life was just a matter of routine. 

The sea is again blue and calm. Ninety days have been as one. Paul leaves you and your friends and goes back to sea, safely headed for Rome. The ship at sea, the landing, the stories of a strange Saviour, that man—

Paul, all become as one great event, crowding everything else out of your imagination. Years pass, age creeps upon you, and grandchildren, and perhaps great-grandchildren go with you a countless number of times to the sacred spot where you stood that day in the rain and waited. Again and again you tell and retell the story to little fertile minds that live and absorb every detail. 

Does your recollection of that shipwreck upon your own native shore at the very spot where you paddled and played in the white crystal sand as a two-year-older need to be reduced to the printed page-cut into the rockfor you to keep from forgetting or to aid your children's memory? 

I now bring you back to your home in Maryland, Virginia, New York, or wherever you were before we went back to live in 60 A. D. on the semi-tropical island of 

Malta. Do you, yes, can you doubt the stories told today by those ancient's children's children of long ago 

Malta? Today they tell you that St. Paul while living on their island told their ancestors about Jesus Christ. 

With them it is a tradition, a part of their family history, but to Christians the world over it is mighty reassurance. 

One more illustration may clinch the thought and better acquaint you with the nature and the workings of your own memory. Let us take some single event which we all experience when we take our first ride on a railroad. [ 20 ]

Have you ever stood on the rear platform of a fast moving express train as it suddenly enters a tunnel? 

There is a terrific roar, a gust of wind. The beating of the car wheels on the rails sounds like heavy sledge hammers pounding on steel. Your ears ring; your eyes have to change their focus; your nose detects various odors and your nervous system quickly readjusts itself to the new order of things.

Then it dawns upon you that the entering of a tunnelopening for the first time is one of life's vivid experiences which records itself deeply into the recesses of your memory. You look up from the flying rails and beyond that the tunnel entrance has turned into a rapidly diminishing circle of light, while all around you there is nothing but walled up darkness broken every few minutes by the flicker of a red, yellow or green, and perhaps an occasional blue signal light. The circle of sunlight in the distance grows smaller as it appears to rush faster away until finally this distant light diminishes to a mere ray and then it becomes transformed into a recollection. The roar and clanging of the steel and what was a sudden gust of wind ceases to excite us. They too have vanished with the tunnel entrance of which they were a part. 

The tunnel entrance was more than a mere physical reality made of stone and cement-associated with it was noise, odor, air and a thousand jumbled mental reactions and observations. The same is true of any startling experience of life. The durability and accuracy of any event depends upon the degree and intensity of excitement with which it is attended. If such a trifling event as entering a railroad tunnel for the first time can create in our minds an impression which lasts for a lifetime, what would one expect of events such as the birth, life [ 21 ]

and death of Jesus Christ; or the shipwreck of St. Paul upon the Island of Malta?

They were physical events attended and followed by violent emotional disturbances, so intense and heart rending as to be removed from the realm of fantastic fiction and placed beyond the touch of exaggerating gossip. 

Today the people of Malta cherish their inheritance and revere their tradition, likewise do all Christians for on this storm-swept rock one finds an event which is as a mighty link in the chain of nearly 2000 years back through the tunnel of life to the reality of Jesus Christ. 

Hindsight is always less difficult than foresight. Socrates in his endeavor to pierce the veil of death and visualize the Immortality of the Soul was reasoning in terms of the future, but in seeking to establish the existence of Jesus Christ as a fact by means of the Malta shipwreck of St. Paul I am dealing with a form of "hindsight".

As great events cast their shadows before them so do they leave their marks behind in the form of what we may call human recollections. The life of Christ as well as the shipwrecking of St. Paul on the Island of Malta both passed into the realm of history upon the death of human recollection. Since history deals in past facts it becomes necessary to look behind history to the event itself in order that we may convince ourselves of the accuracy of the printed narration now found in the New 

Testament of our Holy Bible.

I am attempting to prove the existence of a man named Paul by means of establishing his shipwreck upon the Island of Malta as an unquestionable fact. If you agree that Paul was shipwrecked on the island, that he was a real and not an imaginary man then I have succeeded in leading you back through the tunnel of the ages to with- [ 22 ]

Local Language and Religion Take on International Aspect

It often happens that the governing of a colony by a nation like England, which gives to its own people so much political freedom, makes it necessary for that Mother Country to be equally liberal towards its colony. 

After England won the battle of the Nile, the Maltese attacked the powerful French garrison and with the help of the blockade maintained by the British fleet, the French, after two years, were forced by starvation to surrender. The Maltese hated the French; a few because 

Napoleon had wrecked the order of the Knights of Malta; others because he had confiscated the property of the Roman Catholic Church, and a vast majority disliked the French because of the governor whom Napoleon appointed to rule on behalf of the French Republic. This defeat of the French illustrated to England the importance of keeping the goodwill of the Maltese people and respecting their wishes, so, after the war was definitely ended, England had proposed to restore the island to the Order of St. John, but the people who had helped in the conquest of the Great Rock and who hated and feared the Knights of Malta because of their lavish living and abuse of their absolute power, were rewarded by being taken into the British Empire. Thus this Great Order, founded upon principles of fellowship, love and devout Christian mercy, built up and developed to great power by military valour and after three hundred years undermined by worldly riches was finally dealt a death blow by the Maltese people with the aid of their new ally in war and benefactor in peace. England had conquered the island and won the admiration of the people but the problem of [ 25 ]

local government remained unsolved. It merely shifted from the Knights of Malta to London. 

The level of intelligence in Malta was about as high as it was in England although the Maltese had practically no background of political freedom, and consequently were not educated in the art of self-government; also they were faced with a serious language handicap and lack of abundant natural resources. 

The blessing of peace which settled on the Island of Malta after 1815 turned the attention of the people from war to political freedom. The development of a native press and a local self-consciousness brought to the English Government the realization of the fact that the Maltese were becoming political-minded, and as years of peace went on there developed a popular demand for local self-government.

After many years of delay during which time there sprang up a considerable degree of popular resentment the British Parliament in 1921 sanctioned the granting of a charter or written constitution to Malta. Since England's chief interests in Malta were commercial and naval rather than the development of any natural resources, the people of the island were allowed to regulate their local problems, but England retained exclusive jurisdiction over the imperial naval, military and air affairs, and all matters of foreign policy, foreign trade, immigration, coinage and currency, treaties, etc. 

This constitution not only established a new form of government for two hundred and fifty-four thousand human beings, but it brought forth the philosophy of a diocletian crown and responsible republican government with a civil governor representing His Majesty, and controlling all matters that in any way affect the British Empire, and a nominated council in control of local af- [ 26 ]

fairs. Every law is to have the approval of the Senate, the Chamber of Deputies and the governor in the name of His Majesty. The governor has the absolute power to convene and dissolve the two chambers and also to exercise all executive power with the advice of the cabinet, which is composed of seven men whom he appoints upon the recommendation of the head of the majority.

This type of a local constitution would never work in our Philippines where local interests converge with imperial and where the element of American capitalistic exploitation has played such a leading rôle, but, although the constitution of Malta was a step forward in the hope of solving the question-How can nations govern their military colonies?-it has not proven successful. Apparently such innocent local problems as language, finance, religion and emigration fast became international in scope and brought England in conflict with the Vatican and with Italy.

The constitution of 1921 caused the Maltese to become more political-minded and with about twenty-four hundred people to the square mile there soon developed various political parties, some of which were little more than factions.

Nationality of people is made up of many elements; language, race, geography, history, customs, religion, literature, legislation and the conscience of the people, all of which have to be blended or properly balanced before a given group of people can govern themselves. 

In Malta some or all of these elements are colored by an Italian influence. Malta was inhabited and had a language of its own long before Rome was built and before Greece had a civilization, yet today the court pleadings are nearly always in Italian. The Labor Party violently opposed the use of the Italian language yet the Maltese [ 27 ]

are enormously handicapped in migrating to other parts of the Empire by their lack of knowledge of the English language, and it was partly for this reason that the constitution insisted that all children be taught the English language. Further the Maltese seldom emigrate to Italy and English is recognized as being the most useful language for any emigrant to know.

Sir Augustus Bartolo in testifying before the Great Britain Malta Royal Commission of 1931 stated that "The Catholics in Canada teach that union with the Empire is the best guaranty of their faith, whereas in Malta they teach that to be under England is to be under a Protestant country and to teach English is to help Protestant ideas.' 99 While the Italian language has been used in Malta since about 1300 or 1400 A. D. the Royal Commission of 

1911 reported that the language was seldom heard in ordinary conversation. Their literature is of Italian origin and a vast majority of all the people bear Italian surnames. Their customs are nearly the same as in Sicily and geographically the island probably belongs to Italy. 

But for the Maltese a solution of the language difficulties by the general knowledge of English is necessary to alleviate the complications due to over-population. 

During 1929 and 1930 the religious situation in Malta became very intense. The Holy See contended that certain men in power alienated from themselves the clergy and the people who had been "loyal to the British Crown, but also most tenacious to their Catholic Faith." England claimed that the Roman Catholic episcopal authorities of Malta and Goyo had refused to restore to the electorate of the colony complete freedom to exercise their political judgment. All of this grew out of the alleged failure by the Vatican to define the relations be- [ 28 ]

tween Church and State in the islands, which caused England to charge that the Pope in effect was claiming the right to interfere in the domestic politics of a British colony. To prevent threatened disturbances England authorized the governor to exercise his reserved powers under the Maltese Constitution and suspended the elections in the fortified Island which some claim to be the keystone of the British Empire. 

Many of Malta's political leaders contended in 1931 that the crisis was precipitated by the language issue. 

However, Dr. Mizzi contended that the cause of trouble in Malta was not the Italian language but the Roman Catholic Church and he advocated that since the Roman Catholic religion was by custom the religion of the Island elections on the Island should be parochial, and if this advice were followed the internal dissensions would cease and demagogy in politics would be eliminated. 

By 1932 self-government in the Island was again restored, but in 1933 the Governor of Malta under Malta Constitution Letters Patent dismissed the ministers and assumed full control of the Island, because of a grave emergency arising out of a violation of that part of the Constitution which confines the teaching of languages in the Elementary Schools to Maltese and English. There had been established, at public expense, special classes in 

Secondary Schools for children of Elementary School status and thereby teaching the Italian language to Maltese children, whereas Italian was to have been taught only in the Secondary Schools and not to Elementary School children. Yet 30,000 children attend the Elementary Schools but only a few go on to the Secondary Schools. The Maltese government had evaded the spirit and intent of the Constitution which was to stamp out the Italian language. [ 29 ]

This was contrary to the policy laid down by England in the summer of 1932 when responsible government was restored to the Maltese. Also the finances which were in a sound condition in 1932 had by 1933 become gravely prejudiced. The Maltese ministers among other things had been sending Elementary School teachers to Italy for training although the only languages which they could teach in these schools were Maltese and English. Thus the Governor, who is appointed by England, dissolved the Senate and the Legislative Assembly.

The governing of Malta has not been an easy task for the British, and because of the conflict with the Vatican and the Italian language controversy it is difficult to predict just what solution England will make of these two issues and what will be their ultimate effect upon the peace of England. Its Historical Picture 

The international importance of Malta can best be realized from a study of its history, and by means of the historical narrative which follows, I have tried to carry you quickly down through 29 centuries with the hope that its international significance will stand out above mere historical events and by connecting Malta to major world movements to leave one indelible impression on your mind: its strategical aspect which gives to Malta an international significance. 

The Phoenicians, who as early as 3000 B. C. founded city-states along a strip of coast about 200 miles in length between the country of the Philistines and the Hebrews and west of Mt. Lebanon, established about 1500 B. C. a carrying trade by sea. Malta's history dates from the visits of the Phoenician traders. In connection with this commerce by sea and in order to create markets for their surplus products they founded numerous colonies one of which was planted in Melite or Malta. Thus it was that the Phoenicians, who between 800 B. C. and 100 B. C., in acting as a great civilizing agency throughout the Mediterranean area, gave to the little island of Malta an alphabet and a touch of the west Asiatic material culture.

When Rome crumbled, this island passed from one roaming sea power to another until the Arabs took and held possession for about four hundred years. On the ruins of a Roman temple dedicated to Juno they built the fortress of St. Angelo to stand guard over the narrow entrance to Grand Harbor. The Arabs in 1090 paid tribute to Count Rogers of Normandy and probably left the island in 1250. [ 31 ]

Down through the years Malta felt the effect of the rising tides of progress enjoying for about three centuries the blessings of peace. New ideas were brought to its shores by the Crusaders on their way back home from the East. In 1526 Emperor Charles V of Spain, by making a gift of Malta to the Knights of Rhodes, which order of knighthood was thence called Knights of Malta, gave to Malta an ideal, a religious character, a heritage of the Knights of St. John, or Hospitalers; i. e., knights of the hospital of St. John in Jerusalem, founded by merchants from Amalfi in 1070 A. D.

Thus we find Malta benefiting from the civilizing influence of the Phoenicians and from the Crusades, which were the greatest events of the Middle Ages, and on rapidly turning the pages of world history we read of the capitulation and occupation on June 12, 1798, of

Malta by the Egyptian expedition of Napoleon Bonaparte, that master military strategist and seasick sailor.

In March of 1802 the Peace of Amiens was concluded between England who ruled the sea, and France who could not until 1815 be defeated on land. Here again the "half-way" point of the Mediterranean became a matter of international importance, in that France was to restore this island, history's "football" of nations, to the order of the Knights of Malta, but in 1803 new dissensions broke out between France and England, caused by the refusal to surrender Malta and by the weak Treaty of Amiens. Finally in 1814 England gained control of 

Malta, which acquisition was recognized by the (First) Peace of Paris, and later in 1815 the Congress of Vienna put an end to this ball of nations rolling down the hill of international conflict, and gave to England the Mediterranean vantage points of the Ionian Islands and Malta, which were soon to be used by England as a part of her [ 32 ]

world wide networks of naval, maritime, commercial ports and coaling stations. Nelson's battle of the Nile resulted in restoring England's dominance in the Mediterranean waters and the gigantic power of the whole British fleet was based on and anchored to no less a place than the little island of Malta.

It appears strikingly noticeable that England should go in 1815, to the Congress of Vienna having very little personal interest in the territorial reconstruction of Europe, but being most careful to retain this little ninety square mile piece of land as a guaranty for her naval supremacy in the mighty Mediterranean. For England, sea-power and trade have dominated her foreign policy and Malta is an important brick in the highway from England to India. The price which England paid in 

1815 to reach the zenith of her political and military prestige was industrial dislocation and social discontent at home.

After centuries of passing from nation to nation Malta has apparently for the time being come to rest in the iron hands of the British, and it is most significant to note that the British dependencies in Europe now consist of the Island of Malta and Gibraltar, the latter being a naval base commanding the entrance to the Mediterranean Sea from the Atlantic Ocean, the government of which is administered by a crown Governor who is Commander-in-Chief and exercises all functions of civil government and legislation.

Ten years ago I received a letter from James Harvey 

Robinson in which he branded the old saying "History repeats itself" as being a false proposition, but how can anyone look at the historical background of Malta without seeing that history has repeated itself in the past, and it is safe to predict that it will continue to repeat [ 33 ]

itself in the centuries to come. The historical theme of the Island, like the cold precise Bach "Fugue", keeps appearing and reappearing in accordance with definite principles of key relationship, first in one national cleft and then in another. Always it is the same old historical theme, that is, Malta's strategic naval position, and in the future it will burst forth again as one of the focal points of some international conflict. [ 34 ]
