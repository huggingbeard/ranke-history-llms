CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

GENERAL: The experiments in this manual have been selected to illustrate the principles of electrochemistry, both theoretical and applied. A thorough background in the use of the instruments for measuring amperage, voltage, and resistance is necessary before further study of the applications of electrochemistry to electrolysis, electroplating, electrolytic analysis, batteries, and electric furnaces can take place.

In many cases, the experiments should be run with duplicate determinations, since one can not rely too heavily on a single determination, because of the possibility of accidental mistakes. Close checks do not guarantee accuracy but if duplicate results do not check the experiment is obviously unsatisfactory.

For best results and economy of time the first few experiments require that the data be calculated and plotted, if necessary, coincidentally with the performance of the experiment. The student should get into the habit of bringing with him into the laboratory slide rule and useful reference texts. Both graph paper and ordinary paper, together with metric ruler, pencil, and eraser should be permanently kept in the desk. 

The accuracy of the data and results should be carefully evaluated and, when possible, the calculated results compared with the accepted values in the literature. The following tables or handbooks are recommended: "International Critical Tables" (McGraw- 

Hill Book Company, Inc., New York (1928-1929) ); Landalt-Börnsteins' "Physikalisch-Chemische Tabellen"; Van Nostrand's "Chemical Annual"; "Handbook of Physics and Chemistry" (Chemical Rubber Company, Akron, Ohio); and, "Handbook of Chemistry" (Handbook Publishers, Sandusky, Ohio (1939)).

Successful performance of most experiments will require the same careful attention to detail and cleanliness that is customary in other courses in chemistry. The top of the desk should be kept clean at all times. All spilled solutions should be washed up at once. The desk should be wiped free of dust at the start of the period in order to avoid soiling hands, apparatus, and clothing. 

It should be washed off with a sponge at the end of the period. 

Whenever water is needed for making solutions, distilled water should be used.

Efficient work requires that the student possess as part of his locker equipment many articles which are not included in the regulation list. It is suggested that the following be available: sever: large towels, a sponge, hand soap and (or) mechanics' soap, emery cloth or sandpaper of several differont grades, a spool of No. 16 copper wire, a 250 cc. graduate, a 50 cc. pipette, an additional supply of insulated wire, rubber tubing, glass tubing, etc.

CARE OF INSTRUMENTS: Because of their somewhat delicate construction, ammeters and voltmeters should be handled carefully at all times. When moving them from place to place, they should be laid down gently, avoiding unnecessary jars. In placing meters in a wiring diagram, it is helpful to remember that they may be looked upon as the mechanical analogue of an electrolytic cell, in which positive current must enter at the positive terminal (anode) and leave at the negative terminal (cathode). The terms positive and negative poles are reserved for and applied only to primary cells (those which generate an electromotive force). Positive current flows out of a primary cell from the positive pole, so that meter and secondary cell connections are always positive to positive and negative to negative. 1 Ammeters are distinguished from voltmeters by having solid metallic terminals, while voltmeters have rubber capped terminals. 

In assembling electrical apparatus, the following precautions should be observed: (1) An open switch should be included in the circuit next to one of the battery terminals. This is to be left open until the instructor has given his approval of the circuit, and permission to procede with the experiment. (2) A resistance of proper size and variety should be included in the circuit, set initially to its highest value. Before asking for rheostats from the stockroom, the current carrying capacity and maximum resistance that will be needed should be decided. (3) The ammeter is always placed in series with the resistance, and connected initially so as to read upon the ten-ampere scale. (4) The circuit must be so arranged that it is impossible to cut out enough resistance to damage the moters. 

The instruments which have been found most useful and versatile for measuring amperage and voltage are the D.C. Ammeter and Voltmeter, No. 280, made by the Weston Electric Instrument 

Corporation, Newark, New Jersey. The Ammoter has three scales reading 

10, 1.0, and 0.1 amperes; while the Voltmeter has three scales reading 

150, 15, and 3 volts. In every case it is always safest to start with the largest capacity of the meter and thus avoid "burning-out" of coils.

CHAPTER II METERS--THEIR CALIBRATION AND USE 

GENERAL: In practically all subsequent work the student will need a voltmeter and ammeter. In order to secure accuracy of results the meters should be calibrated. At times the resistances of the various meter scales must be known in order to apply corrections for their presence.

The common ammeter (Weston, No. 280) contains three scales: 

10, 1.0, and 0.1 amperes. The voltmeter (Weston, No. 280) also contains three scales: 150, 15, and 3 volts.

Experiment 1 Meter Calibration 

(a) Voltmeter Calibration. The students will connect their voltmeters in parallel across the bus bars upon which a voltage is impressed. The instructor using a voltmeter of known accuracy will impress increasing values of voltage across these bus bars so as to cover completely all three ranges of the voltmeter. The students shall record both impressed voltage and their voltmeter reading, These results shall be plotted with correction to be added to meter reading as abscissa against meter reading as ordinate (Figure 1). (b) Ammeter Calibration. The students shall connect their ammeters in series with one of known accuracy supplied by the instructor who shall allow increasing values of current to flow so as to calibrate all three ammeter ranges. The students shall record true amperage and their meter reading. Correction curves shall be plotted for each range.

By proper choice of coordinates all calibration curves may be plotted upon a single sheet of graph paper (Figure 2).

CHAPTER III CONDUCTORS OF THE FIRST CLASS 

Conductors of the first class are also known as metallic or electronic. An electric current is carried in them by a flow of electrons, producing no noticeable effect other than that of raising the temperature. These consist of the metals, alloys, and a few other substances such as carbon. There is no transfer of matter when the electric current passes through them.

-CHAPTER IV CONDUCTORS OF THE SECOND CLASS 

GENERAL: In conductors of the second class, the electric current is carried by the flow, not of electrons but of electrically charged atoms or groups of atoms called ions. This type of conduction is electrolytic or ionic. Cations are positively charged and more toward the negative electrode or cathode. Anions are negatively charged and more toward the positive electrode or anode. As examples of electrolytic conductors, one may list: (a) certain solid salts, such as AgI, AgNO3, SiH, and NaCl; (b) fused salts in general; (c) solutions of acids, bases and salts in water and other solvents. 

Faraday in 1834 investigated this conduction and concluded that, when a current traverses a solution or conductor of the second class, there is an actual transfer of matter, one portion traveling in one direction and the other portion in the opposite direction. 

When the current passes between the solution and the electrodes, reactions take place such as gas evolution, metal doposition, etc. 

This process he called electrolysis. The amount of electrolysis was found to be always proportional to the quantity of electricity passed; and the amounts of substances liberated or dissolved at the electrodes were always proportional to their chemical equivalents. 

For every electron supplied at the cathode, one electron is removed at the anode, so it is evident that the same amount of olectrochemical action must occur at each electrode. Passage of the curront depends, however, on the cations passing toward the cathode and on the anions going toward the anode. The amount of electricity carried by each is determined by their relative velocities.

GENERAL: If two electrodes are placed in a solution of an electrolyte and a current of electricity is passed, certain electrochemical reactions will occur at the electrodes. These reactions constitute a process which is called electrolysis. 

If E is the impressed voltage, R is the resistance of the electrolyte, and I is the current flowing, it will be found that I does not equal E/R but is less. The electrochemical reactions at the electrodes have made the electrolytic cell into a voltaic cell with a "back E.M.F.", E'. This back or counter E.M.F. is called the polarization E.M.F. The true relation between current, voltage and resistance is:

I = CI 1 E' / R The amount or size of the polarization is a function of many factors, such as: (a) the concentration of the solution; (b) the tomperaturo of the solution; (c) the stirring or mixing of the solution; (d) the type of chemical reactions at the electrodes; (e) the nature of the electrodos; (f) the size of the electrodes; and, (g) the curront density at the electrodes. A study of these factors indicates that polarization effects can be divided into two classes: (a) those due to chemical changes on or at the electrodes, chemical polarization; and (b) those duc to local depletion around the electrodes, concentration polarization.

Polarization phenomena are of great inportance in industrial applications of electrochemistry. It increases the voltage requirad in the operation of electrolytic cells. It changes the naturo of metallic dopo sits in electrodeposition. It decreases the voltage of primary or secondary voltaic cells. During charge of a storage battery, polarization raises the voltage required; during discharge, polarization reduces the voltage obtainable from the battery.

CHAPTER VI ELECTRODE POTENTIALS AND THE IR MEASUREMENT 

GENERAL: When a metallic electrode is immersed in water, there is a tendency for the metal atoms to become ions and go into solution, leaving their electrons on the metallic electrode, so that it becomes negatively charged. This tendency is called by Nernst the electrolytic solution pressure. If the water contains ions of the particular metal, e.g. zinc electrode in a zinc sulphate solution, there is another force which tends to force zinc ions from solution onto the metal giving it a positive charge. The magnitude of these two forces varies from metal to metal, so that the electrode may be: (a) negatively charged with respect to the solution; (b) positively charged; or (c) not charged at all.

When an electrode dips into a solution of its own ions, a potential difference is set up between the electrode and the solution.. 

This is called the single electrode potential but can not be measured directly as there is no complete circuit. To make this measurement, the electrode in question is combined with another one (whose single electrode potential is known) and the o.m.f. of the complete cell is measured.

According to Nernst's theory, the magnitude of the single electrode potential is a function of the two opposing forces, the electrolytic solution pressure of the electrode and the osmotic pressure of the ions in the solution.

The calomel cell is one of a number of half cells or standard electrodes which possess the ability to maintain a constant potential difference between the first and second class conductors of which they are composed. CHAPTER VII DISCHARGE POTENTIALS AND OVERVOLTAGE GENERAL: In the study of decomposition potentials 

(Experiment 12 and 15), it was found that the experimental results for a sodium chloride solution using graphite electrodes did not check the data given by LeBlanc for a similar solution, but using platinum electrodes. Further experiments would have shown that the decomposition potential varied in accordance with the electrodes used.'

The potential which an electrode must attain in order that a particular substance shall be evolved or deposited upon it has been termed the discharge potential of that substance.. Evidently, the decomposition potential of a solution is the sum of two discharge potentials, e.g. the decomposition potential of a normal hydrochloric acid solution would be the sum of the discharge potential of hydrogen on the cathode and of chlorine on the anode.: The reason why different electrodes give different results in the same electrolyte depends on the ease of discharge on that particular electrode. The deposition of hydrogen at the cathode is of importance both theoretically and practically. At an olectrode which is reversible to hydrogen (platinized platinum) hydrogen gas will be liberated at the potential given by the Nernst equation: 

RT nF 1080 10g。 P/P However, at an electrode which is not réversible to hydrogen, this potential will be increased by an amount equivalent to the energy required to liberate the hydrogen on this electrode. The Nernst equation becomes:

E = RT loge P/P + EH, where EH represents the increased potential or hydrogen overvoltage. 

The difference between the potential necessary to liberate hydrogen on an electrode which is not reversible and on one which is reversible in the same solution is known as the hydrogen overvoltage at that electrode. 

Overvoltage depends on a number of factors, some of which are poorly understood. The literaturo often adds to the confusion because of the difficulty of duplicating results with consequent conflicting values. The following facts on hydrogen overvoltage seem to be more or less accepted. (a) Increase in current density increases overvoltage. (b) Increase in temperature decreases the overvoltage. (c) Increase in total pressure on the system generally decroasos the overvoltage. (d) Overvoltage increases with time, although very rapidly in some cases. (e) Superimposition of alternating current on the direct current may decrease the overvoltage.

Many theories have been advanced to explain overvoltage of hydrogen on the cathode. Among these are: (a) the hydride theory, assuming that hydrogen dissolves to an appreciable extent in the electrodo forming some sort of an alloy; (b) the film theory, assuming that the hydrogen forms a film on the electrode surface and that the overvoltage represents the potential drop through this film; (c) the catalytic theory, in which the increased potential is due to the good or bad effect of the electrode to catalyze tho change from atomic hydrogen, H, to molècular hydrogen, Ha; and, (a) the quantum theory, based on a change in the work function for the doposition of hydrogen.

Just as there is a hydrogen overvoltage at the cathode material, there are also oxygen overvoltages and halogen overvoltages on anode materials. There is also definite indication of metal overvoltage, caused by retardation of crystal growth in metal deposition.

Overvoltage is of considerable industrial importance. 

For example, from the single electrode potentials, one would expect hydrogen to be plated out in preference to zinc from a weak acid solution. However, the high hydrogen overvoltage on zinc added to the equilibrium potential of hydrogen brings the hydrogen deposition voltage above the zinc plating voltage. Overvoltage also necessitates the expenditure of more energy in processes involving the liberation of gases.

It is also an important factor in determining the corrosion of metals, the reducing power of different cathodes, and the oxidizing power of anodes.

CHAPTER IX CORROSION 

GENERAL: For many years, the corrosion of metals has received much attention. It is becoming of greater economic importance each year to prevent the corrosion of iron and steel. It has been estimated that the annual replacement due to corrosion may reach as high as two per cent of the total tonnage in use. When one considers that over one billion tons of ferrous metals are in use in the world, the loss due to corrosion is tremendous. 

Corrosion may be broadly defined as the chemical action of the environment on metals, so that undor ordinary conditions of exposure, the products of corrosion consist mainly of oxides (mostly hydrated), carbonates and sulfides. Recent work has indicated that essentially the phenomena of corrosion are the same for all metals and alloys.

CHAPTER X ELECTROKINETIC PHENOMENA The GENERAL: Electrokinetics is the study of electrical phenomena which are due to or influenced by surface adsorption. term "electrocapillary" has frequently included all electrical effects due to adsorption, but its use is generally limited to potentials observed at the surface of a liquid as the surface tension is altered. 

Electrokinetic phenomena are those which arise from matter in motion (hence the name electrokinetic). The latter may be classified as 

(1) Electrophoresis, the migration of suspended solid, liquid, or gaseous colloidal particles under the influence of an external electromotive force; (2) Electro-osmosis, the migration of liquid past a solid surface under the influence of an external electromotive force; (3) Streaming potential, the potential produced by the flow of liquid past a solid surface; (4) Sedimentation potential, the potential produced by particles which are moved mechanically, as by the force of gravity. 

Although these phenomena were discovered as early as 1809 by Reuss, it was not until 1879 that Helmholtz developed a mathematical theory of electrokinetic phenomena. In order to explain these phenomena, Helmholtz made use of the concept of an electric double layer at the surface of the colloidal particle (see Figure 26). 

He assumed a layer of charges to exist in a thin film of the dispersion medium which was fixed to the colloidal particle, and immediately adjacent to this fixed layer of charges, he assumed a movable layer of charges of opposite sign contained in the dispersion medium. 

Where these two phases come into contact, an interfacial potential is produced at the boundary. Helmholtz believed that the distance between the positive and negative layers was of the order of atomic dimensions and that the potential fell abruptly at the interface. 

It must be realized that the net charge on any colloidal solution is zero; the charges in the fixed layer are exactly neutralized by the charges in the solution. Solid +44

This is true of aqueous solutions and most solid substances. A useful guide, known as Coehn's Rule, states + that "a substance having a high dielectric constant is positively charged when in contact with another substance having a lower dielectric constant." The negative charge on the wall causes the formation of a diffuse zone adjoining it which contains an excess of positive ions, in the form of a mobile annular ring or sheath around the body of fluid in the capillary.

If a potential is applied at the two ends of the tube, it is obvious that all positive ions will start to travel towards the cathode, and all negative ions towards the anode with velocities in the neighborhood of 0.5 mm. per min. per volt/cm. This is the phenomenon of electro-osmosis. In the center of the capillary, where equivalent numbers of both kinds exist, they interfere with each other so evenly that the aqueous medium in which they are moving is subjected to no resultant force in either direction. But in the diffuse double layer where an excess of one kind is prosent, a resultant force is generated which tends to carry the surrounding medium along in the direction of travel of the most numerous ion. If the ends of the tube are under no hydrostatic pressure or equal hydrostatic pressure, the viscous pull of the moving sheath or skin of fluid at the wall imparts motion towards the negative pole of the whole column of fluid.

It is obvious that when this tractive effort of the double layer at the wall is resisted by no hydrostatic pressure, a tremendous number of liquid molecules will flow from the end of the capillary for every pair of ions discharged at the electrodes. Under the best conditions, one thousand molecules of water are moved thru a capillary for every molecule of water electrolysed into hydrogen and oxygen. 

Thompson says that if a given amount of coal were used to generate electricity by a steam engine, and the electricity then used to remove water from a wet solid by electro-osmosis, it would remove forty-five times as much as if the heat energy of the coal were used directly for removing water by evaporation.

The reason for the development of streaming potential is easily seen from the discussion of Fig. 29. When the diffuse annular double layer is made to move relative to the oppositely charged stationary layer adsorbed on the wall, an excess of positive ions is at once discharged from the exit end of the tube. - In electro-phoresis, colloidal particles of a liquid, gas, or solid migrate in a definite direction and with a definite velocity if an external electromotive force is applied to the solution. This is true even though the dispersed particles are formed from neutral substances. 

It is reasonable to expect that if an applied electromotive force can cause electrophoresis, the motion of a colloidal particle in the absence of an applied electromotive force will produce a potential. Thus, sedimentation potentials are observed whenever grains of sand or crushed mineral are allowed to fall by the force of gravity through a liquid, and are detected as potential differences between the extreme ends of the liquid column. 

Although at present the importance of electrokinetic phenomena is properly realized only in the field of colloid chemistry, it seems probable that this commercial utilization may be profitable in some of the following places: (1) Dehydration or removal of water by electro-phorosis from muds, pulps, peat, etc.; (2) Electrical tanning, to increase the speed of penetration of tannin into hides; (3) Electrical lubrication, for drawing water out of clay to lubricate brick machinery; and, (4) Deposition of rubber, by precipitation of latex on the anode.

CHAPTER XII CONDUCTOMETRIC TITRATION AND ANALYSIS GENERAL: Electrolytic conductivity measurements furnish an excellent method for analysis of solutions of known constituents but unknown concentrations. It is necessary only that the relation between conductivity and concentration of the solution be known; it. is also important that no other substance present affect the conductivity, unless allowance can be made for it. 

The simplest case would be one in which only one salt was present and whose concentration could be plotted directly against conductivity. Even when two salts are present, if they are closely related the relative amounts of each present may be calculated from the fact that the conductivity of the solution of the mixture is the arithmetical mean of the conductivities of each salt. 

The end-point in titrations can also be found by conductivity of the solution. The conductivity of the solution is dependent both upon the concentration and the mobility of the ions present. Any change in either of these factors produces a change in the conductivity of the solution. In the neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base, the conductivity of the solution will decrease as the highly mobile hydrogon ions combine with the hydroxyl ions forming water and are thus replaced by the less mobile cations of the base. Since the salt is ionized to the same degree as the acid, the conductivity will decrease until all the hydrogen ions are replaced. As soon as the end-point is reached, the conductivity increases due to the prosence of the fast-moving hydroxyl ions.

An important advantage of conductometric titrations is that hydrolysis, solubility, or dissociation of the product does not materially influence the accuracy. It is used, therefore, where other methods fail or where colored or turbid solutions would obscure the end-point. It must be noted that large amounts of foreign electrolytes which do not entor the reaction will affect the results greatly.

Conductometric titrations and conductivity measurements can be used as a quick means of control in many industrial processes. 

The progress of a reaction, the concentration of an electrolyte, the dogree of acidity or alkalinity of a solution can be determined in this way.

Uses of conductivity measurements for industrial control may be classified as follows: (1) Water (a) Purity of distilled water. (b) Concentration of chlorine in tap water. (c) Examination of boiler feed water. (a) Extent of carbonation.

CHAPTER XIV DETERMINATION OF HYDROGEN ION CONCENTRATION 

GENERAL: For many reactions it has been found that the concentration of the hydrogen ion itself is generally of more importance than the actual acid concentration. Its importance in all kinds of industrial, inorganic, biological and physiological processes has made its measurement of great practical value. For example, in many instances rate of corrosion, rate of flocculation of precipitates, and conductivities of solutions are functions of the hydrogen ion concentration. And it can be determined much more accurately in dilute solutions than can the acidity by usual titration methods.

For many purposes it has been found more desirable to express the normality of the hydrogen ion concentration as the nogative logarithm to the base 10. Sorenson proposed this terminology in 1909 and called this negative logarithm the hydrogenion exponent and gave it the symbol pH. Accordingly, the relationship between hydrogen ion concentration, CH, and hydrogen-ion exponent, pH, is: 1 pH = 10810 CH ===3 10810 From this, it can be seen that a hydrogen ion normality of 1.0 would give a pH of 0; one of 0.1 would have a pH of 1.0; one of 0.01 would have a pH of 2.0, and so on.

Many materials when found in solution exert a buffer action on the pH value. For example, if a liter of pure water with a pH of 7.0 is treated with 1 milliliter of 0.01 N HCl, the pH will change to approximately 5.0 since there is nothing to retard the ionization of the acid. However, if both acetic acid and sodium acetate are present, very little change in pH will be observod. The HCl has reacted with the sodium acetato to form acetic acid, which is only slightly dissociated into hydrogen ions and acetate ions, so that there is little change in the pH. Other materials, such as carbonates, phosphates, borates, etc. also act as buffers over different parts of the pH scale.

CHAPTER XV ELECTROPLATING AND ELECTROFORMING 

GENERAL: Electroplating processes are used for covering metallic or non-metallic articles with a metal coating. There are four main reasons for electroplating: (1) to improve the appearance of the surface; (2) to produce a corrosion-resistant surface; (3) to replace worn or broken parts; and, (4) to form a surface that resists mechanical wear. Electroforming consists of the formation of articles by electrodeposition, such as electrotypes. This is sometimes called galvanoplasty.

The plating equipment consists of generating equipment as a source of D.C. current; rheostats to control the amount of current; electrical measuring instruments; tanks to hold the work, usually of wood, sometimes lined with asphalt, sheet lead, or rubber; and often some form of agitation. This is a relatively simple set- 

17. For some industries, such as automobile parts, some form of continuous plating equipment is more economical and efficient. 

In all types of electroplating, it is necessary that the base metal be clean to give good adherence of the plate and an impervious coating. There are two kinds of foreign materials: (i) oxides, rust, scale or tarnish; and, (2) grease or oil and dirt. 

Oxides, rust or scale can be removed from metals by: (1) mechanical abrasion, sand blasting, wire brushes, buffing with abrasives or in tumbling barrels with steel shot or emery; and, (2) by acid pickling, using either sulfuric or hydrochloric acids of suitable strength for iron or steel articles; and a mixture of sulfuric and nitric acids for brass articles. 

Grease or oil can be removed by: (1) the use of organic solvents to dissolve the film of grease; (2) the use of caustic solutions of Na2CO3, Na,PO to saponify and emulsify it; and, (3) the use of electrolytic hydrogen, produced by making the article the cathode in an alkaline solution, using a current density of 30 to 

35 amperes per square foot.

The smoothness and appearance of the plated article are determined by the nature of the deposit. Many factors enter into this. Among the most important of these factors are: (1) Current density. An optimum value of current density exists for each metal plated. (2) Temperature. Temperature aids in controlling the size of the metal crystals. Hot baths increase the size of the deposited crystals objectionably, although the higher temperature aids by increasing the solubility of the constituents of the bath. (3) Metal ion concentration. A low metal ion concentration gives powdery deposits. The higher the concentration, the denser the deposit. (4) The pH of the bath. A low pH, or high acid concentration, tends to yield finor crystals and a brighter plate.

The throwing power of the bath is a measure of the relative rate of metal deposition on parts of the cathode which are at different distances from the anode.

Of great importance in good plating is the solubility of the anode which furnishos the metal ions to replenish those plated out on the cathode. For certain metals such as nickel, chlorides are added to the bath to increase anode corrosion and prevent passivity.

Electroplates are usually tested for adhesion, corrosion resistance, permeability, thickness, and hardness. These tests are many and varied. The corrosion resistance may be tested by a rapid salt spray test, and permeability by the modified "ferroxyl" roagent. Hardness can be determined by the Brinell or diamond scratch method.

Electroforming may be defined as the production or reproduction of articles by electrodeposition. It includes electrotyping, the reproduction of printer's type, engravings, and metals; the reproduction of phonograph records; and the manufacture of seamless tubing. For phonograph recordings, the original wax record is covered with graphite upon which copper is deposited to form the negative master matrix. From this several positive master matrixes are formed by electrodeposition. Seamless tubing is produced by electrodeposition on a revolving mandrel which serves as the cathode.

Experiment 49 - Study of An Electroplating Bath to Determine Optimum Conditions of Operation 

The bath prepared in Exp. 48 is used for plating the motal strips prepared in Exp. 47, and the various factors for optimum operation are determined. To investigate the effect of varying current density and temperature most efficiently, it is suggested that as many as four baths be placed in a metal trough supported upon a ring stand to serve the purpose of a water bath. CHAPTER XVI ELECTROWINNING AND ELECTROREFINING 

GENERAL: In many cases, electrowinning or electrometallurgy can successfully compete on a commercial scale with pyrometallurgy. 

Especially when the ores are of low grade, leaching and electrolysis of the leach solutions to recover the metal is often much cheaper then obtaining the metal through concentrating the ores and smelting. 

In the electrowinning of copper, ores containing as little as 1.5 to 2.0 per cent of copper are treated by a leach solution which becomes the electrolyte for electrolytic precipitation. The stripped electrolyte is then returned to the leaching plant for further use on fresh ores. The recovery is from 80 to 90 per cent of the total copper in the ore. In general the ores are leached with a H2SO4 - Fe2(SO4)3 solution which dissolves the copper from the mixed oxide and sulphide ores. The leach solution is electrolyzed in concrete or lead-lined tanks, using insoluble anodes of 8 per cent antimonial lead and copper cathodes. At current densities of 11 to 

12 amperes per square foot, current efficiencies of 65 per cent at the cathode are usual.

This calcine is leached with a sulphuric acid solution, which is the spent electrolyte from the electrolysis tanks. 

It usually contains about 120 grams of H2SO4 per liter. The leach solution is electrolyzed in load lined or rubber lined tanks, at current densities of 30 to 40 amperos per square foot. The cathodes are alunicum sheets and the anodes are pure lead or a lead alloy.

CHAPTER XVIII ELECTRIC FURNACES AND ELECTROTHERMICS GENERAL: The importance of electrical heating, electric furnaces, and of electrothermic reactions was demonstrated by Davy in 1800 many years before there was enough electric power to make any of the processes commercially feasible. In 1839, Hare operated a small electric furnace and produced calcium carbide and graphite, using battery current. It was 1878, however, before Siemens designed and built the first commercial electric furnace. 

Electrical heating has many uses to which combustion heating can not be profitably applied. It can be used to develop higher temperatures and greater cleanliness, and is more easily applied at the point of usage. No air is needed and "stack losses" are, therefore, eliminated. The control of the furnace is easily made automatic and it is thus more easily operated. Electric heat has a higher relative efficiency than combustion equipment and it offers more comfortable, healthful, and pleasant working conditions. 

Electric furnaces can be divided into three classes: (1) Arc furnaces; (2) resistance furnaces; and, (3) induction furnaces.

An electric furnace consists of the following essential parts and accessories: (1) Some Conducting Material - A vapor from an arc or a resistor, either solid or liquid. (2) A Refractory Confining Material - The furnace must be enclosed by a refactory to hold the charge and retain the heat. 

(3) Conductors to Bring Current into Furnace - These conductors are electrodes, usually of graphite or carbon rods, with water cooled electrode holders. Induction furnaces need no conductors since the heat is generated by passage of current through the charge itself, acting as the secondary of a transformer. The elcctrodes are usually separately formed and baked; however, recently there has been devised an electrode, known as the Soderberg electrode, which is formed as it is consumed and is thus self-baking. 

(4) Source of Electric Current 

The current is produced by a dynamo or generator. It may be A.C. or D.C., and be produced by a steam or by a hydraulic power plant. 

(5) Regulating Devices The electrodes must be moved in 

In addition, or out of the furnace to maintain the current constant. auxiliary transformers are needed to change the voltage whenever necessary. These regulating devices are often made automatic. (6) Meters and Cables Meters are used for measuring amperage, voltage, or wattage used. The cables are often stranded wire of a size capable of carrying all necessary current. GN 

(7) Charging and Discharging Equipment -The electric furnaces are either intermittent or continuous in operation and must have facilities for charging or discharging them accordingly. 

Refractories for electric furnaces consist of bricks, often specially shaped, and granular packed materials. They are classed as acid, basic, or neutral. Good refractories should be infusible at the temperature of the furnace, should be unattacked by the action of the metallic slags in the furnace, should retain the heat of the furnace, should have a low coefficient of expansion to avoid cracking or spalling, and should be strong enough to withstand the mechanical abrasion of the charge. The chief acid refractories are fire-clay and silica. They are moderately priced, are very refractory, and are quite strong. They can be used up to temperatures of 1600° C. Magnesite and dolomite are the chief basic refractories. 

They are very refractory but are not as strong or as cheap as silica. 

They can be used up to temperatures of 2000° C. Chromite, silicon carbide, and fused alumina are classed as neutral refractories. 

They are very strong and highly refractive materials but are somewhat expensive. They can be used at temperatures up to 2000° C. also. 

Carbon or graphite is the best refractory known, never having been melted, but it will volatilize around 3600° C. However, it can be used only in a roducing atmosphere or it will readily burn. 

Electric furnaces have been chiefly used in metallurgy in the refining of steel and in the production of special alloy steels and ferroalloys. When no other means is available for producing the necessary high temperatures, electric furnaces must be used. Thus calcium carbide can be made from lime and carbon at the high temperature of the electric arc:

CaO + 3C CaCz + CO, (1500° C.) 

This process was first developed in 1892 by Wilson in America and 

Moissan in France. Silicon carbide or "Carborundum" is produced when silica is reduced by carbon at high temperatures:

SiO2 + 30 

Sic + CO, (2000° C.)

This was discovered accidentally by Acheson in 1891 when he attempted to make diamonds by dissolving carbon in molten clay. Silicon carbide has great value as an abrasive and as a refractory. Boron carbide was reported by Ridgway in 1934. It was made from boric acid and coke, similarly to silicon carbide, at 2500 to 2600° C. It is especially hard and as an abrasive has been substituted for diamond dust. Graphite is produced commercially by the Acheson process in electric furnaces in which the charge furnishes the resistor. According to the present theory, carbides are formed by the interaction of amorphous carbon with metallic oxides. Those carbides then break down to give graphite. The charge is run at a temperature of 2200° C. 

The electric furnace is used also for fusing of various raw materials. When bauxite is fused, "Alundum" is formed, which is extensively used as an abrasive and refractory. The furnace temperature is 2000 to 2200° C. Mullite is a highly refractory material of the average composition 3 Al203.2SiO2. It is formed by fusing an alumino silicate above 1545° C. The conversion is represented by the following equation: 3(Al2O3. SiO2) → 3 Al2O3.2SiO2 + SiO2. 

Fused quartz is made in both clear and opaque forms. It is used for special laboratory ware, and for transmission of ultra-violet light. Pure silica sand is fused and worked at 2000° C. to give opaque ware. For clear ware, pure clean quartz crystals are selected and fused at about 1800° C.

Experiment 65 - The Production of Aluminum Bronze 

The indirect arc furnace (Fig. 49, A) is charged with a mixture of 90 parts by weight of copper scrap and 10 parts by weight of aluminum scrap.

The current flowing through a resistance furnace is nearly proportional to the applied voltage. In an arc furnace, however, the current does not depend directly on the applied voltage but may even increase when the voltage is decreasing. This is due to the fact that the resistance of an arc becomes less as it increases in cross-section. A minimum of 25 to 35 volts is needed to start the arc; often the voltage is greater than this, depending on the spacing of the electrodes. 

The arc is started and the aluminum bronze melted. Tempera tures of the arc and of the molten metal are measured with the optical pyrometer. Voltage and amperage readings are taken every 10 minutes.

The bronze is cast into small sand or chilled cast iron molds. 

How does the indirect arc operate? How does it compare with the direct arc? What is the production of aluminum bronze per kw.-hr.?
