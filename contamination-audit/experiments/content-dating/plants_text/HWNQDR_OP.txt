Chapter I  1. Personality and Power. A man's educa tion, it has been said, is the sum total of the great personalities he has met. If this be true, and there are many evidences that it is, it can easily be realized how tremendously important personality is. It is what all men desire, and rightly so, for it is the magic key that unlocks all doors, the open sesame to all success. 

But what is personality? Like the word "charm," like the word "success," it is a word to which we attach many meanings. 

The thing is easier to recognize than to define. But for our purpose here, we may explain personality as the sum of those qualities which make us likable or unlikable. 

Thus we say a man has a good personality if his qualities-appearance, moods, habits, ways of thinking, attitudes toward other people-win people to like him; if he leaves people indifferent, or if they do not notice him, we say he has a negative or a neutral personality; if he "rubs people the wrong

way," or arouses hostility even without trying to do so or being conscious of it, we say he has a bad personality.

Think of the people whom you consider to be successes in your community. Are not most of them people with good personality, people whom other people like? Your family doctor, your lawyer, your banker, the teacher who influences you most-are they not as often preferred because of their likable personalities as because of their knowledge or ability? Sometimes, of course, an unlikable person forces his way to the front, but we usually think he would have gone even further if he had had a good personality. 

Truly, personality is power. 

2. . Did you ever talk with a stranger over the telephone, and form a conception of his character from the sound of his voice and his manner of speaking? Are there radio announcers whom you have never seen but whom you like or dislike from their speech? Of course you will say yes, for it is an experience we have all had, and one from which we can realize that speech is an important part of personality. 

Going back to our definition of personality, we observe that speech is one of the qualities

or abilities that help to make people like or dislike us. And yet in listening to the stranger over the telephone, or in listening to the radio announcer whom you cannot see, you do not get all the speech of the speaker; you get only the audible portion of it. Speech is more than the sound of words; it is in addition the posture of the body, the expression of the face, the glance of the eye. 

If then you are moved to like or dislike a person merely from hearing him when you cannot see him, how much is his speech likely to influence you when, as normally, the sound of the words is reinforced by the visible appearance of the whole person. A little reflection will convince you that speech is, indeed, the most important single factor of personality, for it is almost the sole means by which you communicate with your fellows. 

3. Speech and Speech Making. It might be well at the very begining to get clear one idea concerning speech. Freqently when a student hears the word speech, he thinks of public speaking, of platform addresses. But we have been using the word speech, in connection with the relation of speech and personality, in a much broader sense. We mean

all spoken language, your conversation in your home and with your friends, your replying to questions in the recitation period at school, your talking over the telephone.

All that we have said about the importance of speech applies to these every-day forms of speech even more, for the average person, than to the platform speech, for everybody uses some of these forms of speech every day; and many people will not be called upon to make public addresses during their whole lives. Bear in mind, then, throughout this discussion, except in the chapters dealing with platform speaking, when we say speech, we mean all your use of spoken language. 

4. Habits of Speech that Mar Personality.

It might be well before we discuss the technique of speech to call attention to some of the more common bad habits of speech that mar personality, as well as to some of the good habits of speech that we should cultivate. The listing of these habits will form a sort of chart to guide us in our speech development.

The habit of avoiding speech, from fear, or laziness in thinking; being too laconic. 

Talking too much; and especially,

thoughtlessly. 

Use of slovenly enunciation. Stammering. 

Speaking with a bad voice; especially, monotone. 

Expressionless speaking; lack of vivacity, animation, enthusiasm. 

Out-of-the-window speaking; fear of the auditor's eye. 

Use of sloppy sentences: the "and-andand" habit; the "you know" habit. 

Formal and affected speaking. 

5. Habits of Speech that Strengthen Personality. The list of bad habits of speech which mar personality will naturally suggest their opposities which should be cultivated. 

A few positive good qualities of speech that should be striven for are the following:

Spontaneity and ease in speaking. 

Clear but unaffected enunciation. 

Pleasing voice. 

Self-confidence and directness. 

Vivacity and enthusiasm. 

Good use of language. 

6. Bad Habits of Speech. To make our selves clear, we should offer a few words of

explanation in regard to the qualities of speech which we have listed in the foregoing two paragraphs. 

7. The Clam. We listed first among the undesirable habits of speech the habit of silence. Perhaps you know students who have that habit, who hang silent on the outskirts of every conversation, who never recite unless called by name, and then as briefly as possible. Perhaps you have the habit yourself. Analyze the cause of the habit. Is it fear to expose one's ignorance in the presence of wiser people? Is it native shyness? 

Is it mental laziness, the avoidance of the labor of putting thought into sentences?

Whatever it is, strive to overcome it. Don't be a clam. You can only learn to speak by speaking. Next 

8. The Windbag, the Rattlebrain. we listed the habit of talking too much, and especially, of talking thoughtlessly. Perhaps more of us are guilty of this bad habit. It is a part of our love of showing off. Avoid it like poison, for nothing more quickly earns the dislike of our fellows. Learn to listen, as well as to talk. The other fellow likes to have his share of the conversation. Especially avoid the habit of merely “rattling

on." Don't be a windbag, a bore, or a rattlebrain. 

9. The Lazy Tongue. The use of slovenly enunciation is a peculiarly American habit of speech. All of us are guilty of it at one time or another, and some of us all the time. 

Learn to sense the beauty of clearly and properly spoken words, and you will be on the way to overcoming slovenly by enunciation. "Wod-juh say" will then cease to represent "What did you say?” and “Did-juh” will not do service for "Did you?" Bad enunciation comes generally from haste, lip-laziness, or mere copying the bad habits of others.

Develop hobbies, friends, enthusiasms, and your speech will begin to take on vitality. 

13. The Far-away Eye. Out-of-the-window speaking is the characteristic of the person who is chronically shy, who hates to look people in the eye. Overcome that shyness and develop self-confidence, and you will be on the way to overcoming this bad habit. 

Remember that no speech "registers" which is not direct, and nothing helps in directness more than looking into your auditor's eye.

14. Sloppy Language. Sloppy sentences and weak and colorless diction go hand in hand to mar the effectiveness of speech. 

Does that mean that we should always use full sentences? By no means. Good idiomatic English is often marked by the use of detached words, phrases, clauses, even broken sentences. But when full sentences are used, they should be correct in construction. 

Beware the habit of stringing sentences one after another by the connective "and." Beware the "also" habit in beginning sentences. Beware monotony of construction; vary the pattern. So far as diction is concerned, learn the value of the pungent, the salty, the aromatic words of the common people. No diction is ever good which does not draw constantly from the sluices sluices of current speech. 

15. Speech on Stilts. A final habit of speech, one often affected by those who seek to be correct, is the habit of affectation, of over-meticulous nicety. It manifests itself sometimes in pronunciation tion, sometimes in stilted and and enuncialanguage and imagery. Don't make your little fishes talk like great whales, as it was said that Samuel Johnson did.

16. Speech Beautiful. It will not be necessary at this point to expatiate upon the good habits of speech already listed as desirable for cultivation as aids to personality. They are doubtless sufficiently clear in meaning to require no explanation; moreover, the methods by which they may be acquired will be discussed in subsequent chapters. But one idea should be insisted upon, which, if firmly held, will inevitably lead to the acquisition of all good habits of speech. That is, that speech is not only a means of communication; it may also be an adornment to the personality. It is not only an organ of utility; it may be an agent of beauty of the highest order. Get this concept of Speech Beautiful, and you will never be satisfied with a rasping voice, a lazy tongue, or an inert brain that shows itself in muddy sentences and sloppy diction. Your speech should be a glorious ornament to your personality.

Chapter II  

There is a story told from the Persian which illustrates that the beauty of the human voice has long been recognized. According to the story, a teacher of the Koran was one day in the temple reading aloud from the sacred book in a monotonous and strident voice. Not a soul was listening. A priest happened along and asked him, "Why are you reading from the Koran?" "For the glory of Allah," was the reply. "Then for the glory of Allah, do not read in that voice," admonished the priest, "for you give Allah no glory when you read in such a manner that none will listen." 

What the priest said with respect to the voice in reading is equally true, of course, with respect to the speaking voice. If you want attention, you should develop a voice that is at least tolerable, and if possible, beautiful. 

1. How the Voice is Produced. We learn from the study of the science of physics that any sound must originate in some vi-

brating body or membrane. The sound is carried through the atmosphere as a series of waves, or alternate condensations and rarefactions of the air molecules. In the case of the human voice, the vibrating membrane from which the sound proceeds is the two vocal cords which are stretched across the interior of the larynx. A very intricate system of muscles move the vocal cords in several ways: bringing them together, or moving them apart, tightening or loosening them. The action of the cords as moved by these muscles and by the column of air forced up through the cords from the lungs, produces the vibrations that are heard as the voice. When there is no phonation (voice production), the cords are relaxed and wide apart; the air of our breathing passes through them without producing vibrations. When they are drawn together, they vibrate, and voice is produced. The tighter they are drawn by the muscles, the higher is the pitch of the voice. The more violently they are agitated by the escaping air, the louder (more intense) is the voice. 

Pitch and intensity are two of the physical elements of the voice. A third physical element, quality, depends partly upon the

fact that the cords vibrate not only as parts. The vibrations of the parts of the cords produce overtones, and the number and nature of the overtones affect the quality of the voice. The quality of the voice is also affected by the physical condition of the cords and the surrounding cartilages and tissues of the larynx: It is thought also that the quality of the voice is affected by the sympathetic vibration of three columns of air, that of the aesophagus; that of the mouth cavity; and that of the nasal passages. Certainly we know that when the nasal passages, for instance, are closed by the mucus discharge of a cold, the quality of the voice is greatly affected.

The fourth vocal element which we shall discuss, time or rhythm, depends not upon the physical production of the voice, but upon the psychological effect upon the hearer of the rate and succession of voice sounds. 

2. Voice Quality. It will be clear from what has been said that the basic quality of the voice is in part something over which we have no control. Nature gives us vocal cords of a certain texture and length, a larynx of a certain size and shape, and these factors influence the quality of the voice, and we can-

not change them. But the study of voice production reveals that there are factors which we can control. The emotional state we happen to be in at the time of voice production affects the texture and shape of the larynx, the aesophagus, etc. When we are frightened, there is a tensing of the muscles of the throat, and we can hear the effect in a rasping and tremulous voice. We can learn to control our emotional states, and so affect the quality of our voices. If you want a light and pleasing voice, relax; if you want a vibrant and commanding voice, develop the emotion of courage, of the desire to dominate. 

3. Developing the Voice. In addition to your ability to influence your voice quality by controlling your emotional states, there is no doubt at all that by appropriate exercises you can strengthen your breath control, which will affect the dynamics (volume or loudness) of your voice; you can widen the pitch range; and most important, you can improve the quality of the voice. The first step is to learn to hear your own voice, so that you are conscious when it is pleasant in quality, when unpleasant. Strive to keep the quality clear and resonant at all

times. Practice deep and regular breathing. 

Finally, practice daily reading aloud, preferably from poetry of a musical quality and predominance of open vowel sounds. The poetry of Tennyson offers many excellent selections; examples are "The Lady of Sha lott," the Garden Song from “Maud"

Poe's poetry offers good examples for developing quality; read "Annabelle Lee," "Ulalume," "The Bells.' You can extend your own list, and add to it from your favorite poets. Be sure to read aloud, varying the pitch and volume, and read regularly. Get the utmost of resonance into your voice. You can develop the resonance of your voice by the practice of humming tunes regularly. 99 

4. Kinds of Voice Quality. What has been said thus far with reference to voice quality concerned the normal or basic quality of the voice of any person. In addition to the use of the word quality in this sense, it is also used to designate types of voice appropriate to the communication of different emotional states in reading, acting, or speaking. That is, every person has a certain basic quality of voice for ordinary speaking; and in addition, he may in expressing varying emotions use other differ-

ent qualities of voice. Usually eight such qualities are designated, with the accompanying emotional states as follows:

Normal quality; used generally in unemotional speaking. It is ordinary voice of intellectual speaking. 

Orotund; a deep-pitched, vibrant quality, suitable for the expression of awe, grandeur. Read Byron's "Apostrope to the Ocean" in orotund.

Pectoral; also deep pitched, but hollow, suggestive of deep awe, reverence; Jacob's words in Ex. 28:16-17 are read in pectoral.

Guttural; a low-pitched, broken quality expressive of anger and rage. 

Aspirate; either a whispered, unvoiced quality, or a voiced quality suggesting a whisper; used for secrecy.

Nasal; the unpleasant, high-pitched quality of one who talks "through his nose"; no particular emotional content; used in dialect and character protrayal. 

Falsetto; a very high-pitched, thin voice, above the normal register; used in character portrayal. 

Oral; a weak, thin voice of middle range, expressive of physical weakness, sickness, low vitality. 

5. Voice Pitch. Under pitch, we have to distinguish between range and melody. By range is meant the highness or lowness of the pitch, and particularly the extent over which the voice passes in going from the highest note used to the lowest note used in speaking. By melody is meant the pattern formed by the succession of speech notes

used in speaking. Listen to one speaking whose voice ranges up and down the scale, not arbitrarily, but according to the thought and emotion expressed, with upword turns (inflections) on questions and incomplete ideas; with a tendency toward high pitch in excitement and hilarity, and low pitch in solemnity and reverence; if the effect is pleasing, we say the speaker has a good speech melody. 

6. Monopitch. One of the commonest faults in speech melody is the fault of monopitch (often wrongly called monotone); that is, staying on or nearly the same pitch level. Nothing is more devastatingly dreary than monopitch. To overcome it, practice reading or speaking at different pitch levels, at first arbitrarily, to accustom your voice to changes of pitch. Then let the voice range up and down, until you have broken up the habit of monopitch. 

7. Singsong. Another common fault of speech melody is the fault of singsong (party also faulty rhythm.) It consists generally in alternating between two, and only two, different pitch levels. Overcome it with the same exercises as for monopitch.

8. Other Faults of Melody. Another very common fault of melody, to which public speakers are especially liable, is the habit of using repeated falling inflections at each pause in the sentence. They should be used only when there is a completion, or partial completion of thought. Where the thought is suspended or incomplete, there should be a rising inflection. Take for instance the first sentence of the Gettysburg Address: 

"Fourscore and seven years ago, our fathers brought forth upon this continent new nation, conceived in liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal." Many readers would read it with falling inflections on "score," "ago," "fathers," "continent," "nation," "liberty," "proposition," "men," "equal." In proper reading, falling inflections should be used only on "nation" and "equal." The effect of this excessive use of falling inflections is a deadly monotony that puts the audience to sleep. This habit of excessive use of falling inflections is usually coupled with another bad habit, that has to do with time rather than pitch, but which may be spoken or here; and that is, the habit of grouping the words ar-

bitrarily according to recurrent rhythmic groups, rather than according to the sense. 

This recurrent grouping also contributes to monotony. Break up one habit, and you usually break up the other. 

Another fault of melody is the fault, likewise common to public speakers, of repeating monotonously, at regular intervals, a favorite phrase of melody, which may be correct in itself, but which becomes tiresome from repetition. Speakers who use a declamatory style of speaking, rather than conversational melody, are liable to this fault. If you can learn to think always as you are speaking, rather than speaking from memory, you will be able, with the assistance of a critic, to overcome this melody habit. 

9. Force. One of the physical elements of voice of vast importance, but which can be treated in short space; is the element of force. It has to do with loudness, volume, or intensity of the voice. Its importance can be judged by the statement of a prominent teacher of speech in one of the universities. 

In naming four objects to be striven for in a beginning college course in speech, he names as one of them "the development of the abil-

ity to speak loudly enough to be easily heard." The writer has for years attended chapel services in an auditorium of rather bad acoustics, where many famous speakers are brought to address the students. Dozens of them fail to "get over" because they do not speak loudly enough. 

10. Proper Breathing. Behind the proper use of force, there must be proper breathing. Cultivate the habit of breathing deeply, with full use of the diaphragm. A wellknown professor of speech in one of our universitoes is called by his students "the bellyman" because of his harping upon the importance of the abdominal muscles in breathing. 

11. Voice Projection. In addition to proper breathing, you must cultivate the ability to project the voice properly. Keep your head up and your mouth well open; a closed mouth muffles your speech sounds. If you are making a public address, speak to those on the last rows. Those on the forward rows will hear you, if those in the rear do. 

12. Proportion in Force. While it is necessary to insist vigorously upon the importance of speaking always loudly enough

to be easily heard, a word of caution is necessary. Occasionally one meets with a person who offends by speaking too loudly. 

Make yourself heard, but don't roar like the bull of Bashan. If you are making a public address, proportion your force to the size of the room in which you are speaking. It is just as bad to speak too loudly in a small room as to speak too quietly in a large chamber. Particularly is it important not to speak too loudly when carrying on a conversation with one or only a few people. A quiet, well-modulated voice is generally more pleasing than a booming voice. 

13. The Time Element. We have dealt with the three physical elements of voice, quality, pitch, and force. A fourth vocal element, time, has to do not so much with the physical way in which voice is produced, as with the way in which the flow of voice is broken up into segments, so to speak, by the action of the lips, tongue, jaws, and teeth. These "segments" (syllables) may be long or short in time duration; they may follow slowly or rapidly one after the other. 

14. Rate. There are several terms which designate divisions of the element time which we should distinguish. The first

is rate. By rate we mean the rapidity or slowness with which the speaker utters words or syllables. Rate depends partly upon the habit of the speaker, partly upon the thought or emotional content of the thing spoken. Some speakers habitually speak like a whirlwind; at the other extreme is the speaker who drawls. The mean between the extremely rapid and the extremely slow rate is generally preferable. The speaker whose enunication is sharp and clear can speak faster. Generally beginning students in speech have to be cautioned to slow down rather than to speed up. They often tend to speak too fast from a nervous haste to get done; the result is poor enunication and the impression of lack of poise. Go slowly enough to create the impression that you are master of your time; not so slowly as to lag behind the thinking of your hearer or hearers.

We said that rate depends partly upon the thought or emotion of the thing expressed. Especially impressive thoughts have to be delivered more slowly. In narratives where there is rapid action, the rate of speaking speeds up. Usually the speaker who is thoroughly in the mood of the ideas

expressed varies his rate subconsciously. 

15. Quantity. A second division of the time element is quantity. By quantity is meant the duration of a single syllable. The accented syllables of the words that carry the leading ideas of the sentence are generally lengthened in speaking. 

16. Pause. An extremely important division of time is pause. By pause we mean intentional cessation of the voice to mark relations in meaning, or to give emphasis. 

Pause to mark relations in meaning generally follows punctuation. That is, we pause where in writing we would have commas, colons, semicolons, dashes. Pause for the sake of emphasis is even more important. It is an extremely common fault of the young student in speech, for him, like Antony, to "only speak right on," with no attention to the relative importance of ideas expressed. 

Make your speech, not a flatland of level monotony, but a land of mountain peaks and hollows; put relief into your speaking, and there is no means more effective than pause. 

Pause after your important ideas, to let your hearer grasp their significance. Pause between the major divisions in your thought so that your hearer may observe the prog-

ress of your thought. 

17. Rhythm. A final division of the time element is rhythm. Sometimes the term is used to cover the whole time element. In the narrower sense it designates the regular recurrence of stressed and unstressed, or of long and short syllables. You are familiar, of course, with rhythm in poetry, but probably you have never thought of rhythm in connection with prose. Το see the importance of rhythm in prose, take, for instance, Lincoln's "Gettysburg Address." Take any sentence in it, and substitute words of more or fewer syllables, and with different accents, and observe how frequently part of the beauty of the passage is marred. For example: "Eighty-seven years ago our fathers brought forth upon this continent, etc." Do you like the effect?

One can gradually develop a sense of rhythm by listening to speakers who have it, and by reading speeches of the great orators who were masters of it. Read the speeches of Lincoln, Webster, and especially of Burke. In your own speaking, try to avoid the habit of hesitating, stopping abruptly, speaking in sudden jerks, all of

Try to which mar the rhythm. achieve smoothness and fluency. This does not mean that you should neglect what has been said about pause. Proper use of pause will contribute to good rhythm, but hesitation or jerkiness always destroys good rhythm.

1. An excellent exercise for the development of the voice is the "figure eight" exercise; intone the vowel sounds ay, ee, oh, aw, oo, aw, oh, ee, in succession, repeating at will. You will note that these vowels proceed from the most open, ay, to the most closed, oo, then back to the most open. As you intone, keep on one pitch level. Start with a high pitch; later successively on lower pitch levels. This exercise can be used to develop endurance as well as tone by continuing it as long as you are able to sustain the tone. 

2. Make a list of half a dozen good poems from your favorite authors which you can read aloud for voice development. Select those with a good rhythm and a predominance of vowel sounds.

Now analyze it: in what quality of voice would the author deliver it, in the main? Are there any parts where another quality

Chapter III  1. Speech an Expression of the Whole 

Body. It is a common thing to hear people speak of the "organs of speech," designating the tongue, the lips, the larynx, etc., as if these organs were solely concerned in the speech activity. As a matter of fact these organs are not primarily organs of speech. Their chief function is in connection with the taking of food and breathing. 

Speech is a secondary or "overlaid" function. The truth is, that the whole body is involved in the speech activity. As we said in discussing  

(Chapter I), speech is more than the sound of words. We convey ideas and emotions by posture, by expression of the face, by the glance of the eye, by gesture, and indeed, by the whole "set" of the body. It is vastly important, then, that we should master this side of speech. 

2. Action. The term action is used to designate that part of the speech activity which addresses itself to the eye, as distin-

guished from the voice, which addresses itself to the ear. It includes posture, facial expression, use of the eyes, gesture, and all other movements of the body that reinforce or clarify the expression of thought and emotion, 3. Posture. By the term posture is usually meant the position of the body of the speaker as he stands before his hearer. It might equally well mean, however, your position as you sit before the person with whom you are speaking; and indeed, sitting posture is no less important to your effectiveness in speech than standing posture. 

Look over your classroom during a recitation period and imagine yourself in the place of the teacher. Would you not be able to tell, from the posture of the students, who were alert and eager to participate in the discussion, who were sleepy and inattentive, who were unprepared and fearful of being called upon? Of course you would. Then reverse the position: now you are yourself; you know your lesson, and you want to make the best recitation possible. In other words, you want to make your speech effective in communicating what you know to your teacher. Remember that your posture,

which reveals something about your mental state, is part of your speech: make it effective. Sit up straight, not stiff, but alert and attentive. Look at your teacher. Do not play with things in your hands or shuffle your feet; these motions betoken nervousness, not composure and confidence.

So far as standing posture is concerned, much the same thing may be said. Stand erect, but not stiffly so. Feet should be placed so that the heels are within an inch or so of each other, the line of the feet making an angle of not more than sixty degrees, one foot a little advanced beyond the other. 

Head should be up, eyes forward, arms hanging naturally at the side when not used in making gestures. 

4. The James-Lang Theory of Emo tions. A number of years ago two psychologists, James and Lange, developed the theory of emotions which bears their names and which has something to teach us in regard to action in speech. The theory concerns the relation between bodily and emotional states. It had long been believed that the emotional state precedes the bodily response. That is, it was believed that the cat saw the dog, got frightened, and then its

hair stood up and its back arched. James and Lange held that the reverse was true: the cat saw the dog, arched its back and erected its hair, and then felt frightened.

It may be said that at the present time the James-Lange theory is not entirely accepted; nevertheless, it offers a valuable suggestion to the beginning speech student. In terms of action, it is to the effect that if we stand in an alert position, we are likely to be alert. If we assume an air and a posture of confidence, we are likely to feel confidence. Grit your teeth and clench your fist, and you can develop the aggressiveness you need to carry your point in an argument. 

5. Facial Expression. There is a subtle connection between facial expression and inward emotional state which every one is aware of, though it is difficult to define or describe it. We know at once, by minute tensions and relaxations in the muscles of the face of the person to whom we are speaking, whether he is worried, embarrassed, relieved, or delighted by the news we convey to him. Likewise, your auditor knows whether you, the speaker, are interested or uninterested in what you are saying, whether you are at ease and confident,

whether you are frightened. That is, he knows unless you have trained yourself in hiding the signs, unless you have cultivated a "poker face."

The significant point for the speaker in this subtle face language is the fact that the emotions which your facial expression reveal tend to be communicated to your audience. Make then the most of your facial expression. Don't stand like a blank wall before the person to whom you are speaking. 

Let your face speak alertness, interest, pleasure, enthusiasm. Practice before a mirror to see that your face registers the emotions you want to convey. 

6. Use of Eyes. The use of the eyes might be included in a discussion of facial expression; but they are an agent of expression of such superlative importance that they deserve special consideration. You must learn to focus your eyes directly on the eyes of the person you are speaking to, if you would establish communication. It will not do to look in his general direction, or to glance at him occasionally, or to look at him surreptitiously when you think he is looking the other way. If you are talking to several people, don't look over their heads; don't let

your eyes rove restlessly from one to another without ever alighting; don't look upon the floor. You must focus your eyes, not always, it is true, upon one member of your audience, but always upon someone in the audience. 

There is no other way by which you may maintain communication with them. 

7. The Truth about Gesture. No doubt one of the most difficult parts of action for the beginner to learn is gesture, the use of the limbs-especially of the hands and arms-in communication. The beginner who stands up before an audience for the first time is bothered with the mere fact that he has arms and hands, which seem woefully in the way, far from thinking to use them in speaking! But part of the difficulty with gesture comes from the holding of mistaken notions in regard to the relation of gesture to speech. I remember seeing once a certain college football coach address a meeting, following a group of intercollegiate debaters. 

The debaters, all of them trained speakers, had naturally and effectively used gestures. 

The coach, seeming to feel that it was up to him likewise to use gesture, did so, very awkwardly and ineffectively, though what he was saying and his own feeling did not at all call

for the use of gestures.

One who is familiar with the practice of great orators knows that they vary widely in the use of gestures. So great an orator as 

John Bright, reckoned in the middle of the last century one of the most effective speakers in England, almost never used a gesture.

On the opposite extreme stands Rufus Choate, the great American lawyer, whose gesturing someone described as like that of "a monkey in convulsions." 

There are 

8. The Function of Gesture. 

two fundamental principles that lie behind all gesture. First, the function of gesture is to reinforce and illustrate the thought and emotion that are being expressed by the speaker. It does not simply go somehow with speech, as a dress suit goes with a formal dance. If it does not reinforce or illustrate the content of the speech, then-no gesture. 

9. Impulse Precedes Gesture. The second principle is, before there is any gesture, there must be the impulse to gesture, the desire to communicate with gesture. If you don't feel like making a gesture, don't make it. It would probably seem awkward and ineffective. But of course, you say, if I could

get over the seeming awkwardness of gesturing, I might learn to gesture effectively, and so become a forceful speaker. Well, the thing to do is to become a foceful speaker first, and gestures will then come naturally. 

That is, become so interested in what you are saying, so earnest in your desire to communicate your ideas to those with whom you are speaking, that inevitably you will make gestures, often without realizing that you are doing so. Then your gestures will not seem awkward and artificial, for they will be natural.

Manuals of speech delivery explain many different kinds of gesture, and their signifiance. The beginner in speech need trouble himself little about the kinds. It he masters these few simple principles, he will have made a good beginning. Don't worry about gesture at the start of your speech experience. After you have begun to develop some confidence on your feet, and some fluency in the expression of your ideas, then you may begin to study gesture. Very likely you will be using gesture before you are at all conscious of it. 

10. Some Do's and Don't's of Action. The teacher of long experience in training public

speakers discovers that students year after year act pretty much alike. They make the same mistakes and they show the same aptitudes for growth. The following suggestions are the fruit of much observation of the bad and the good habits of action which speech students tend to develop. 

Let us first list some don't's: 

Don't stand with your hands clasped behind your back, while you are talking. 

It betokens withdrawal from your audience. If you don't want to use your hands for gestures, let gravity take care of them: let them hang naturally at your sides. 

Don't put your hands in your pocketsthis for boys. Fortunately, girls are not tempted to do so.

Don't lean upon the lectern. Nervous speakers often do so, as a way of pretending composure or familiarity with the audience. It is bad form. You cannot do it if you are alert and intent upon communicating. 

Don't stand rigidly in one place, as if your feet were nailed to the floor. Especially, don't stay behind the lectern.

Don't walk around too much; it detracts attention from what you are saying.

Don't play with notes, books, the tableware, etc., while you are talking. It dissipates the attention of your hearer. 

And now for some positive suggestions: 

Try to see yourself as others see you. Remember that you are speaking with your whole body. 

Be sure that you are properly clothed: coat buttoned, tie straight, as well as trousers creased! For the girls, tasteful clothes, and not too much makeup! 

Keep your best foot foremost when you stand before an audience or a single person. 

Speak "heads-up" just as you play football or baseball "heads-up."

Chapter IV. 

 1. Relation of Speech and English. In recent years, there has been a growing recognition in the educational systems of this country of the intimate relation between training in speech and training in English. 

As yet the importance of speech training as a part of the training in the use of the mother tongue has not been as clearly admitted here as in England; but it has been sufficiently recognized that many states now require at least a half year of "oral English."

The exponents of speech education, on their part, have long recognized, in theory and in practice, the intimate nature of the bond between good speech-voice, action, enunciation, etc.-and good English, that is, proper diction, syntax, style. The teacher in either field, speech or English, ought constantly to coordinate the work of the two fields. Better still, the two fields should be regarded as one, as they were in the educational practice of ancient Rome, as one may

observe from Quintilian's famous educational treatise, the Institutes of Oratory. 

2. Oral Use of English. How,vastly important it is that the pupils of our public schools should receive instruction in English with a view to its use in spoken language, is apparent from a consideration of how much more we speak than write. Few of us after we leave our composition classes in school or college will do any writing except the writing of letters, and then our letters will be read by comparatively few people, and will have little influence on the opinion people form of our knowledge of English. Consider, on the contrary, the use of our speech; by it we communicate with practically all our associates, and probably ninety-nine people out of every hundred with whom we come into contact, who have any opinion at all as to our knowledge of the English language, have formed the opinion from the way we use the language in talking to them. 

3. Grammatical Correctness. In speaking as in writing it is necessary that the language used be grammatically correct. Wide reading of good English and association with cultured people ought to develop a feeling for good English, for correctness in English is a

matter of usage; that which is approved in usage by a large number of cultured people is good English, even though it may be illogical and of illiterate origin. The surest way, however, to acquire a knowledge of what constitutes correctness in English is to study carefully and consult frequently a good manual, like Woolley's Handbook of

7. Slang. While we are dealing with diction, we should say a word about slang. 

The dictionary defines it as "language or words consisting either of new words or phrases, often of the vagrant or illiterate classes, or of ordinary words or phrases in arbitrary senses, and having a conventional but vulgar or inelegant use." High school students need especially to be warned against the use of slang, for the reason that they are of the age to be especially attracted by it. It has the appeal of the bizarre and the unusual, but tends to corrupt the taste and encourage indolence of thought. It may be said, however, that there are different degrees of offensiveness in slang. Some of it is merely a striving for the picturesque, and may be excused if not overdone; some of it is a substitute for thinking. Of the latter class is "Oh yeah? You tellin' me? So what?" It belongs to the moron, not to the intelligent student. The latter can make his speech spicy and pungent without resort to these ragged shreds of illiterate speech. 

8. Pronunciation. The correct pronunciation of words is extremely important in good speech. While there are variant pronunciations allowed by many authorities on

many words, other commonly heard pronuniations are condemned by all authorities. You should make it a habit to consult a good dictionary whenever you hear a pronunciation different from yours for a particular word. You will frequently discover that your pronunciation has been wrong. In order to use a dictionary intelligently to ascertain pronunciations, you must know the key to pronunciation used by the particular dictionary. Usually it is given either at the foot of the page or at the beginning of the dictionary. A half hour's study should be sufficient for you to commit the system of diacritical marks to memory.

10. Style. Thus far is this chapter we have dealt with the matter of being correct, both in grammar and in the use of words. 

But good English is more than a matter of correctness. English to be good must also have style; that is, strength, vigor, individuality, flavor. Style is usually defined as the manner of expressing oneself; it is peculiar to the individual. Buffon, the famous French critic, said it was "the man himself." It has to do with the kind of vocabulary used, the habit of sentence structure, whether prevailingly long or short, simple or complex, the use or avoidance of figurative language; also, in no small measure, with one's way of thinking, whether abstract or concrete, philosophical or practical. One man is bold and daring in his expression, another always

cautious, orderly, conventional, yet both may have good styles. 

11. Differences between Oral and Written 

Style. What we have just said has reference to style in general. The man whose style is distinctive will reveal his traits of style both in his writing and in his speech. 

Nevertheless there is a distinct difference between a good style in writing and a good style in speaking. In writing one need not be so very much concerned about logic and coherence as in speaking. Of course the writer should seek to be logical and coherent; but the reader, if he does not understand a sentence the first time he reads it, may go back over it. Moreover, he fixes his own rate of reading. Not so with the hearer; his rate of hearing is fixed for him by the speaker. Nor can he go back over the speech, if he does not understand the first time what has been said. Therefore the speaker must be instantly intelligible, must seek clarity above all things. This means that he must avoid long or involved sentences or circuitous methods of expression. 

He must be straight-forward and direct. He must use much more of illustration and example than would be necessary to a writing

style. His language must be kept simple, even colloquial. 

9. Acquiring a Style. 

Various writers have given recipes for the acquisition of a good style. Generally the study of authors with a recognized good style is recommended. 

Thus Samuel Johnson said with regard to the style of Joseph Addison, "Whoever wishes. to attain an English style, familiar but not coarse, and elegant but not ostentatious, must give his days and nights to the volumes of Addison." Benjamin Franklin in his autobiography tells how he acquired his style. He practiced paraphrasing, using the 

Spectator papers as his model. He would read a paper studiously, then put it away for several days, then try to reproduce the thought in his own language. When he had finished his paper, he would compare it with the original.

The student of oral style can with great profit study the speeches of the great orators; I would recommend especially Webster, Lincoln, Burke. It is instructive to compare the early speeches of Lincoln and Webster with their later speeches. Both show decided growth in style. Lincoln's early speeches are often crude and vulgar; his later

speeches, particularly those delivered after he was elected President, are remarkable for their chaste beauty. Webster's early speeches are bombastic and florid. His later speeches are clear, logical, vigorous. 

While the student should study the style of other writers and speakers, he should study most his own style. There is the danger if he does not that his style will become a mere vapid copying of the style of the writer or speaker he most admires, or worse still, a sort of reminiscent echoing of many writers and speakers. Seek above all things to be yourself; independence and honesty in style are better than elegance. Don't hesitate to venture, to be daring, to be experimental. And if anyone criticizes you for being too ornate, too expansive, or calls your style bombastic, don't let that worry you. It is better for a young writer or speaker to be florid and bombastic than to be puerile and tame. Time will tone down a florid style, but it is difficult indeed to "pep up" an insipid one.

Chapter V. 

Thinking on Your Feet 

1. Fluency and Smoothness. Whether you are speaking before an andience, are participating in a general conversation, or are having a conference with a single person, you will want to acquire the qualities of fluency and smoothness. Fluency connotes a ready flow of ideas and words; smoothness the ability to speak without halting and stumbling. Both depend partly upon the ability to control psychological processes while speaking. The latter is particularly important when you are standing before an audience, when you are likely to be afflicted with that horrible thing we call stage-fright. 

2. Thinking on Your Feet. This ability to control your psychological processes while speaking, to follow your thought without becoming confused and losing the thread of your discourse, is sometimes spoken of as the ability to "think on one's feet." It is an ability the real value of which is easily perceptible. The leader in any group where men participate in discussion is likely to be

the person who can most effectively think on his feet. Many a person who lacks this ability feels, and perhaps rightly, that he has just as much knowledge, just as much intelligence, as the fluent individual who carries off the palm in every extempore discussion. If only he could develop the ability to express himself clearly, concisely, directly, and without the confusing effect of selfconsciousness which his rising to his feet always induces! 

Thinking on one's feet is not different from thinking at one's desk, except that added to the problem of thinking in general you have the problem of controlling stage fright, of acquiring such a mastery of yourself and your ideas that you do not become panicky from looking into the eyes of people sitting before you. The problem of thinking in general cannot be considered within the scope of this discussion; but the problem of learning to control your psychological processes as you stand before an audience may be given some consideration here. 

Perhaps the first cause of stage fright is the unusualness of the situation in which, standing before an audience, you find yourself. A multitude of eyes are turned toward

Thinking on Your Feet 67 you expectantly; here are a crowd of people waiting for you to say something that shall be of value to them. You have the feeling that you are unequal to the situation; that you have nothing to give; that there are those before you who will know more than you do about any subject you touch. 

3. Overcoming Stage Fright: Preparation. 

There are two ways in which you may strive to overcome this devastating feeling. You may fortify yourself against the hour of trial by making as thorough a preparation for each speech occasion as time allows. You know in advance that when your occasion comes, whether it be a platform speech, a conference, or a conversation, your old enemy, stage fright, will be waiting to attack you. Forewarned is forearmed: get ready for him. 

4. Acquire a Stock of Ideas. But how, you ask, shall I make preparation for what, after all, may have to be an extempore, or even an impromptu, speech? First, by acquiring a stock of general ideas on the subject on which you will have to speak. This means reading, conversing with intelligent people, keeping your eyes and ears open. It means developing the habit of keeping up

with the times, of reading current periodicals and newspapers, and of making newspaper clippings and of taking notes. 

5. Make Outlines. A second and most important step in preparation is the making of outlines of ideas to be presented. Usually a short speech (and generally even a long speech) will contain only two or three main ideas. Work out in your outline the main ideas, and their relationship to each other and to the central idea of the speech. Think through the way by which you intend to develop your ideas to prove, to illustrate, to explain. Your outline will include the suggestion of these supporting elements. 

The several types of outlines which may be used will be discussed at length in Chapter V. 

6. Overcoming Stage Fright: Practice. 

The second way of overcoming stage fright is practice. The experience of standing as often as possible before an actual audience, in an actual speech situation, will help you gradually to overcome the feeling of unusualness in the situation, which is a large part of stage fright. The advice said to have been given once by Edward Everett 

Hale to a young man who had asked how he

Thinking on Your Feet 69 might learn to be a public speaker bears on this point. "Whenever anyone is fool enough to ask you to make a speech," Hale said, "you be fool enough to accept the invitation." 

Every young man or young woman who has participated in a large number of interscholastic or intercollegiate debates before real audiences, will testify to the value of the continued practice before an audience in helping to overcome stage fright. That is only one of the values to be obtained from training in debate, but it is a very real value. 

If you have an opportunity to engage in debating, by all means seize it. Next best to that experience is participation in the programs of young people's societies in our churches, or in the programs of literary societies in our schools. Even the making of classroom reports and of recitation, which we shall consider in Chapter VI, can be made an exercise in speech training, if you make proper preparation and think of the report not merely as reciting something you have committed to memory, but as communicating your thoughts and your ideas to your teacher and classmates. 

7. Practice Extemporization. But there is another type of practice than the practice

you get in speaking before real audiences, and that is the practice you can give yourself in speaking before an imaginary audience. One can scarcely overmphasize this element of practice-the practice of speaking aloud in extempore style, alone before a mirror, if possible; going over again and again the main ideas of your speech, probably never twice in the same words; smoothing, improving, making clearer, more forceful each time you go over the speech. Nor need you confine this sort of practice to whole speeches. Practice developing ideas on various topics, topics of current news, outstanding problems of discussion of the day.

It will greatly help you in developing a sense of communication, about which we shall speak presently, if, when you practice extempore speaking in your own study, you imagine an audience before you. It was told of Henry Flood, the great Irish orator, that when he first came to London, he used to go to a grove in the rear of his boarding house and practice making speeches to an imaginary House of Commons. His land-lady recalled that there was one tree more imposing than the rest which he always addressed by

Thinking on Your Feet 71 the title "Mr. Speaker." Henry Clay, we are told, made his first speeches to the cattle in the stable where as a young man he performed his daily chores. By the alchemy of his imagination the cattle were transformed into human auditors upon whom he was practicing. 

What these two great orators, and many others, did, you, the beginner in the art of speaking, can profit from doing. Skill in extemporization comes from practice in extemporization, just as skill in playing the piano comes from hours of finger exercises and practice scores. 

8. Writing Speeches. 

The beginner may accept the advice to seize every possible opportunity to speak in public; he may make diligent general and special preparation; he may subject himself to the discipline of extemporizing before an imaginery audience. 

But he still asks, ought I not to write out at least my first speeches? By all means; write out not only your first speeches, but most of your speeches. But write them rather as an exercise to develop your powers of expression, than as a set form to be read or repeated verbatim when the time comes to make your speech. There is no doubt that careful

composition in the quiet of your study does have a very great benefit in helping you to phrase your thought felicitously even when you are not remembering what you have written, but really "thinking on your feet." 

9. The Problem of Memorizing. Ought we never, then, to commit a speech to memory? Rarely; the beginning speaker will seldom if ever be called upon to deliver the type of formal speech which allows of memoriter delivery. Such speeches are the polished orations of great orators; set speeches, where the form of the speech is often equally important with the content. Even then the great orator may not use a memoriter delivery, or at least not throughout the entire speech. In the second place, the memorized speech is much more likely to fail in establishing contact with the audience than the extempore speech, especially with the beginning speaker. There is not likely to be the reality of the thinking process in delivering the memorized speech as there is in the extempore speech; and that reality of thinking while speaking produces subtle effects in speech melody, rhythm, force, which characterize the so-called conversational style of speaking. The conversational style,

Thinking on Your Feet 73 in turn, commands attention of the audience, and that is the supreme desideratum. Getting the attention of the audience is what we mean by "getting our speeches over," establishing contact with the audience, communicating with the audience. 

10. A Good Memory Essential to a Speak er. From what has just been said about rarely committing a speech to memory, it is not to be understood that there is no place for memory in the process of speech making. A reliable memory is not only a desirable quality in one who aspires to be a successful speaker; it is an absolute requisite. The speaker should have a memory well stored with ideas, specific facts, pat illustrations. The great writers on rhetoric in antiquity, Cicero and Quintilian, require that the orator shall have at his command the sum total of human knowledge-philosophy, history, poetry, law, science. That is, of course, too much to expect of any modern orator; but the principle still holds. The accurate and ready memory, the well-stored memory, is the arsenal from which the fluent speaker draws his weapons as he carries on his battle.

There is yet another thing to be said about the business of memorizing a speech. 12. The Sense of Communication. 

erence has been made several times in this chapter to the sense of communication. By this is meant comprehension by the speaker, while he is speaking, not only of what he himself is saying, but of what is going on in the minds of his auditors. Sometimes the sense of communication is taken for granted when we say that a man thinks on his feet, though the phrase does not necessarily connote it. It is, however, just as important as the ability to control one's psychological processes while speaking, which we have said is essentially what thinking one's feet means. One who has the sense of communication not only entertains ideas; in addition to holding ideas, he is motivated by the desire to arouse those ideas in others, and an awareness of whether or not he is succeeding in doing that. If not, he tries to discover why not; he tries a new line approach; he introduces new material; he on of changes his technique of speaking; he

speaks more deliberately; he uses direct address more frequently; he may speak more quietly, or more loudly; he may use more pause. All the time he is studying the reaction of his audience. They are talking to him (if he has not entirely lost them) all the time he is talking to them. Only they use a sign language, which he must be skillful to read. 

A cultivation of the sense of communication depends primarily on the attitude with the speaker approaches his task. Enemies of it are the desire for display, whether of ideas, of assumed knowledge, of rhetoric, or of voice and person; and the habit of soliloquizing aloud. Remember that when you get up to speak, your job is to get your speech over, not to get it off. Don't speak absent-mindedly; speak directly. Don't be satisfied to tell why you believe in a certain idea; make your audience believe in it. Do not allow yourself by what you say or the way in which you say it to alienate your audience. You must conciliate and win them, to you and your idea.

After you try to recall them, you discover that there are some that escape you. You go back to your

outline to look them up. After a little intense self-quizzing like this, the outline is yours.

Take turns in asking questions, and in correcting the answers given. A group of students thus cooperating will cover more points of a subject than one alone is likely to think of. 

13. Participation in the Recitation. 

What we have said thus far has to do with preparation for the recitation. We must now consider how you may make the most of the opportunity for which you have prepared. 

There are several methods of conducting a recitation, the most common being the question-answer method, in which the teacher quizzes orally the members of the class on the lesson assignment.

The first thing that we may say with respect to your preparation in this type of recitation concerns your posture and general attitude. It matters not whether you are required to stand in answering questions or allowed to remain seated-teachers

differ in their requirements as to that-you should maintain an attitude of alertness and attention. Teachers consciously and unconsciously rate students on their general attitude during recitation period, and it is always to your advantage to appear (and to be) wide awake and interested.

Secondly, listen to the teacher's questions and be sure you know what they mean. Some questions, of course, call merely for facts; you either know or don't know the answer. 

Reply to such questions promptly, being honest enough to admit ignorance when you do not know. Other questions call for thinking, rather than merely remembering. Keep your head when you are presented such a question. Do not go off half-cocked, and reply before you have weighed the answer. 

Another point that demands your consideration is the fact that a recitation is a social activity: there are other students beside yourself. Don't consume too much time in your participation in the recitation; don't volunteer more information than is called for. Give the other students a chance make their contribution. 

14. Class Reports Another type of recitation is that in which members of the

class are assigned the making of reports on topics given them by the teacher. Usually a time limit or a word limit is fixed for such reports. Obviously such reports give you a splendid opportunity to practice all you know of the art of public speaking. 

Commonly such reports are read from manuscript, and badly read; frequently they are written with no other thought than that of accuracy in stating facts. Of course you should be faithful to the facts; but that is no reason why you should not prepare your report in the form of an attractive speech, logically arranged, with due attention to good rhetoric and to an interesting as well as accurate presentation of the facts. Then when you come to deliver the report, don't read your manuscript: talk it to the class, and give it with enthusiasm and vitality. 

Face the class, and speak with head up, voice clear, enunciation sharp and distinct. 

You can speak thus, if you will; make up your mind that you will. 

15. Questioning and “Quilling". A properly conducted recitation is not only a period in which the teacher questions you to find out what you know. It should also be a teaching period, in which your ideas are clar-

ified and integrated. To that end, every thoughtful student should avail himself of the privilege of asking his teacher questions on points connected with the lesson; but be sure you don't ask questions on points of information you could have looked up for yourself. The good teacher welcomes questions that reveal careful thought on the part of the student, but he is inclined to refuse to answer questions that the student should be able to answer himself.

There is another type of question that you should avoid, and that is the question that is designed to constitute a "quill." A teacher with a sense of justice recognizes that type of question, and rates you down for it. If the teacher doesn't, you may be sure the other students do. Don't try to claim an unfair share of the teacher's attention.

toys with his books or pencils. The teacher may be lenient and overlook these things, but that does not make them any better.

The great evil of such careless and indolent speaking is that is fixes itself in the habits of speech of the student. Later, when he comes into the speech class, his speech teacher must labor to overcome the habits he has fixed. are

Chapter VII  1. Speech and Job Hunting. Not long ago the personnel representative of a large industrial concern which every year employs dozens of university graduates visited the campus of one of our universities to interview some of the men who were about to graduate. He met in a group about a score of men, all of whom had been trained in a very efficient department of business administration. At the conclusion of the interview, one of the students very frankly said to the personnel man, "Would you mind telling us how you size us up?" The personnel representative was interested by the question, and for answer slouched into a chair in an attitude in imitation of some of the students before him, looked away from them and out of the window, and started to talk to them in a purposely hesitant and disinterested fashion. "Suppose," he said, "I should sit before a business prospect and talk to him in this fashion. What chance do you think I would have of doing business with him?"

With this graphic comment upon the attitude of some of the young men before him, all of whom were desperately in earnest about the business of getting jobs, he proceeded to give them a lecture on speech and salesmanship. He meant by salesmanship, as he told them, not merely the selling of merchandise, but the very much more important business of selling themselves. He brought home to most of the students before him, something that many of them had never realized, the dollars and cents value of learning to talk effectively. 2. 

Persuasion-Learning What People 

Like. When I refer to effective speech, I mean very much more than good voice, good posture, good enunciation, though all these are important. I mean speech which as its total effect produces a desired result; this is effective speech in a literal sense. 

Certainly a thing which all of us desire ardently is a job, and success in that job. How can we make our speech help us to achieve this desire? 

First, by improving our speech, in the mechanics of voice and action, in ways already discussed (Chapters II and III), to the ut-

most of our ability. But there is something further, and that is the art of persuasionlearning what people like and what they dislike, and using speech to appeal to their likes, and to avoid their dislikes.

A few years ago at one of the annual meetings of the National Association of Teachers of Speech, a prominent teacher of speech from an eastern university was talking of the effect of debate training on prospective business men, and particularly, salesmen.

He maintained that the effect of debatetraining was not good, because it made those who received it too aggressive. In the discussion which ensued, it developed that this particular professor had had no connection with intercollegiate debating since the days when he himself had been an intercollegiate debater. In those early days, almost no attention had been paid in debating to the element of persuasion.

The professor was right in his general position that any type of speech training which develops unlikable characteristics is defective. The art of persuasion, which must always be the highest study of the student of speech, has as its first principle that speech to be effective must appeal to the likes of

the audience, whether that audience be a single person or a group of people. 

3. Impelling Motives. Writers on the subject of speech psychology from earliest times have attempted to analyze the motives that lie behind human action. Aristotle, the great Greek philosopher, in his treatise on rhetoric, written about 330 B. C., makes a list of motives to which the speaker may appeal. Later writers, down to the present time, have followed him more or less in detail. One of the best modern statements of the motives of action is that of Prof. A. E. 

Phillips*. He lists as the most powerful human motives the desire for self-preservation, property, power, reputation, affections, sentiments (ideals), and tastes (aesthetic). 

We might illustrate the value of such a study of motives by reference to an automobile salesman. Suppose he approaches a young doctor as a "prospect." How could he appeal to each of the foregoing list of motives in the doctor, who as yet does not own a car? 

Self-preservation: he could point out to *Effective Speaking, Chicago, The Newton Co., 1908.

the doctor that he cannot compete in his profession with the other doctors, all of whom have automobiles, unless he buys one. 

Property: the salesman would demonstrate the beauty and the efficiency of the car, to provoke the desire to own it for the sake of owning it. 

Power: the salesman takes the doctor for a ride, and shows him how easy and pleasant it is for him to drive the car. 

He arouses in the doctor the desire to learn to drive for the pleasure of being able to drive. 

Reputation: the salesman makes the doctor He feel that in order to live up to his station in society, he should have a car. must be known to have one, even though he may have little use for it. 

Affections: if the salesman has not made a sale by appealing to the foregoing motives, he takes the doctor's wife and children out for a ride in the new car. 

They want it, and the doctor, because of his love for them, must buy it.

Sentiments: it is somewhat difficult to illustrate an appeal to the sentiments

(ideals) in an effort to sell an automobile. But suppose the salesman should point out that the doctor would be stimulating employment if he bought a car. That would appeal to the doctor's sense of sympathy for the unfortunate unemployed-an ideal. 

Tastes: the salesman could demonstrate the superior points of beauty of the car, and arouse the desire to own it for the beauty of it.

A knowledge of what motives move people to action, it will readily be seen, is tremendously important in determining what we shall say when we talk to people for a desired object, what line of approach we shall take, what arguments we shall advance. 

4. Broader Aspects of Persuasion. Touch ing the springs of action in people is part of the business of persuasion, but it is not all of it. There is a broader aspect of persuasion as part of the art of speech, where persuasion merges into that intangible something we call the personality. That is to say, your speech and your actions reveal to your associates, to your employer, to your business "contracts," elements of your character

which make them like or dislike you, quite apart from what you say to them. It is very much your business, then, to cultivate those qualities of character, which, revealing themselves through your speech and action, will win respect and liking for you.

5. Speech and the Professions. 

What we have just been saying concerns your getting along with people, and applies to your speech effectiveness whatever may be the work you are engaged in. There are certain professions, however, for success in which ability in speech is not only desirable, but an absolute requisite. Chief among these are the ministry, certain types of legal practice, politics, lecturing; these demand not not only general speech effectiveness, but specifically, skill in platform speaking.

Two other professions, teaching and salesmanship, demand a high degree of effectiveness in speech generally, though not necessarily ability in platform speaking. 

6. Speech and the Ministry. If you are thinking of entering the ministry, you should make it your duty to determine whether you have or can develop the ability to speak effectively in public. If not, you may as well conclude that the ministry is not for you. No amount of the sense of being called, no degree of spiritual fervor, can compensate for inability to speak effectively. Of course this is not to be understood as implying that success in the ministry means only good preaching. It means

much more than that, but it also means that, as dwindling congregations throughout the nation testify; as overflowing congregations, in spite of the materialism and indifference of this age, where there is good preaching, also testify. 

7. Speech and the Law. There was a time when every lawyer had to be a public speaker. That is no longer true. Many kinds of the most remunerative legal practice nowadays do not involve public speaking. But the lawyer who wishes to plead before a jury, particularly in criminal practice, must train himself in public speaking as a condition of his success. Likewise must the lawyer who aims to make the law a stepping stone to public service or politics. 

In any event ability to speak effectively is an asset of peculiar value to a lawyer. 

8. Speech and Politics. If you would understand the necessity of ability in public speaking for the politician who aspires to high advancement in a public career, you need only take a poll of the leaders in political life today, both in the United States and abroad. Viviani and Briand in France, Mussolini in Italy, Hitler in (Germany,

Roosevelt and Borah, La Follette and Nye, to say nothing of Long and Coughlin-all these men have achieved distinction and advancement through the power of clear, incisive, and forceful speech. It has been a truism in American politics since the time of Lincoln that no one may aspire to the highest office in our republic without the gift of speech; only one or two in that time have succeeded in reaching the presidency who have not been good public speakers. 

9. The Professional Lecturer. Finally, among those who should be classed as professional public speakers, we should name the professional lecturer. Since the days of the founding of the Lyceum in New England, about a century ago, he has been an important agency in the development of American culture. The lecture platform has attracted, at one time or another, some of our foremost thinkers, writers, statesman.

Emerson made it almost his lifetime work to lecture before the public; Mark Twain, Henry Ward Beecher, William Jennings 

Bryan, and many others have found lecturing an interesting and a lucrative profession. Even at the present time, our best lecturers command fees of from three to

five hundred dollars for a lecture, and may have fifty or more engagements in a year.

So lucrative a field has of course attracted many men, some of whom, having won fame in other pursuits-literature, exploration, science, etc.-have sought to "cash in" on their fame by going to the lecture platform.

Invariably their success has been subject to their ability to make good as public speakers. The public soon tires of gazing at really great men if they pose as lecturers and cannot speak effectively. 

10. Speech and Teaching. The teacher, we have said, is not, in the same sense as a preacher or a lecturer, a professional public speaker. Nevertheless, while his speaking is confined to smaller and more intimate audiences in his regular teaching work, it is a very important part of his equipment for his profession. If he aspires to rise high in his profession, it is almost imperative that he learn to speak effectively. The superintendent of schools, the college dean or president, receives numerous invitations to speak in public. If he makes good, opportunities for advancement and wider usefulness open to him. If he fails, he is doomed to a narrower field of service.

11. Speech and Salesmanship. Salesmanship, like teaching, requires ability in an intimate kind of speaking. Particularly, it requires ability to meet all kinds of people, to talk to them face to face, to read their thought and to meet even their unvoiced objections while giving them a sales talk.

Here the art of persuasion meets one of its severest tests, for, as every salesman of experience knows, an unfortunate reference or a tactless remark on some subject that may have nothing to do with the object to be sold may ruin the effect of the best sales approach and the glibbest sales talk. 

12. Speech in Conference. Closely allied to the kind of speech ability demanded of the salesman is that required of the executive in every kind of human enterprise. He must meet men in conference, measure his mind with theirs, study their motives intimately as he talks to them across the mahogany desk. This kind of speaking does not require the ability to organize speech material, as platform speaking does; but it necessitates clear and quick thinking, the maintenance of poise and self-control, and especially, the ability to read the thoughts and emotions of the man before you.

13. The Attainment of Poise. Throughout this book, we have insisted upon the point of view that speech is not only a means of communication with our fellow, but may also may be an adornment and a thing of supreme beauty. After stressing that point of view, it may seem paradoxical to conclude with a discussion of the relation of speech to success in your job. But the two points of view are complementary and not antithetical. 

If your speech becomes a thing of beauty, it will be because, from mastery of the technique of thinking, of voice, and of action, you have also made your speech an efficient instrument of communication. As an efficient instrument of communication it will have a vital bearing upon your success in your vocation, whatever that may be.

The thought has been much stressed in modern educational theory that the function of education is to develop all the latent powers of the individual, so that he shall have at least the chance to become all that his natural endowments entitle him him to become. With that thought we are in hearty accord. Our aim in our speech education should be to make our control over the instruments of speech, that is, our think-

ing, our emotions, and our whole bodies, so complete that we shall be able to meet successfully any emergency that faces us that calls for communication with our fellows.

That is what what we usually designate by the word poise-self-mastery, self-confidence, a fearless facing of the tasks and responsibilities of life. You may be sure if through training in speech you attain to such poise, your speech will be an agent of efficiency in helping you to success in your job, and at the same time will be a cultural adornment to your personality.

1. Gift or Acquisition? Charm, according to the famous dictum of Sir James Barrie, is sometimes which, if you have it, nothing else matters; if you don't have it, nothing can be done about it. There are many who regard skill in conversation in the same light. It is, they think, a gift of the gods; you either have it or don't have it. Once in a century a brilliant conversationalist appears, a Charles Lamb, a Madame de Stael, a Margaret Fuller d'Ossoli. Wit, humor, wisdom, vivacity, sarcasm, brilliant imagination flow from them. Ordinary mortals form the background against which they scintillate, and perhaps glow faintly be reflection of their brilliance. But they are to be accepted, not explained. Their conversational powers seem unrelated to wisdom, to literary style or power in the written word. Samuel Johnson lorded it in conversation over the most brilliant men of England of his day, yet wrote in a ponderous and heavy style. Contrast with him his friend

and contemporary Goldsmith, author of some of the most readable poetry and one of the best plays that came out of the eighteenth century. Yet as a conversationalist, Goldsmith was a failure; "Goldsmith is a fool, the more wearing for having some sense," said Horace Walpole, after having been to a party with him; and one of his contemporaries said of him that he "wrote like an angel, but talked like poor Poll."

If this fatalistic view of conversational power were correct, there would be little use in writing on the subject of . But perhaps this view has reference to the genius in conversation, and genius in any field is rather a matter of birth than of acquisition. At any rate, we may endeavor to analyze the power of conversation, to determine what it consists of, and to point out some of the pitfalls that beset the path of the young person who aspires to its mastery. 

2. Ideas. We have already said that in any field of speech activity, matter is of first importance. A smiling face, a musical voice, a happy disposition are assets to the conversationalist, but they are not enough.

A present day journalist, telling of his ex-

perience as a "ghost" writer, speaks of a certain prize fighter in whose name he was writing a pseudo-autobiography. He wanted some human interest details from the man's youth. "What experiences did you have in your childhood days that might be of interest?" he asked. "Well, I had a dog," the puglist replied, and that was the utmost that he could extract from him. Needless to say, the pugilist was not noted for conversational powers. 

You may as well accept the fact that you will never shine in conversation if you have a horizon that juts down in front of your nose, so that every time you step forward, you bump into it. Push your horizon away from you. It would probably be presumptuous for anyone in the present day to say, like Francis Bacon, "I take all knowledge to be my province;" but on the other hand there is a certain core of knowledge which ought to be the property of every educated, intelligent person. That would include a knowledge at least of the main outlines of world history, some acquaintance with classic literatures, with a more detailed knowledge of English literature; at least sufficient acquaintance with the history of philosophy to know who

have been the great creative thinkers of the world, and what they have stood for; finally, an awareness of what is going on in the world today, in politics, in art, in literature, in human thought.

There is no sovereign way by which you may acquire this core of knowledge, save by wide reading, by association with cultured people, and by keeping an open, alert mind. 

A college course will by no means guarantee it to you; on the other hand, you may make yourself a cultured man or woman without ever seeing the inside of a college.

Or to the "funny sheet"?

How many of the current magazines are you acquainted with? As to books, do you limit yourself to novels, and the lightest possible? Do you read biography, psyshology, science, history? Do you ever get hold of the Saturday Review of Literature or the

One of the most brilliant men we ever had in the White House was Theodore Roosevelt, brilliant especially as a conversationalist. Of

him the great English critic John Morley said, after a visit to this country, that he had been impressed by two things, Niagara 

Falls and Theodore Roosevelt. Roosevelt's conversational powers were certainly in part due to the fact that he was an omnivorous and a voracious reader. Ike Hoover, in his "Forty-two Years in the White House," says that Roosevelt devoured all the current magazines as fast as they appeared on the newstands, and would frequently read two books through in a single evening. But even these differ greatly in style. Some can be skimmed through quickly; others demand close reading, careful weighing of the thought,

consultation of reference books. This we call intensive reading. Both kinds are indispensable to the alert mind.

Avoid groups whose intellectual interests do not, or cannot be made to, coincide with yours.

7. Courtesy-Listening. not monologue, though the egoist frequently tries to make it such. As in all speaking, your success in conversation is measured by the approval of the group with which you converse. Even the most brilliant talker

cannot with impunity monopolize the conversation. Arnold Bennett in his memoirs comments upon a party he had attended, where were present a number of famous literary men and women, among them Bernard Shaw. "Shaw talked all the time," says Bennett, "which is a blank sight too much." If Shaw couldn't "get away with it," be sure you can't. Learn to listen, as well as to talk. A good conversation should be free and easy, not formal, but the principle of courtesy is not vacated by informality. 

8. Tact. Along with courtesy, it is well to couple a fair consideration for the feelings of other people, which we call tact. 

Some topics of conversation must be avoided because they arouse controversy or touch upon sore spots. Argument, except playful, good-natured argument, has no place in conversation. Steer away from the topics that are likely to lead to it.

There is a story which illustrates the blundering lack of tact which often leads to the painful embarrassments with which we are all familiar. A man had committed suicide by hanging himself in the attic. A neighboring woman with a deeply sympathe-

tic nature but a tactless habit of saying the wrong thing, desired to express her sympathy to the widow. Her daughter warned her, "Be careful, mother, what you say; you know you always say the wrong thing." "I'll talk about nothing but the weather," the mother promised. 

Sure enough, when she arrived at the home of Mrs. Smith, where the tragedy had occurred, she introduced a reference to the weather. "It's terrible weather we're having, isn't it, raining every day?" "Yes," said Mrs. Smith, "I can't get my clothes to dry." "Why Mrs. Smith," said the tactless one, "you shouldn't have any trouble to get your clothes dry; you have such lovely attic to hang things in."

Which illustrates the truth that even the weather is not always a safe topic! 

9. Emotional Control. A happy disposi tion, we have said, is an asset to the conversationalist. There can be no freedom and ease in conversation unless there is a feeling of friendship and equality among the participants in it. Moods and emotions that in-

terfere with this friendliness must be ruled out or controlled. If you feel blue, depressed, discouraged, you had better plead indisposition than go to a party, unless you are sure you can rise above your mood. The blues are a wet blanket to conversation. 

10. Wit. We raised the question at the beginning of this chapter, without answering it, whether conversational power is a gift or can be acquired. Whatever be the answer, there is certainly one quality of high adornment to the good conversationalist, namely, the quality we call wit, which owes more to native gift than to cultivation. 

It consists essentially in the ability to see quickly amusing associations between words. and ideas. By its very nature, it is spontaneous and unpremeditated, and cannot be forced. You know the type of person who sits up nights thinking of funny things to say, and how flat his efforts at wit can be. 

If you have the gift of wit, use it, but better be content with humbler gifts if it is not yours. 

Cicero, in his discussion of wit in De

Oratore, makes a suggestion of the danger of wit, even in those who are gifted with it.

There are those, he says, who would rather lose a friend than a joke. Wit is not, like humor, always kindly. It can be mordant, malevolent, sarcastic. Except when it is used intentionally to discomfit an enemy (and then it has no place in conversation), it had better be kept within bounds. friend is more to be desired than a joke, yea, than many fine jokes!

11. Humor. Humor, we have said, is always kindly. It makes fun of human foibles and weaknesses, but sympathizes with them. 

It is, in essense, the acceptance of life, and at the same time, the refusal to take it too seriously. It owes less to intelligence, more to mood, to temperament, to innate philosophy than does wit. It can be premeditated, and is therefore more capable of cultization than wit. Still, it is well to issue a word of warning. If you would bring humor to the aid of your conversational ability, don't set about to collect funny stories. Rather, cultivate that understanding of human nature (including yourself) that sees its weaknesses and pretenses, along with the tolerance that forgives. 

12. Grace. An ability that is always an adornment to the conversationalist is the

ability to make a well-turned compliment. 

Courtesy demands the recognition of kindesses conferred in the amenities of social life; how much better it is to express gratitude or pleasure in new and fresh ways, than to fall back upon the hackneyed phrases which everybody utters. Grace of expression comes as a part of liberal culture. You can, if you set out to do so, acquire it. Behind it must lie a sincere feeling of good will toward your associates, and of genuine appreciation of the good qualities of others. 

Don't be content to express that feeling bluntly and cumbersomely; learn to clothe your thought in words that will illumine it. 

Certain types of education develop grace of expression. It was much cultivated in the South before the Civil War, and even later. There is a beautiful example of a graceful turn of language in the opening of 

Henry Grady's speech on "The New South."

Grady, a celebrated newspaper editor and orator of Atlanta, Georgia, was speaking before the New England Club of New York

City in 1886, when the bitterness of the Civil War was not yet allayed. He opened his speech by saying: "Mr. President and

Gentlemen: Let me express to you my appreciation of the kindness by which I am permitted to address you. I make this abrupt acknowledgement advisedly, for I feel that if, when I raise my provincial voice in this ancient and august presence, I could find courage for no more than the opening sentence, it would be well if in that sentence I had met in a rough sense my obligation as a guest, and had perished, so to speak, with courtesy on my lips and grace in my heart." 

13. Some Do's and Don't's. We have already uttered some cautions against the things to be avoided if you would cultivate charm in conversation, like the danger of talking too much. We might add the following injunctions: don't simper; don't gush; don't flatter; don't pose. Finally, as a positive suggestion, the adage, "B natural, or you'll B flat."

Chapter IX  

One of the oldest forms of human entertainment historically is to be found in speech activities. In the remotest times and among the most primitive peoples, the teller of stories and the reciter of ballads had a position of honor and influence in society. Before literature was committed to writing, it was transmitted from generation to generation by word of mouth. The reciter was as instrumental in its preservation as the composer; and indeed, reciter and composer were probably generally the same. The Homeric poems were thus communicated orally, as were the early epic poems of almost all countries the Nibelungenlied, the Chanson de Roland, Beowulf, the sagas of Scandinavia. Among primitive peoples today the practice of entertaining by story telling and reciting still holds sway; travelers in Outer 

Mongolia report seeing large audiences of illiterate and untutored nomads listening in open-mouthed wonder and enjoyment to the

professional teller of stories as he repeats the time worn tales of their folk-lore. Beyond their rude sports of racing and wrestling this is almost their only form of entertainment, and certainly their only intellectual activity. When therefore you participate in the reading, interpreting, or acting program, you can consider that you are carrying on a very ancient tradition of entertainment. 

1. Types of Speech Entertainment. We might well think of conversation as one of the most universal forms of speech entertainment, but of course, its purpose is not exclusively entertainment, and at any rate, we have already given it extended consideration. 

The other common types of speech entertainment are the telling of stories (extended narration, as distinguished from short anecdotes, which we shall discuss in Chapter X), interpretative reading, impersonation, and acting. We shall consider each of these separately. 

2. The Art of Narration. The ability to narrate effectively provides not only a thrilling form of entertainment, particularly for children, as the popularity of our many radio

broadcast bedtime story programs witnesses, but it is a most efficient form of education. 

The skilled narrater can awaken and mold the emotional and imaginative life of his young listeners, and give them a power of enjoyment of imaginative literature which shall be of permanent spiritual value to them. He can do more; he can implant ethical and social concepts that will lie at the very root of character. All children who have been allowed their birthright live in a world of imagination, a world that is no less valid and vital than the world of reality. The wise teacher uses that imaginative life of the child to implant admiration for beauty and truth, for courage and honesty and unselfishness, through the art of story telling. 

3. Kinds of Narrative. There is such a tremendous wealth of narrative material available in English, that the teller of stories is embarrassed to make selection. He has to select from historical narrative, folk tales, fiction of various sorts, juvenile stories, fables, biblical narrative, etc. Several principles may guide you in selecting your stories.

First, adapt your story to the age of the group for which you are selecting it. The

very excellent collection of stories contained in My Bookhouse,* a set in six volumes edited by Olive Beaupre Miller, will help you in following this principle. The stories in this collection are arranged in order of increasing difficulty from Volume I to Volume VI; the earliest are for two and three year olds; those of the last two volumes are quite suitable for adults. A second principle to follow in selecting stories is the principle of literary merit: with so many stories available that you cannot in a lifetime use all that are available, you should use only the best. They are the stories that exhibit the greater truthfulness to life, the more beautiful language, the more universal appeal to interest. 

You may have difficulty at first in knowing what constitutes literary merit. With the help of good critics and wise teachers your taste will develop, so that you will be able to recognize, for example, the superior quality, from a literary standpoint, of a story like 

Ruskin's "King of the Golden River." A third principle of selection is the principle of variety. If you want to be good teller of stories, you must have a varied repertoire. 

*Published by the The Bookhouse for Children, Chicago

4. Effective Narration. It is not possible, within the very limited space of a few paragraphs, to do justice to the principles of an art that might well merit a whole volume for its discussion. However, we might offer a few useful and helpful suggestions. To begin with, if you would tell a story effectively, you must be thoroughly in the spirit of the story as you are narrating; you must live the story as you tell it. That means, of course, that you must know the story perfectly before you start to narrate, know not only the words, but all the dramatic possibilities of the story. As a matter of fact, it is not necessary that you commit the story to memory as you will find it in your text or collection. Generally it is better for you to extemporize through most of the story, as you can thus better preserve the appearance of spontaneity: but you must know thoroughly the sequence of events and the effective points of the story, so that you can tell it glibly and unhesitatingly. You will discover that you cannot hold the attention of the average audience if you attempt to read the story from manuscript or use notes. Manuscript or notes inevitably get between you and the audience, through this is not necessarily

true in the case of interpretative reading, for reasons that we shall speak of later. 

So far as voice and action are concerned, all that has been said in Chapters II and III concerning technique of speech are applicable to story telling. In narrating a story you are communicating with your audience in your own person; you must preserve the sense of communication with your audience by directness of address, by the use of eyes and gestures, by proper melody, by effective use of pause. Above all things you must cultivate a pleasing voice quality. A raucous, harsh, or rasping voice is an effective obstacle to good narration. 

5. Interpretative Reading. In interpre tative reading somewhat the same principles obtain that govern good narration. In interpretation, however, you have a wider range of material from which to select, including both prose and poetry, and including forms of prose that you do not deal with in narration. The effect that you seek in interpretation is often a purely emotional effect; it may be created, through the medium of voice and action, by fragmentary and disjointed material such as would have no place

in narration, where the interest is in the tale, and where unity and coherence are essential. In one respect, however, interpretative reading and the telling of stories are the same, and that is in the fact that in both the performer is speaking in his own person. Of course, in both he may within limits personate to represent characters of his story or reading, but in the reading or story as a whole he speaks as himself, not as someone else. In impersonation and acting, on the other hand, the performer goes out of his own person, and puts himself into the character of some else.

There are some other types of reading not included in the foregoing classification. The dramatic monologue, for instance, is a type of reading that may be either prose or poetry, in which the reader represents throughout one character. Usually that character is represented as participating in a dialogue, but there are silences for the speeches of the other character in the dialogue. Then there is the type of reading called the humorous reading, generally prose, though it may be poetry, the main purpose of which is to excite laughter. 

7. Cutting for Reading. In interpreta tive reading it is necessary for the effect desired that the text be followed more closely than is required in telling a story. But much of the prose material used for interpretative reading was not written especially for oral interpretation. It becomes necessary therefore to adapt many stories for oral interpretation by cutting them. That is to say, portions of the written story not essential to an understanding of the plot and action of the story may be omitted. Especially, long descriptions, explanatory passages, and analyses of character may be left out. Some-

times, instead of entirely leaving out an explantory passage, it is abridged or paraphrased. Nothing must be cut out which is essential to the action of the story. Finally, you will be guided as to the amount to be cut by the length of the reading which you desire. 

8. Creating the Mood. As we have said, many interpretative readings have for their purpose the creation of an emotional effect or a mood in the audience. There are moods of gayety, sadness, exhilaration, repentance, indignation, shyness, anger, etc. Before the reader can create the mood for his audience, he must feel the mood himself. If he does not feel it, his reading lacks sincerity, and nothing is more fatal to effective reading than insincerity, affectation, pretense. And before he can himself experience the mood of his selection, it goes without saying that he must understand the intent of the author of his selection. No meaning, emotional or intellectual, of the author of the piece of literature being interpreted can be communicated to the audience unless it is first thoroughly grasped, experienced, assimilated by the reader. Therefore, as a first prin-

ciple, you must live with your selection until it becomes a part of you before you can interpret it for anyone else. 

9. Interpreting Technique. All that we have said in the preceding paragraph has to do with the subjective state of the reader, his mental content and emotional experience. 

This is of course basic; but good reading depends also upon the reader's mastery of the objective technique, that is, the symbols of speech, voice and action. 

10. Grouping, Phrasing, or Centering. One of the methods by which the reader communicates a correct understanding of the intellectual content of his reading is by a correct grouping of his words so as to give due emphasis to the important ideas. Sometimes this process is called phrasing, and sometimes centering. It breaks up the even continuity of language by the use of pause and changes in rate, so as to stress important ideas, and keep unimportant ones in a subordinate position.

While the main effect of grouping is an intellectual effect, it also has a relation to rhythm, which is concerned chiefly with the emotional effect of reading. The grouping

must not be allowed to interfere with a rhythm appropriate to the mood of the selection. 

11. Tone Coloring. The chief vocal ele ment in the expression of emotion, as we learned in Chapter II, is voice quality. It is an extremely important element for the oral interpreter. The good reader must have a flexible voice, must have at his command a wide range of quality. The process of shifting from one quality to another to express changes in emotion is often called tone coloring. It is partly a subconscious effect produced in the reader by his own emotions, so that the first thing the reader has to do, if he would use effective tone coloring, is to feel the emotions of the selection he is reading. But the effective use of vocal quality is not wholly a subjective matter; the reader must, in analyzing his selection, determine what qualities of voice are appropriate to the interpretation of the emotional content of the passage; and more important, he must often practice assiduously to develop the necessary voice qualities. 

12. Manuscript or Memory? The question is often asked by the beginning student in

interpretation, ought I commit to memory all the selections I shall read? The answer is that generally you can interpret better from memory than from manuscript; but it is less imperative that you read from memory than that you avoid a manuscript in telling a story, because in the story the interest is mainly in the action of the story, and consultation of text interferes with the maintenance of the story interest; moreover, in reading you must follow the text of your selection more closely than is required in narration, and the burden upon the memory if it were required to commit to memory everything read would be too great. 

13. Building the Reading Program. If you are called upon to give a complete reading recital of an hour or more, it is imperative that you organize your program with great care. The most artistic reading will often fail if the selections read are uninteresting to the audience. It will take a good deal of experience for you to know what will "go over" with the audience, and what will not. 

The following suggestions may help you until have had experience in testing the likes and dislikes of audiences.

1. Vary your program; read a variety of types of selection: dramatic, lyric, narrative, humorous. 

2. Be sure to have at least one selection that has a spiritual message, but avoid the "preachy" or obviously didactic selection. 

3. Don't make your selections too far over the heads of your audience; on the other hand, don't waste your time reading literary trash. 

4. Put first on your program a selection which has strong appeal to the interest of your auditorsgenerally a narrative where the interest is in the story. 

5. Close with a short number generally, and one with the quality of charm, if possible. 

13. Impersonation. At the begining of this chapter we listed among the forms of speech entertainment, in addition to those already discussed, impersonation and acting. 

It will suffice for the purpose of this discussion merely to describe each, as impersonation is seldom practiced for its own sake, but is generally a part of interpretative reading, and especially of acting; and acting is a whole art in itself, and can only be treated adequately in an extended discussion.

By impersonation is meant the representation of the character of someone other than the performer. As we said, in reading the

reader remains in his own person; he gives you the thought of the author as he understands it. In impersonating, the performer represents himself as someone else. He may or may not have the aid of costume and stage properties, but he will be likely to create them by suggestion, if he does not actually have them. 

14. Acting. In acting, the performer also goes out of his own person, and represents himself as someone else. He has the aid of costume and stage property, and he performs in company with other actors. In speaking lines, he communicates not directly with the audience, but with his fellow actors on the stage.

Acting differs from the other forms of speech entertainment discussed in this chapter in that it calls to its aid a number of arts other than the speech art, the arts employed in the creation of stage scenery, costuming, lighting, etc., as well as the art of dancing, and to some extent, music.

Chapter X. 

The After-Dinner Speech 1. An American Speech Form. Perhaps no form of platform address has received more cultivation in American than the afterdinner speech-if it can be called a form of platform address. America is the land of organization, and no organization but must have its annual banquet. Add to these annual banquets the testimonial dinners, the dinners launching community drives, banquets to foreign celebrities, etc., and we can understand why a celebrated after-dinner speaker like Chauncey Depew should have, in the city of New York alone, dinner speaking engagements five nights every week for many weeks on end.

In recent years there has been some tendency to deprecate the extensive cultivation of after-dinner speaking in America, from the fact that so many banqueters have suffered from long-winded and dull speakers. 

Nevertheless, no type of public speaking program offers finer opportunities for culti-

vating the graces of speech than does the after-dinner speech. And considering the fact that our makers of banquet programs have been in the habit, often, of inviting to speak those who were celebrities but untrained as speakers, it is remarkable rather that we have had so many good after-dinner speeches, than than there are so many bad ones. 

2. Building a Banquet Program. From what has just been said, you may infer correctly that the success of an after-dinner program depends largely upon the person who arranges the program. If that task falls to you, make sure of at least two things-first, that the program is kept within reasonable time limits; second, that your speakers are the best speakers that you can possibly get.

In regard to the first point, the time limit, it is safe to say in general that the program ought not to be allowed to run over an hour unless you have a very important occasion or very unusual talent for the program. most patient of audiences in these days of high-tension living will hardly endure the best of programs more than an hour. In re- The

gard to the speakers you put upon the program-two or three at the most, with the one most likely to make a sure-fire speech kept to the last. Except in the very informal affair, like a reunion at a fraternity, where the speeches are less important than the opportunity to hear "just a word" from old friends, don't yield to the common custom of calling for impromptu remarks from all the celebrities present. But perhaps this latter suggestion applies rather to the toastmaster, who may or may not be the person who arranges the program. 

3. Ingenuity. The builder of the pro gram can often help to assure a successful affair by bringing a little ingenuity to bear in arranging the program. Keep in mind that the main end of the after-dinner speech is entertainment. A clever assignment of subjects will often put both audiences and speakers in the right mood for a good program. Perhaps the cleverest after-dinner program in America are those put on annual ly by the Grid Iron Club of Washington. 

They usually take the form of a satiric review of public events and public characters. 

The nature of the gathering for the ban-

quet will often suggest a scheme for the assignment of topics. For instance, at a recent banquet of a college debating club, the program was made to parody an Oregon-style debate. One group sat at one table, to represent the affirmative, another group at another table to represent the negative, and a third group at a third table to represent a board of judges. Topics for the speeches were in the form or propositions and questions drawn to suggest the ridiculous. In this case there were more speeches than would usually be put upon the program, but all of them were limited by the chairman to two minutes. This was possible from the fact that the program was pretending to follow the conventions of debate, where strict time limit on speeches is enforced. 

3. The Toastmaster. Equally important to the success of the program with good speakers is a good toastmaster. Not infrequently the best of programs is spoiled by a blundering or inept chairman. Particularly do toastmasters err in talking too long and in trying to claim too much of the limelight. 

The toastmaster is not the chief speaker, and ought not to usurp the attention that belongs to the chief speaker.

4. Introducing the Speakers. The chief task of the toastmaster is to introduce the speakers. In order to do this properly, the toastmaster should know as much as possible about each speaker. He must know too, of course, the topic upon which the speaker is to speak. The length and nature of the introduction will depend on how intimately the chairman knows the speaker, and how well the speaker is known to the banqueters. It is proper to review the achievements of a speaker if they are not well-known to the audience, even to praise the speaker for his achievements, but do not flatter him. Flattery of a speaker is always more embarrassing than helpful to him. 

Chairmen sometimes err in imagining, where the tone of the meeting is one of joviality and gayety, that it is incumbent upon them to introduce each speaker with some joke or story at his expense. Such jokes are often far-fetched and dull. Better content yourself with a simple, dignified but informal presentation of the speaker. In referring to the subject upon which the speaker is to speak, be careful not to say anything that would embarrass the speaker, as by presupposing that he will take a certain position on

his topic. Do not make it necessary for him to contradict or qualify what you have said. 

Following is a typical brief introduction: "Ladies and Gentleman: It is my happy privilege to introduce next a speaker who has distinguished himself no less for scholarship and literary accomplishments than for meritorious public service. I present the 

Honorable James Morgan, sometime professor of Political Science in Vanderbilt University, author of a number of books and many magazine articles on subjects related to politics and public service, now representative in Congress from the 17th District of Pennsylvania. Mr. Morgan will speak on the topic What Price Patriotism.' Mr. Morgan." 

5. The After-Dinner Speech. It has been suggested that the purpose of the after-dinner speech is usually entertainment. This being true, it follows that the after-dinner speech is less concerned with logic and form than most platform speeches. It allows for wide variation as to content and method of treatment, but it must not be didactic or argumentative. It permits of the employment of wit, humor, allegory, parody, imagination; it may even be cast in the form of

verse, though perhaps that is exceptional. 

Above all things avoid being heavy or dull or too long-winded. James Russell Lowell once gave a recipe for an afer-dinner speech that is still worth quoting. It should contain, he said, a quotation, a joke, a platitude, then-stop. And the ability to know when to stop is important in the after-dinner speaker. 

6. Serious Treatment Sometimes Allowed. 

It happens not infrequently that the circumstances in which a banquet is held allow, and sometimes demand, serious treatment of serious subjects in the speeches. Some celebrated speeches in the history of oratory have been after dinner speeches. Grady's speech on "The New South," to which reference has been made (Chapter VI), was given at a banquet, as was George William Curtis's speech on "Liberty under the Law." Both were extremely serious speeches, and both had important consequences. Curtis's speech was thought by many to be responsible in large part for the solution of the problem that arose from the famous Hayes-Tilden election controversy of 1876. Yet both of these speeches approached the serious element with a humorous introduction.

It might be said that the better after-dinner speakers, even in speeches that are mainly humorous or of an entertaining nature, inject some serious ideas, a bit of their philosophy of life, especially in the concluding portions of the speech. 

7. Delivery of the After-Dinner Speech. 

The circumstances of a banquet program call generally for the intimate and personal conversational style of speaking. There is usually no occasion for elaborate and formal appeal to the emotions. But that does not mean that the speaker should be casual or careless in his speaking. All that has been said on technique of voice and action (Chapters II-III) should be remembered in the delivery of the after-dinner speech. Stand erect, facing your audience (the chairman will see to it that the speakers are so placed that they may face the audience); don't keep your hands in your pockets; don't lean over the back of your chair; don't toy with the glasses and the table silver. These are habits into which after-dinner speakers sometimes fall. Speak loudly enough so that you may be heard easily, but not too loudly to be intimate with your audience.

8. Addressing the Chairman. It should be observed that the title of the presiding officer at a banquet is "Toastmaster" whether it be a man or a woman-"Mr. Toastmaster," "Madam Toastmaster." The title "Madam" is used whether the lady presiding be married or single. There is no such word as "Toastmistress." Of course, it is always permissible to address the presiding officer as "Chairman," but "Toastmaster" is generally preferable. 

Speakers should address the Toastmaster at the opening of their speeches, though it is not improper, and sometimes effective, to precede the address by a quotation or an anecdote. Grady used that device effectively in "The New South." 

9. Poise in the After-Dinner Speaker. No quality is more imperative in the after-dinner speaker than the quality we call poise. 

The essence of poise is self-confidence and self-control. If the speaker is nervous, the audience is nervous in sympathy; if the speaker shows ease, the audience is likely to be at ease. This is especially true in the case of the after-dinner speaker, who stands very close to his audience, often in the midst of them.

It is, of course, possible to be nervous and yet conceal the signs of nervousness. You doubtless have heard the young speaker who has said, after the delivery of a speech, "I was frightened to death," but who had exhibited no appearance of fright. Every experienced speaker has many times felt fright and nervousness, yet managed to conceal the appearance of it. It manifests itself to the audience ordinarily in trembling of the hands, in unnecessary body movements, in quavering voice, and somtimes in voice failure. Often the taking of a deep breath will help to steady the nerves. Don't let yourself hurry through your speech, without due emphasis on the important ideas, for that also is a sign of nervousness. Above all things don't confess that you are frightened or nervous, for while that may win you the sympathy of those who like frankness, it stamps you at once as an inexperienced speaker. Keep your best foot foremost. 

10. Telling a Story. After-dinner speaking is often associated with the art of story telling, meaning by "story" a joke or a short humorous anecdote. Unfortunately some

after-dinner speakers tell entirely too many stories, stringing them together with little pretense at connection. While sometimes a story may be told for its own sake, without any pretense at connection, generally there should be some point to the story. Usually it is advisable to use not more than two of three in a speech; frequently a good one well told is better. If the story is told for its own sake, and not to illustrate a point, don't pretend that there is a connection. It is inane to say "That reminds me," when there is nothing to remind you of anything.

One of the first things to be said in connection with the telling of a story is that the teller must know a good story from a bad one. To be good, a story must not only have an amusing "point"; it must be fresh; it must be instantly intelligible; and the point must be properly placed so that there is not a long explanation to be made after the point is reached; in the best stories, the point comes at the very conclusion. 

To tell a story effectively, the teller must know the story thoroughly, so that he envisions all the necessary details from beginning to end. It ruins a story for the teller to have to say, "I forgot to say-". He

must be able to tell the story glibly, so that he keeps ahead of the thinking of the audience. Above all, he must know how to time his point accurately, giving it due emphasis.

If he hurries over the point, so that the audience misses it, the story falls flat. 

Let us take for example the old story of the gushing woman who came up to Eugene 

Field after one of his lectures. She exclaimed, "Oh, I'm so glad to meet the author of 'Little Breeches." "Excuse, me madam, but you've got the pants on the wrong man," said Field.

That is the essence of the story, but if it were told in that form, it would be unintelliible to the average audience, who could not be relied upon to know that John Hay, and not Eugene Field, wrote the poem "Little 

Breeches." This fact must be suplied in the telling of the story, but must precede the point, which should come as near as possible to the end of the story. In good telling the story would approximate the following form (an error of the chairman has been assumed, which would give occasion for the telling of the story): 

The Toastmaster's error in introducing me, a very natural error, reminds me of an error

once made by a gushing lady who was attending a lecture by Eugene Field, celebrated author of "Little Boy Blue" and other well known juvenile poems. After the lecture, the lady rushed up to Mr. Field, exclaiming, Oh, I'm so glad to meet the author of 'Little Breeches.'" a poem which was written, as you know, not by Eugene Field, but by John Hay. Mr. Field set her right by saying. "Excuse me, madam, but you've got the pants on the wrong man.” 

You will note that the point of this story comes in the words "pants on the wrong man," which in our second version of the story come at the very end. In the first version, they were followed by "said Field," which, though only two words, obscure the effect of the point. 

11. Apropos Stories. A good deal of the success of a story comes in its appropriateness, and is therefore a matter of chance. 

The good story teller frequently is the one who knows a great many different good stories, so that when occasion offers he can tell an appropriate one. You must know when not to tell a story, as well as when to tell it. 

The best story may fail, even with good tell-

ing, if there is no proper occasion for it.

12. Concluding the Program. The Toast master must know how to close a program gracefully. It is generally in order to express the thanks of the banqueters to the principal speaker or speakers of the program, but do it briefly and with as much of an air of sincerity as you can command. It is generally not wise to comment upon ideas expressed. At the conclusion of your brief remarks, simply announce the adjournment of the meeting.

Chapter XI  Types of Platform Address. 

Under the term platform address is generally included all kinds of public speeches in which there is a relatively greater degree of unity and perhaps more formality of preparation and delivery than are found in the after-dinner speech. Under the term are sometimes included sermons, political addresses, commencement addresses, chapel speeches, and various kinds of speeches of courtesy. Each of these various types of platform addresses has certain qualities peculiar to itself, and we shall consider the more general of them separately; but first there are certain qualities common to all of them which should be considered. 

2. A Central Idea. A relatively high degree of unity, we have said, is a characteristic of the platform address. Perhaps we ought to have said should be a characteristic, for many speeches fail because of a lack of unity. Necessary to unity in a

speech is a central idea, which is usually expressive of the purpose of the speech. That central idea should be capable of expression in the form of a proposition, that is, a declarative sentence. Suppose you want to give a chapel address on liberalism in politics. 

You can say a great many things on that subject, and unless you narrow the topic to a central idea, your speech is likely to have your audience wondering what you wanted to say, and why. Suppose, then, we agree to stress this central idea: there are two kinds of liberal, the dogmatic, and the reasonable, liberal, and the truly intelligent liberal is the reasonable liberal. This idea might then be developed by giving concrete examples of the two different kinds of liberal, with a contrast of them. 

3. Subordination. In addition to having a central idea, in order to have unity you must learn to subordinate; that is, keep your less important ideas contributory to your main ideas. This is done by giving more space to the more important ideas; by giving them the more prominent positions, at the beginning and at the conclusion; and especially by the use of partitions, internal and

concluding summaries, and by repetition. By partition is meant the announcement of the outline of the speech, made at the beginning of the speech. Thus Burke, at beginning of his "Speech on Conciliation with the American Colonies," tells us the three main divisions into which he proposes to divide the speech. By an internal summary is meant a paragraph in which you review what has been said in the section of the speech just preceding. A concluding summary reviews the whole speech. Both internal and concluding summaries review only the main ideas, thus making them stand out above the less important ideas. 

4. The Political Address. The political address has for purpose the advocacy of a candidate for office in whom the speaker is interested, or a cause which he hopes to see adopted. It is one of the most common and most important forms of platform address.

It wields a very great influence in the political life of the nation, but it is not confined to politics, in the common sense of the word. 

Ministers of the gospel use the political speech when they urge the election of candidates for church offices at assemblies and

conferences, and when they argue for certain politices of government of the church. 

You make a political speech when you get up in your class meeting in high school to plead for certain class policies. 

One principle that the young speech student needs to keep in mind in the political speech (as well as in many other types of speech), is that the purpose of the speech is to win supporters to your candidate or your project. You are wasting your time if you are speaking only to give relief to your anger or scorn, or to assail your opponents. Of course, in an audience composed already of members of your party, you may stir up enthusiasm by attacking the opposition party, but it is generally wise so to speak as to win to your side even your opponents, if there are any in your audience. This means that you must be conciliatory and pleasing, as well as logical and forceful. 

5. The Nominating Address. A special kind of political address much used in all types of organization in America is the nominating address. It is customary in the nominating address to follow a certain fixed mode of development, in which first is set

forth needs of the hour (which, if the party is out of power, is to save the country from the ruinous policies of the party in power: if the speaker's party is in power, to continue the glorious achievements of that party); secondly, the qualification of the candidate to be named are described; finally, the candidate is named. The withholding of the name of the candidate until the end is for the sake of climax and surprise. Both are usually fictions, and it is just as well to name the candidate earlier in the speech, especially if he happens to be the incumbent in office whom you are renominating.

Nominating addresses are frequently made too bombastic. Praise your candidate but don't "lay it on too thick." Sincerity and conviction are much more appealing than mere assertiveness. 

6. The Commemorative Address. A kind of platform address, and one which has furnished some of our finest examples of finished oratory, is the commemorative address. 

It takes many forms-dedicatory addresses, anniversary addresses (Fourth of July, Mem orial Day, etc.), commencement addresses, speeches at the unveiling of monuments, at

the laying of connerstones, etc. In most cases, the material of at least part of the speech is dictated by the occasion. That is, there must be due reference to the event that is being commemorated. A really able speaker, however, does not content himself with saying the obvious thing; he seizes the occasion to teach some great moral lesson, to transmit some message of lasting significance to the audience. Thus Charles Summer, speaking on the Fourth of July at the outbreak of the Mexican War, chose as his theme "The True Grandeur of Nations," and tried to impress the truth that not in military prowess, but in intellectual and moral achievements lies the true greatness of nations. Webster seized many of the great occasions of his commemorative speeches-the two Bunker Hill Addresses, the Plymouth 

Rock Address, the eulogy of Adams and

Jefferson-to inculcate the idea of solidarity and unity in the nation. . 

If you are called upon to deliver a commemorative address, ask yourself what ideas must be expressed because of the nature of the occasion; secondly, what opportunities are offered by the nature of the occasion to inspire or instruct your audience. When you

prepare your speech, you have to keep in mind both elements. 

7. The Chapel Speech. A type of plat form address in which the high school student should be especially interested is the chapel address. It is a regular feature of the instruction and machinery of management at most high schools, as well as at most colleges. Such speeches may have several purposes; some are religious or inspirational in nature, making a contribution to the spiritual needs of the students who are the audience; some are of the nature of lectures, dealing with with a wide variety of topics within the intellectual interests of the students-art, literature, science, travel, current events, etc.; some are familiar talks by the principal or a faculty member dealing with matters of school routine or the behavior of students; some are "pep" talks in connection with athletics or other school events. Outside speakers are frequently called in for chapel talks; others are given by the principal or faculty members; and in many schools the commendable practice is followed of occasionally calling upon seniors or juniors to lead the chapel program. In

many of the larger city high schools the high value of this speech activity is recognized in a special training period called the auditorium hour, in which the students are given practice in speaking and reading in the auditorium. 

If you are called upon to participate in or lead a chapel program, your first task is to decide what you shall do. Sometimes that problem is solved for you by an assignment from the teacher or officer in charge of the chapel program. If not, you must make your own decision. If you are to make a speech, select your topic to be as timely as possible; select one which is within your own interest and ability to treat. In developing it, be sure that you have a definite purpose in it, and a central idea. Make everything you say contribute to the growth of that central idea. Try to get an interesting opening and a snappy conclusion. Above all, make your speech concrete, not abstract. 

We shall talk of this element of concreteness more at length when we come to discuss illustrations.

What we have said thus far has to do with the material and organization of your chapel speech. On the head of delivery we shall

have some suggestions to make presently in the sections dealing with Types of Delivery. 

8. Speeches of Courtesy. A very common type of platform address with which every leader in school or community affairs must make an acquaintance sooner or later is the speech of courtesy. It is the speech of ceremony, in which your problem is to say as gracefully as possible what etiquette or tradition demands, rather than to develop ideas. Common examples of the speech of courtesy are the presentation speech, the acceptance speech, the speech of introduction, the speech of welcome, speeches at the performance of official duties. 

In spite of the fact that the content of the speech of courtesy is determined by the occasion, there is still opportunity for originality and ingenuity in the mode of expression. Obey the conventions in what you say, but avoid the stereotyped and the hackneyed in the way you say it. Gracefulness of language, which we discussed in connection with , is one of the chief reliances of the speaker who excels in the speech of courtesy. 

One of the best ways of acquiring a sense

of appropriateness and beauty of expression in speeches of courtesy it to study carefully good models of such speeches. You will find some excellent evamples in O' 

Neill's Modern Short Speeches, an inex pensive one-volume collection published by 

The Century Company. The study of models will be of the more value to you in this type of speech, because generally the speech of courtesy is so short that it can be carefully prepared in advance and delivered from memory without overburdening the memory or losing the sense of intimacy with the audience which is likely to result from memoriter delivery in a longer speech. In studying models, you must not slavishly copy, but you will gain many ideas of a more excellent way of speaking from the ingenuity and cleverness of masters of the art of public speaking. 9.

Illustrations. When we were discussing the chapel address, we gave the injunction to be concrete, not abstract. Concreteness means that quality in speech by which the speech makes itself instantly intelligible to the audience to which it is addressed. It means talking in terms which

the audience understands. It is a relative term. If we are talking to a group of scientists on a scientific topic, we can use scientific terms which to the ordinary person would be entirely abstract. For the great majority of people, to be concrete means to speak in terms of sensory experiences. We usually think in terms of sensory images-sights, sounds, tastes, smells, etc. If we can use language that provokes the recall of such images, we secure the attention of our hearers. That is why illustrations are of such vast importance in platform addresses. 

It is not too much to say that they are the very life blood of speeches. Listen to a skilled speaker who knows the value of good illustrations. Watch the audience and see how they react to him: he is explaining an intricate point of his thought, using abstract terms. The attention of his audience wayers, as indicated by a rustling and a murmur. Then he says, "Let me illustrate," and he enters upon a vivid bit of description or narration, and immediately a hush sweeps over the audience, and the listeners unconsciously lean forward in their seats. The skilled speaker knows this almost magical effect of concreteness, and he keeps the ab-

stract explanation of his speech to the minimum, and uses concrete illustrations to carry his thought. 

10. Sources of Illustrations. 

You ask, where shall I get illustrations? The answer is, if you wish to be a public speaker, you must cultivate the habit of collecting illustrations as a physician or a sociologist collects case histories. You pick up the sport page of a newspaper one day, and you read the casual story of a baseball game played between a team of policemen and a team of prisoners. The story tells how the result of the game turned upon a ninth inning decision of the umpire, who, unable to see the play, had appealed to one of the prisoner players. The latter unhesitatingly reports the truth of the play, though his truthfulness loses the game to his team. 

You say, what a magnificent illustration of the effect of a manly appeal to a sense of good sportsmanship, even in a criminal. Yes; and if you are wise, you promptly make a clipping, and file it away where you can find it again, in a looseleaf notebook or a card file, and you have an illustration which may come to your assistance many times.

Of course, there are books of illustrations, but they are never of as much value to a speaker as those he has collected for himself, from his reading, from conversations, from his own observation and experience of life. Those which you collect for yourself have a quality of reality, of truth and vitality, which illustrations taken from collections are likely to lack. You must collect illustrations assiduously; better still, store your mind with them, so that you can recall them without the labor of going to your files. 

They will give compelling power to your speeches. 

11. Types of Delivery. The kind of de livery you intend to use in your speech will influence to some extent the nature of your preparation. There are three kinds of delivery commonly used: extempore, memoriter, and manuscript delivery. 

12. Extempore Speaking. We have already described extempore speaking in Chapter V, in which we dealt with Thinking on Your Feet. It is speaking in which there may be any degree of preparation except the selection of the language in which the thought is to be expressed. That is left to the spur of the moment.

Generally for all purposes extempore speaking is the most effective kind of speaking, because through it the speaker can maintain closer contact with the audience than he can in other types of speaking. However, there are some dangers in extempore speaking. One is the danger of wordiness, of repeating oneself and consuming too much time in the expression of an idea. Another is the danger of using a loose style. One can phrase his thought more felicitously when he is sitting at his desk writing than when he is speaking on his feet. But if the speaker trains himself to avoid these two dangers, he will find that for most speeches, extempore speaking is best. 

13. Memoriter Speaking. In memoriter speaking, the speech is carefully written out, committed to memory, and delivered from memory. It has the advantage of making it possible for the speaker to use his very best rhetoric, but several great disadvantages. In the first place, it is very difficult in memoriter delivery to keep a close and intimate contact with the audience. The speaker is striving to remember, rather than thinking out toward the audience, and the result

is a certain evenness of voice melody, rate, and force which makes for monotony and dissipates the attention of the audience. 

Moreover, the burden of committing to memory many speeches, except very short speeches, is very exhausting. The speaker who speaks much must learn to avoid dependence upon memorized speeches. 

14. Manuscript Delivery. There are those who would say that manuscript delivery is not really public speaking, but merely reading. It may be admitted that it is much abused, but still there are some circumstances in which it is permissible to read from a manuscript, as in very important speeches, like the President's inaugural address, where every word must be weighed. 

Moreover, if the speaker is a trained speaker and is thoroughly familiar with the thought of his speech, it is possible for him to use a manuscript and still make an effective presentation, with the manuscript interfering very little with the directness of his contact with the audience. However, that being said, it is only fair to warn the beginning speaker against dependence upon a manuscript. If in your first speeches you

feel that you must have a manuscript, make sure that you use it no more than absolutely necessary. and wean yourself from the use of a manuscript as soon as possible. 

15. Combination of Syles. Many speakers who are effective extemporizers nevertheless prepare certain passages of their speeches, in which they wish to use especially effective rhetoric, and commit those passages to memory, delivering the rest of the speech extempore. It they can preserve a conversational style of delivery throughout, so that the whole appears to be spontaneous and direct, that method of delivery may be very effective. John Bright is said to have practiced it regularly.

Chapter XII  1. Growing Popularity of Speech Contests. 

In recent years, speech contests of various kinds, and particularly debate, have come to occupy a very important part of the speech education program in our American schools. 

In the decade preceding 1929, state after state organized a state-wide high school debating league. Expansion of debate activity among the colleges had come even earlier. And even since the coming of the depression that followed 1929, speech contests in the schools have nearly held their own, and indeed, in some cases have expanded. 

There has been an interesting development, for instance, in extempore speaking and 

Shakespearean Reading contests sponsored by many of the colleges as a part of their program of interesting students in coming to college. The annual contests of the National Forensic League, the high school speech fraternity, have been expanding in size and scope. In the convention of that

organization held this year* at Kent, Ohio, there were teams of high school students present from nearly every state in the union. 

Contests were held in debate, extempore speaking, oratory, and dramatic reading. 

Another recent expansion in speech contests is in the development of state-wide and sometimes nation-wide speech contestsdeclamation or oratory contests-in the interest of propaganda for various causes. Ex amples are the annual Prince of Peace De clamation Contest conducted among high school students by the Ohio Council of 

Churches, and the Peace Oratory Contest among the colleges of the various states.

2. Importance of Preparation for Con tests. These various speech contests are of significance educationally only as they promote better speech in the contestants and perhaps a consciousness of good speech in the nation as end results. The contest element supplies the motive for a good deal of hard work in training, that might not otherwise be done, and there is the value of the contests. Contestants want to win, naturally, and they soon learn that they have * 1935.

little chance to win, whatever their native abilities, unless they dig in and make real preparation. But the real importance of the preparation from an educational standpoint, like the importance of the contest itself, is in the training received in the course of preparation, not in the winning or losing of the contest. 

3. Preparation in Debate. We shall dis cuss in some detail, with a view to offering practical suggestions of value to the coach and the contestant, the methods of preparation for the various types of speech contests which we have mentioned, and inasmuch as debate is still the most general type of contest, we shall begin with that.

Let us assume that the question for debate has been chosen, the debate squad selected, and arrangements made for a meetof the squad at least an hour twice or three times a week. In the final stages of preparation, meetings will frequently require an hour and a half or two hours. 

The first thing that will be done in the squad will be to make as thorough a study of the question as time will allow. Members of the squad, under the instruction of the

coach, should make a bibliography on the question, unless you are fortunate enough to have a librarian do that for you. Then every member of the squad should read as widely on the question as possible. At first the discussion in the squad meetings will be in the nature of reports of reading, followed by impromptu discussions of the significance of such reports. As soon as possible the question will be analyzed by the whole class working together, until there is some agreement on what constitutes the issues on the question. By issues is meant those main questions, divisions of the proposition which constitutes the subject for debate, upon the answer to which will depend the solution of the main question. 

4. Notes and Cards. It should be impressed strongly upon debaters that while they are reading on the question, they should be collecting notes, quotations, data, etc., with careful indication of the source of all material collected. Always note the name of the author, the title of the book or magazine,the volume and page of every note taken. Students are frequently careless about this, with the result that when they

wish later to give exact statistics or an exact citation for some evidence offered in a speech, they are unable to do so. Of course when you are advancing an argument, you need not always cite volume and page, but you should always be able to do so if challenged.

In addition to collecting general facts, statistics, etc., on the question, debaters should systematically prepare rebuttal cards which will cover every possible argument that can be advanced upon the opposing side, as well as your own arguments. That is, your rebuttal cards will contain evidence and outlines of arguments to support your own case; and outlines of refutation for all the arguments which you will discover are advanced on the opposing side. 

Use the kind and size of card you find convenient for these notes; a 5"x8" card is recommended. 

5. Practice Speeches. After several ses sion of the class have been used for reports on readings, and discussion of the reports, the members of the class will start making speeches. It is a good plan for the coach to allow members of the squad to choose the

side upon which they wish to argue, but assign them contentions to defend on their side of the question. Every member of the class should thus practice making speeches on all the contentions of his case. By contention is meant a main assertion on one side of the question, which is supported by evidence and argument; by case is meant all the contentions on one side. 

6. Practice Debates. Practice in making individual speeches will be followed soon by practice debates, at first without rebuttal, later with rebuttal. Thereafter, until the contest debate for which preparation is being made is held, there will be one practice debate after another. Each will be followed by criticism from the coach and discussion by the squad. At first these practice debates will be held before the squad only; later if possible they will be held before real audiences, as often as possible. Criticism by the coach at first will pertain to arguments, evidence, analysis of the question, etc. Later criticism will be more concerned with technique of speaking, voice, pronunciation, action on the stage, and finally, learning how to hold the attention and interest of the audience.

7. Writing Speeches. It should be the aim in debating, whether in high school or college, to train the debaters to speak extempore, with little or no dependence on notes. However, to achieve this aim, most students will be required to write many speeches. Speeches should be written, however, not to be read from manuscript or committed to memory, but to force the writer to think through his argument carefully, and to train him in the judicious selection of language. When he delivers his argument, he will follow more or less closely the written speech, even though he is not attempting to remember it verbatim. 

8. Rebuttal Practice. Training in debate should emphasize the idea that a good debate should be a clash from beginning to end.

Every speech after the first should contain refutation as well as constructive argument. 

The rebuttal speeches, supposedly devoted entirely to refutation, should do two things: they should attack the arguments of the opposing side; they should reinforce arguments on your side that have been attacked. 

And while doing both, they should be as unified as possible.

The only way to achieve skill in rebuttal, or, more broadly, in refutation, is by practice, practice, practice, coupled with intelligent criticism. 

9. Preparation for the Oratory Contest. 

In preparing for the oratory contest, the first thing necessary is to select the subject of your oration, as that is not done for you, as in the case of debate. Choose a subject that deals with some live modern problem, whether of government, of international relations, of sociology or economics. It must be a topic that permits emotional appeal, for your success in the oratory contest depends much more upon emotional appeal than in debate. 

10. Writing the Oration. Having selected your topic, your next task is to write the oration, assuming, of course, that you have studied the subject upon which you are to write sufficiently to give you a conviction on it. In writing, you must use your own best style. Literary finish, like emotional appeal, is desirable in the oration. But do not make the mistake of borrowing the style of someone else. It will sound hollow when you speak if you do so. Especially do not borrow

the language of someone else, unless you acknowledge it. An oration should not be, as someone has put it, a mosaic of stolen gems. 

In treating your subject, you should endeavor to deal with it in a constructive manner. Don't think that you must offer a new and novel solution to a problem that may be incapable of any complete solution; but on the other hand it is not sufficient only to state the problem. You must at least point in the direction of a solution.

In method of treatment, above all things be concrete. Search long for good illustrations. Year after year oratory contests are won by graphic illustrations, coupled with fair or good delivery. Try especially to have an arresting introduction and an effective conclusion. Several years ago one of the college oratory contests was won with an oration which began, "I stand before you a prisoner condemmed. I am condemmed to die in the next war." The next year that oration, with eight or ten others, was used as a declamation in a state-wide contest. 

Participants in the declamation contest had freedom of choice among the orations used as declamations. About eight out of ten in

the state chose the oration beginning, “I stand before you a prisoner condemned." It was an exceedingly challenging introduction.

A device frequently used with good effect to get both a good introduction and a good conclusion, together with a sense of unity in the oration, is to start with an illustration that compels attention, and then have a return reference to it in the conclusion. 

11. Preparing the Delivery. As soon as the oration is written, it must be committed to memory. There must be no half-way business about this; memorization must be perfect. You cannot deliver effectively if you are stumbling for words. 

Practice in delivering the oration can begin even before the speech is committed, and indeed, had better begin before it is committed, for under good teaching, correct methods of delivery, correct inflections, pauses, etc., can be committed to memory along with the words. In training for delivery, nothing can be substituted for long and careful practice under good criticism. 

Melody, voice quality, force, tempo must all be made correct for the thought expressed.

After the necessary practice in delivery has been gone through, before your teacher or other critic alone, it is a good thing to practice before a speech class, inviting general criticism from members of the class. 

You will get some extremely helpful criticism. Then, if possible, try out your oration before real audiences, and observe how they react to it. 

12. Climaxes.

As a final suggestion, I would urge attention to the element of climax in your speech. This demands attention both in the writing of the oration, and in its delivery. You ought to have one major and several subordinate climaxes in an oration, where you have an intensity of emotional appeal that will grip the audience. 

Winning orations are not flatlands of sustained excellence; they observe the principle of subordination, which we discussed in 

Chapter XI. They have relief: high peaks of emotional appeal, set off by lower passages of calmer and more reasoned discourse. 

13. Preparation for the Extempore Contest. Success in the extempore speaking contest depends chiefly upon the ability to organize material logically and to speak con-

versationally and directly, together, of course, with the ability to extemporize smoothly. These are just the qualities which are developed by good debate training; hence training for the extempore speaking contest should be similar to training for debate. First study the question as widely as possible; then practice in outlining speeches upon various phases of the question. Practice extemporization of such outlined speeches, either to yourself in your own room, or, better, before your teacher. As in the extempore contest you cannot have a set speech, there will be no need and no opportunity to have the specific training in the technique of delivery which is a part of the preparation for the oratory contest. 

14. Preparation for the Reading Contest. 

The process of preparing for participation in the interpretative or dramatic contest is similar to the preparation for the oratory contest. There must be intensive training in the technique of voice and action, with perfect memorization. Of course, you do not have the problem of writing your selection, as in the oratory contest, but you have the equally important problem of making a

Judges in reading contests are supposed to rate only the excellence of reading, and not the selection, but it goes without saying that a good selection has a better chance to win than a poor selection. 15.Practice before Real Audiences. there is any secret of preparation for success in any kind of speech contest, it is painstaking and thorough preparation, started six weeks at least before the contest (longer for debate), and as much practice as possible before real audiences. Only by such practice can the participant in a contest acquire the sense of ease and familiarity with an audience, which are essential to his most effective work. In the final stages of training and practice, contestants should try to get away from remembering the criticism on technique of delivery which have been made by the coach. Try to get into the spirit of communication with the audience. Otherwise your delivery is likely to sound mechanical. Practice before real audiences, in

advance of the date of the contest, will help you to acquire this sense of communication, and is therefore very desirable.
