INTRODUCTION General Considerations Affecting the Teaching of Israel in Canaan SPIRIT OF THE TEACHING 

Israel in Canaan* is a textbook written for children who are on approximately the seventh grade level in their public school work. It constitutes a comparatively enlightened and mature treatment of biblical history for boys and girls who have reached the period of adolescence. The author of the book approaches biblical history in the spirit that a final and definitive story of the biblical period cannot at present be written. Nor for that matter is it ever likely to be written. The teacher will, of course, take this factor of tentativeness into account in her approach to the teaching problem. 

Pupils must adopt a critical, open-minded attitude toward matters that are not accurately known and, therefore, subject to difference of opinion. They should be granted opportunities for frank and free discussion. 

At the same time, what is traditional in biblical history must be approached in a respectful and reverent spirit. We must constantly bear in mind that what the Jews thought about their early history is perhaps as important as the very facts of that history.† Our *In this connection read Ahad Ha'am's essay on Moses found in his Selected Essays, pp. 306-329. † Golub, Jacob S., Israel in Canaan, Union of American Hebrew Congregations, Cincinnati, O. 1

attitude must, consequently, be one of interpretation and supplementation of the biblical account rather than one of disruptive and unsympathetic criticism. 

THE CONTENT OF THE COURSE 

In teaching biblical history in the past, little attempt was made to distinguish between history and literature, between fact and hypothesis, between truth and myth. 

Emphasis was placed almost exclusively on the military, political, or religious phases of history. Textbooks in biblical history concerned themselves very largely with such matters as the rise and fall of kings, the bloody work of military heroes, and the abstract visions and ideas of the prophets.

The textbook upon which this course is based, Israel in Canaan, attempts to approximate more closely the canons of good history set up by reputable, scientific historians. Thus, for example, a great deal of emphasis is placed upon the social, economic, and cultural phases of history. In this connection, attention of the reader is called especially to Sections I and VI entitled respectively, "Who Were Our Earliest Ancestors?" and 

"How Did Settled Life Change Our Ancestors?". 

These chapters constitute essentially a sociological analysis of the cultural and economic conditions existent in Israel at two different stages of its history. In addition, at no point in the textbook is there to be found any indulgence in mythological or supernatural explanations of human phenomena. Pupils are taught to think of biblical history in human terms and are sup-

ORGANIZATION OF THE COURSE 

It will be noted that the course is organized in a series of large units or topics each of which is aimed at some fundamental understanding. The pupil is not presented with a vast array of isolated and unrelated facts that serve no particular purpose and contribute nothing to intelligent apprehension of important ideas. Instead all facts given are intended to aim at some comprehensive and broad understanding. Thus, the pupils are introduced to such topics as "Who Were Our Earliest 

Ancestors?" and "How Did Israel Become a United 

Kingdom?" Details and facts are presented only in connection with developing certain important concepts or generalizations. The pupils are asked to engage in numerous activities-reading, exercises of various kinds, oral discussions, map work, drawing, collecting pictures, dramatization, visiting museums, etc.—all with the express purpose of making them intelligently appreciative of some of the major aspects of early 

Hebrew history. 

THE TEACHING OF THE TOPIC 

Each topic is, generally speaking, to be taught in accordance with a definite plan that is to some extent modeled after a procedure originally proposed and developed by Professor H. C. Morrison of the University

of Chicago.* The procedure embraces the following steps: 

A. EXPLORATION PERIOD 

As a form of introduction to the new topic, the class is given a preliminary oral quizzing on material that may constitute a part of the apperceptive background of the pupils and that may, at the same time, be relevant to an understanding of the unit about to be studied. Such tying up of the familiar with the unfamiliar may serve as a contributory factor in motivating the study of the new unit. In addition, the pre-test will serve as a basis for the teacher in directing the study of the unit. 

B. PRESENTATION PERIOD 

The teacher presents the new topic through a brief talk lasting not more than ten or fifteen minutes. The purpose of this presentation is to acquaint the class with the major aspects of the topic or problem that is to be studied. The presentation is really the story of the topic briefly told. It serves to give the pupil a sort of bird's-eye view of the topic in its entirety and to orient him for his further study.

Through this and the preceding step an interest in the content of the topic is to be aroused. The pupils are led to see the importance of engaging in the study and to develop a real desire to do so.

D. ORGANIZATION PERIOD 

This step consists of the cooperative development of an outline of the topic by the entire class. The purpose here is to give the pupils an opportunity to think through the topic in its entirety and to organize it in their own terms. All outlines are, of course, to be copied into the pupils' loose-leaf notebooks. 

Needless to say, the pupils must be trained gradually for development of skill in outlining. For that reason it is suggested that for the outline of the first topic a very simple procedure be used and that the process be made gradually more complex as the pupils progress from topic to topic. 1. The teacher writes on the blackboard a list of the major points that are to go into the outline. These points are written in confused order. The children then arrange the points in the correct order by assigning the proper numbers to them. 

2. The pupils themselves suggest the major points that are to be included and then arrange them in the correct order as indicated above.

6. Each pupil constructs an outline of his own. He has individual conferences with the teacher who guides him through the process.

E. RECITATION PERIOD 

The Recitation is essentially the retelling of the presentation but this time by the pupil in his own terms. As a result of all his study during the Assimilation Period the pupil has had an opportunity to make the topic definitely his own. Through the Recitation he expresses himself about the topic; he tells the story in terms in which he conceives it. 

Needless to say the pupil must receive training in this phase of the study activity as well. The basis for the recitation is supplied through the outline that is developed during the Organization Period. At first the outline is left on the board and the pupils make use of it as they recite. Pupils are called upon one at a time and are asked to develop briefly one or two

points. The other pupils in the class give corrections or additions after the speaker has finished but under no circumstances do they interrupt him while he is speaking.

As the pupils progress from topic to topic they may be expected to increase the length of their recitations by covering an ever increasing part of the outline. By the time the fourth or fifth topic is reached they should be able to tell the entire story of the topic with the aid of just the few main points jotted down on a card. 

The story can be repeated three or four times by as many pupils. The other members of the class make necessary corrections or additions. The pupils who are not called upon to recite on a particular topic might also be asked to write up their recitations in the form of a written composition based on the outline.

A. WIDE AND ABUNDANT READING 

At the end of each textbook chapter, as well as in every assignment, definite provision is made for additional reading over and beyond what is required in the text itself. The purpose here is to supply the pupils with as deep and rich an understanding of the particular

It is important that the pupil become aware of the differences in emphasis and point of view assumed by different writers in interpreting certain phases of biblical history. They should be made cognizant, also, of the difficulties involved in attempting to reconstruct a picture of a period of Jewish history that remains, of necessity, vague and shrouded in mystery because of the paucity of records that we have in our possession. 

As one phase of the child's supplementary reading activity, the references to the Bible or to the Bible Readers are especially significant. Rabbis and teachers have in the past constantly deplored the fact that our children remain forever ignorant of the most noteworthy achievement of the Jewish people in the realm of religious thought and literature, namely, the Bible. 

It is fortunate that the teaching procedure outlined above makes it possible for at least the brighter and more ambitious members of the class to read a considerable amount of Bible material. It makes little difference whether the reading is done in the Bible itself or in the Bible Readers that were originally planned expressly for young people. Probably the very brightest and ablest pupils will be able to manipulate the Bible and read it with understanding. For the rest the Readers will serve an almost identical purpose. It is not the intention of the writer in this connection to advocate that all study of the Bible should partake of the nature of reading supplementary to the

study of history. Even if desirable, it is virtually impossible to introduce certain portions of the Bible as collateral history reading material. But certainly such reading will serve as an additional device for acquainting our children with a field of literature which it is our sincere aspiration they understand and appreciate.

Frequent use is made of such exercises as completion, true-false, multiple-choice, and matching tests. Nor is it necessary to discuss the advantages of these tests as compared with the older type of test that has been commonly used in most schools.

It is important that the teacher realize at the very outset that the task of conducting a course in biblical history in accordance with the procedure outlined above is no easy one. The work can be successful only if the teacher devotes himself to it in truly conscientious fashion. It is essential, to begin with, that the teacher should be thoroughly familiar with the materials of instruction. He should make certain that he himself knows the answers to the various questions that are propounded to the pupil. In this connection

He should know how to guide the pupil in the various types of supplementary activity that are suggested to him. Conscientiousness in looking over the pupil's Workbook, notebook, and tests, is also urged as an essential aspect of the teaching procedure. This analysis of the pupil's work will result in the accumulation of considerable evidence concerning the process of learning as it goes on in the individual pupil. Such evidence is necessary if the teacher's guidance of each individual in the class is to be truly intelligent and productive of results.

It is important also to watch the pupils carefully while they are engaged in their study activities. Only in this way can the teacher discover the nature of the difficulties with which his pupils are beset. EXPLORATION: INTRODUCING THE STUDY OF BIBLICAL HISTORY 

The course begins with a preliminary developmental discussion which is to constitute a general motivation for engaging in the study of Jewish history and, more specifically, biblical history. A few questions such as the following are propounded to the class:

How many Jews do you think there are in the world at the present time? (16,000,000.)

In what countries are Jews to be found? (U. S., Palestine, Russia, Poland, Roumania, France, England, other countries of Europe, China, S. Africa, etc.) 

By comparison with others, do you consider the Jews a large or small people? (Small.)

Are the Jews of any importance in the affairs of the modern world? (Of very great importance.)

Name some famous Jews of America. 

Name some Jews who have a world-wide reputation.

Through these questions the class is led to see that at the present time Jews the world over occupy very

prominent positions in many fields of achievement. 

They have their eminent musicians, scientists, thinkers, religious leaders, and statesmen. 

But it is to be further indicated by the teacher that not only in the present do the Jews figure prominently but that they have played an important role throughout the history of the world, ancient, medieval, and modern. They lived in the days when Egypt and 

Babylonia, Greece and Rome were at the height of their glory, and when the nations of the present-day world first came into being. In the middle ages Jews were among the prominent teachers of Europe; they occupied positions as leading physicians and philosophers. In ancient times the Jews wrote some of the world's best literature, the Bible. They gave to the world the prophets whose teachings are now accepted by all the great civilized peoples. Christianity and Mohammedanism received a great deal from Judaism. 

It is assumed that by this time the pupils have become somewhat interested in the history of the Jewish people and are eager to learn more about it. The teacher now proceeds to point out that the Jews were not always a great people but that, on the contrary, they had very humble beginnings. However, though still on a rather primitive level when the ancient civilizations of Egypt and Babylonia had already reached their height, the Jews were able not only to overtake but even to outstrip them. By the time of the prophets the Jews had, at least in certain respects, left the other civilizations of antiquity far behind.

The teacher now proceeds with questions of the following kind: 

Does any one know where our earliest ancestors came from?

What kind of people were our ancestors when we first hear of them? 

What sort of life did they lead? 

Have you ever heard of Abraham? Do you know any stories about him? 

What kind of life did he lead? 

The last four or five questions asked will probably lead to some fragmentary information that will be neither accurate nor specific. It is assumed, however, that the teacher has succeeded in arousing the curiosity of the class to the point where they are eager to hear an organized account of the origins of the Hebrew people that will be both interesting and informative. In other words, the class has now reached the stage where it is ripe for the teacher's presentation.

PRESENTATION 

In the very early days, when some of the famous empires of the ancient world, such as Egypt and Babylonia, were already at the height of their glory, our

ancestors were still a humble primitive people. At that time they did not lead the settled life of a people living in its own country but wandered about from place to place in the vast stretches of the Arabian Peninsula. They were nomads and did not know what it meant to live in one place for any long period of time. 

When the bit of pasture and the spring of water in the oasis gave out, they were forced to pull up their stakes, gather together their belongings, and move on to new camping grounds that offered some hope of a better livelihood.

Life was hard in the desert. Food was scarce and consisted mostly of bread made of barley flour and dairy products. Water was even more scarce, and even the small amount that existed had to be fought for with other thirsty wandering tribes. Warfare was frequent, for often it was necessary to attack a passing caravan in order to obtain a supply of food. 

Nomads lived in tents which, besides housing the family, sometimes served as a shelter for animals as well. The furnishings of the tent were few and very crude. Mats of straw or goat's hair served as beds at night and as seats in the day time. A round piece of leather served as a table.

In the tent the women did most of the work which consisted of cooking, baking, and weaving. They ground the flour too, and carried the water, and sometimes even tended the flocks. As their share of the work the men did most of the sheep tending, sheared the wool, or slaughtered the animals. They tanned the

hides and made of them sandals, straps, and bottles for water and wine. In addition, they made bows, arrows, and slings.

The nomads were organized into clans which were really large families consisting of a number of smaller families who were descendants of the same ancestor. 

The head of the clan was the Patriarch or the father. 

The eldest son of the chief wife was next in importance. Then there were the father's wives and their children, the grown sons and their wives and children, the sons' married sons and their wives and children, uncles, aunts, cousins, and finally the slaves. The Patriarch was the supreme ruler of the clan and even had the power of sentencing an offender to death. He acted as judge in quarrels, as priest at family gatherings, and as chief in war and peace. The men of the clan were more important than the women and met together in council to discuss all important undertakings. 

In the law of the desert clan, might was right. 

Fighting was common. The men of one clan aimed to protect their own kin and felt responsible for one another. Therefore, if one of their number was killed by a member of another clan, the entire clan would seek revenge by killing some member of the enemy clan.

Within the clan, too, the laws and customs were for the most part severe. When one member of the clan injured another the former was injured in exactly the same way. Thus, one who knocked out a tooth of another would have his own tooth knocked out. But the nomads were not always cruel. They

would even go so far as to protect a stranger, to feed him, and grant him shelter, provided he came to their tent and asked only for food or lodging. 

The religion of the nomads was simple and crude. In the desert people believed in spirits. Spirits were good and bad. They would do harm but they could also bring good luck. They lived in oases, springs, rocks, or trees. To keep on good terms with them one had to bring them food or drink. The dead, too, were looked upon as spirits and were to be worshipped and feared. 

A special group of men, called magicians or soothsayers, were believed to have power over the spirits. 

By muttering certain sounds or by performing certain acts and dances they could prevent the spirits from doing evil. Only a special magician, or witch doctor, could cure a person from sickness. Amulets or charms were also thought of as being able to prevent bad luck. 

Some of our ancestors, who were only partly nomadic and who were influenced in their religious ideas by the settled peoples near whom they lived, believed not only in spirits but in other gods as well. These gods were looked upon as great chiefs who were more powerful than their present ruler. The gods were, however, not pictured as human chiefs but rather as animals or as human figures with the heads of animals. They were thought to live on the tops of mountains, in sacred groves, in wells of water, or in large solitary stones. Sacrifices and gifts were brought to them, and at the religious gatherings that took place out in the open air, singing, dancing, games, and feasts

were a part of the ceremonies. Sometimes when a nomad wished to have his god near at hand, he would make a picture or likeness of the god. These likenesses, now known to us as idols, were believed to possess the magic powers of the god.

We have now completed what we think is a picture of our earliest ancestors. Of course, we are not certain that they really were exactly as they have been described. Our ancestors did leave us some information about themselves, but, of course, this information is not at all complete. The only written record that we have of these early days is the set of stories told by word of mouth for hundreds of years until they were finally written down in the Bible. Some of these stories found in the Bible lead us to believe that our ancestors of old were very similar in their beliefs and ways of living to desert-dwellers of the present day. But we cannot be certain that these stories that have come down to us are altogether accurate. For when a story was told and retold by the Patriarch or some professional story teller it was probably changed a great deal. And a story may have been told for a thousand years or more before it was finally written down in the form in which we have it today. What the Bible stories tell us about our earliest ancestors may be generally true. But they do not tell us enough nor do they relate events exactly as they took place. You will be much interested to know what the author of our textbook has to say about these early tales that are found in the Bible.

What the teacher will do under given circumstances will depend a good deal upon the type of activity that she can direct most competently. At the same time they have an opportunity to do some independent thinking about events that have actually taken place in history but about which they have as yet been given no real data. Take, as a further example, the discussion of Topic F, "Why is a blood feud a bad thing? Do we have any examples of it in our presentday world?" When the Assimilation Period is over the class proceeds to work on its developmental outline. Again the pupils have an opportunity to review the material of Topic I, this time in its entirety. While the outline is being developed, the children should be free to give reasons why they think it necessary to include one point in the outline in preference to another, why point number one should precede point two, etc. It is recommended that for the first outline the teacher make use of the simplest device among those suggested in the Introduction to this teacher's book. This device would call for the teacher's listing on the blackboard the major points that are to go into the outline. These points are written in confused order and it is then left to the pupils to arrange them in their proper sequence by assigning proper numbers to them. The teacher might list on the board the following major points of Topic I:

1. The Hebrews were latecomers in the family of ancient nations. Egypt and Babylonia had reached the height of their glory before the Jewish nation was born. 

2. Our ancestors were probably nomads who came out of the Arabian desert. 

3. Our ancestors were organized into clans which were really large families consisting of a number of smaller families who were descendants of the same ancestor. 

4. The clans had a type of government of their own with the Patriarch as the supreme ruler. 

5. The clans had their special laws and customs which were for the most part quite severe. 

6. Clan life in the desert was very hard. Obtaining food and drink was a very difficult task. 

7. Our ancestors had strange ideas about God, their religion being very simple and crude. 

8. Our ancestors worshipped in ways that were very different from our own. 

9. Our ancestors believed in magic and superstition. 

10. Our ancestors had certain beliefs about their own origins that were later written down in the Bible. 

It will be observed that the main points of Topic I are listed above in a possible correct sequence. This is, of course, not the order in which they should be written on the board for the children to rearrange. It is important to note also that there is not necessarily only one correct sequence. Provided the children work out some sort of logical arrangement, the results should

be considered satisfactory. An orderly but free giveand-take should be permitted among the pupils while each advances his reasons for placing the points in a particular order. The final arrangement should be arrived at, however, with the consensus of the entire pupil opinion. The outline agreed upon should, in the end, find its way into the pupil's notebook.

EXPLORATION: INTRODUCING THE STUDY OF TOPIC II 

The study of Topic II might well begin with a consideration of the holiday with which practically all Jewish children are familiar, namely, the Passover. 

This approach is recommended as being in consonance with the principle that good teaching always proceeds from the known to the unknown, that it commences by drawing attention to phenomena with which the pupil has already had some amount of contact. Besides, familiar experiences not directly connected with the work of the school are especially effective in arousing the interest of the pupil when referred to by the teacher in the course of a lesson. The teacher's questions, resulting from the approach suggested, might be conceived of as taking the following form:

How many of you have heard of the holiday of Passover? 

Do you observe the holiday in your home? 

How do you observe the holiday?

Do you know the reason for the celebration of Passover?

Beginning at this point, the teacher is able to question his class in some detail regarding the content of the topic that is about to be studied. The attempt here should be to draw out from the pupils as much information as they possess concerning the Israelites in Egypt and the Exodus. Doubtless the pupils will remember some of the related Bible stories familiar to them as a result of previous study. The story of

Egyptian civilization, too, will not be totally unknown to the pupils, since the history of the ancient world is at present taught in a great many elementary public schools. The questions that would be put to the class (beginning from the point where we left off above), might be the following: 

How did the Israelites get to Egypt? 

How did they become slaves in Egypt?

Were the Egyptians, during the period that we are studying, more or less civilized than the Israelites?

What were some of the great achievements of the 

Egyptians at this time?

Do you think that the Israelites became as civilized as their Egyptian neighbors during the period that they lived in Egypt? 

How were the Israelites freed from slavery?

For what great achievements in the history of the 

Israelites was Moses responsible?

Of what importance was the Exodus in the history of the Jewish people?

As a result of the exploration suggested, the teacher will have a fairly good conception of what his pupils already know concerning the story of the Israelites in Egypt and the Exodus. His teaching of the topic will consequently be guided by the knowledge thus derived. 

He is now in a position to determine what must be stressed in the teaching of the topic and what must not. His presentation which now follows will, of course, be colored considerably by what he has learned from the exploration. This presentation might con-

ceivably take the following form, but it must be remembered that each teacher must work out a presentation of his own that will be suited to his peculiar class. 

PRESENTATION 

According to the Bible story, the Israelite clans came to Egypt because of a famine in Canaan (probably about 1350 B.C.E.). There they lived in a special district assigned to them known as Goshen. They probably mingled very little with their Egyptian neighbors, and, therefore, did not learn much about the great civilization that the Egyptians had by this time built up. For the Egyptians already knew how to write, how to build magnificent buildings and temples, how to paint beautiful pictures, and how to do a great many other important and useful things, all of which the Israelites at this time knew very little about. 

The Israelites appear to have lived rather happily in 

Egypt until the great Pharaoh, Rameses II, came to the throne. This Pharaoh, enriched as a result of the conquests of the Pharaohs who ruled before him, decided to become a great builder. Since he needed workers, he decided to make use of all foreigners living in his kingdom as well as prisoners of war and native Egyptian serfs and slaves. He was especially eager to enslave the Israelites because he feared revolts in the newly re-conquered land of Canaan. And he believed that, since the Israelites were related to the Canaanites in race and language, they might join the Canaanites in their revolts.

Thus the Israelites became slaves in Egypt. For a long time, (we do not know exactly how long), they groaned under the rod of the Egyptian oppressor. At last a leader and deliverer rose up among them whose name was Moses. During a period of epidemics and disturbances in Egypt, the Israelites under the leadership of Moses made their escape from Egypt (1220 

B.C.E.). True, the Egyptians pursued them. But as a result of what seemed to the Israelites a great miracle at the Red Sea, they made good their escape. The pursuing Egyptians were drowned in the sea and Israel marched victoriously into the desert.

Moses now began the work of organizing the separate Hebrew Israelite clans into a single nation. The 

Patriarchs of the clans were asked to give up part of their power in order to help form the union. Moses was made the leader of the Israelites and appointed captains of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens to assist him and to perform some of the duties which formerly belonged to the Patriarchs. The Bible tells us, too, that there was also a council of seventy elders which helped decide all important questions that arose. 

Moses also taught the Israelites the ideas of a great religion. He asked the Israelites to accept a new God, who was the God not merely of one clan but of the whole nation. This God was more powerful than any of the clan gods whom the Israelites had worshipped and was perhaps even mightier than the gods of the other great nations. This God had saved them from 

Egyptian slavery and, if they proved worthy, would

continue to help them in the future. But the Israelites must accept certain new ways of living if they were to be considered worthy in the eyes of their new God. 

These new ways of living were prescribed in the Ten Commandments. The commandments said that the 

Israelites were to worship only one God even though they might still believe that other gods existed. In addition, they must act properly towards their fellow men; they must not rob, kill, or bear false witness; they must not desire that which did not belong to them. 

You can readily see how important was the work that Moses did for the Jewish people. He led them from slavery to freedom; he organized them into a nation; he taught them the beginnings of the religion which is still the foundation for the religions of a great part of the present-day world. But the Jews throughout the ages have not been ungrateful and have always recognized the greatness of Moses. For centuries Moses has been looked upon as the most important character in Jewish history and to the present day we love and respect him. 

And the great event for which Moses was responsible, the Exodus, has been regarded as perhaps the greatest event in Jewish history. The Exodus was both the birth of the Jewish people and the birth of the Jewish religion. Nor have the Jews ever forgotten their slavery and oppression in the land of Egypt. Knowing what it meant to be mistreated and downtrodden, they have always tried to treat the stranger, the weak, and the poor with kindness and mercy.

The following is a possible type of presentation.

PRESENTATION 

Palestine is a small country situated on the southeastern shores of the Mediterranean Sea. It is but 150 miles long and between 40 to 80 miles wide at various points. 

The country is about as long as the distance it would take a fast train to travel in three to four hours.

It is about equal in size to the state of New Hampshire. 

Though small in area, Palestine is not a country that can be very easily described. For instance, in giving a description of it, we cannot simply say that it is a fertile country or a barren country. You will ask why.

The answer is that Palestine is divided up into a num-

ber of regions each of which differs from the other in fertility, climate, rainfall, and vegetation. One section of the country is so hot all the year around that nothing grows there, not even grass. Another section is temperate in climate where rich fields of wheat and corn may be found. Still a third section is so cold that its mountains are snow capped in both summer and winter. 

It is essential that we know what the most important sections of Palestine are. If we were to use an aeroplane and travel across the width of the country, we would soon discover that Palestine is divided into four long strips, each running north to south. (Indicate these strips on the map as each is mentioned.) Beginning at the extreme west we find a stretch of fertile territory which borders on the Mediterranean Sea. This region is known as the Coastal Plain. To the east of the 

Coastal Plain is a rugged mountain range, known as the Central Range, which runs through the entire center of the country. This mountainous territory is perhaps the most important part of all Palestine. On its eastern side the Central Range sinks very rapidly into a very deep valley through which flows the largest river in the entire country. This river is the Jordan and the steep valley is known as the Jordan Valley. East of the Jordan Valley the land again rises rapidly and we find ourselves in the fourth strip of territory known as the East Jordan Plateau.

Having pointed out the four main stretches of territory into which Palestine is divided, let us now give our attention to examining each of these larger sections

in greater detail. We shall start this time from the eastern side of the country and travel westward. 

The East Jordan Plateau has always been known as rich pasture land where camels, sheep, and donkeys may graze the year round. The climate of this region is temperate. The land is well watered for it has three fairly large rivers running through it, the Yarmuk on the north, the Jabbok in the center, and the Arnon on the south, the latter flowing into the Dead Sea. This section receives plenty of rainfall and is rich in grass and trees. In parts, the soil is even suitable for the growing of wheat.

The Jordan Valley is the tropical section of Palestine. It is, for the most part, barren wilderness receiving little if any rainfall during the entire year. It is the part of Palestine that is least inhabited. 

The Central Range, already described as the most important portion of Palestine, is really divided into a number of separate sections each having somewhat different characteristics. On the extreme south is the 

Negeb, a district which receives little rainfall and is, therefore, only slightly better than a desert. Travelling northward from the Negeb, the mountain range rises and becomes steep, rugged, and very rocky. This section is known as Judah. The soil on the mountain sides is thin and rather poor while the valleys are narrow and rocky, and, therefore, difficult to cultivate. However, grape vines and olive trees grow here. Portions of the land, too, are suitable for grazing. The climate is temperate and comfortable.

The region north of Judah is known as Samaria. The hills here are not as high nor as rugged as those of Judah. The valleys, too, are wide and fertile. Here one finds fields of wheat and barley, large vegetable gardens, vineyards, and orchards. Cattle, too, graze in the meadows. This section of the country is very pleasant to live in, and has always been one of the favorite spots in Palestine.

North of Samaria, the Central Range is broken into by the Plain of Jezreel through which flows the Kishon River. The Plain of Jezreel is the most fertile section in Palestine where one may find large fields of golden wheat. The climate here is, however, rather warm and at times even uncomfortable.

North of the Plain of Jezreel is the district known as Galilee in which is to be found the Sea of Chinnereth. This section is rather hilly country but is almost as fertile as the plain for it yields many and varied crops. Galilee is one of the pleasant and desirable sec tions of Palestine. 

The Coastal Plain remains to be described. As has already been pointed out, this plain is a fertile strip of territory bordering on the Mediterranean. At its southern end it is about 40 miles wide but it becomes gradually narrower as it proceeds northward. Rich fields of wheat and corn are to be found here as well as fruit and nut trees, fig palms, pomegranates, citrons, and oranges. The climate is rather hot, but relief from the heat is sometimes afforded by the breezes coming in from the sea.

Thus we see that Palestine is a land that has different geographical characteristics as we go from district to district. The climate may be anything from tropical to frigid. The altitude of the land varies all the way from a valley as low as 1300 feet below sea level to mountains as high as 9000 feet above sea level. The soil is fertile in some spots while in others it is nothing but barren waste. Various products are grown in the country depending on the climate, soil, and rainfall of the particular region. If, therefore, we are to have any accurate knowledge of the geography of Palestine, we must make each special section the subject of careful study.

The following is an example of a presentation that might conceivably be made by the teacher.

PRESENTATION 

We know that probably during the early part of the twelfth century B.C.E. the Israelites began to invade and conquer the land of Canaan. Exactly how the invasion and conquest took place we do not know, for the Bible is not altogether clear on this phase of Hebrew history. Probably some of the Israelite tribes, led by the tribe of Judah, invaded Canaan from the south marching northward through the Negeb until they reached the mountainous country in the lower part of the Central Range. The majority of the Israelites, however, led by the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh, probably invaded Canaan from the east. The East Jordan

country, especially that part of it known as Gilead, was invaded while Moses was still alive. This section seems to have been conquered without much difficulty and was used as a base for the attack on the West Jordan Country. The tribes of Reuben and Gad made the 

Eastern Plateau their permanent home. 

With the death of Moses, Joshua, of the tribe of

Ephraim, became the leader of the Israelites. It was at his command that the Israelites crossed the Jordan and moved against the middle portion of the Central Range. 

In this territory the hilly sections and small villages fell into the hands of the Israelites, but the fortified cities were too much for the poorly armed invaders. Thus, the important cities of the land, such as Shechem, Gezer, Taanach, and Megiddo, remained in the possession of the Canaanites. The tribes of Ephraim, Manasseh, and

Benjamin settled in that part of the middle portion of the Central Range which the Israelites succeeded in conquering.

The remainder of the tribes, namely, Issachar, Zebulun, Naphtali, and Asher, pushed northward into Galilee. At first they were successful in conquering this territory, but within a very short time, they lost their newly won possessions and were in turn subdued by the Canaanites.

Thus the early invasion of Canaan by the Israelites resulted in four Israelite settlements each separated from the other. In the north were the tribes of 

Issachar, Zebulun, Naphtali, and Asher, separated by the Canaanites in the Valley of Jezreel from the tribes

of Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin settled in Samaria. These tribes in Samaria were also separated from the tribes of Judah, Simeon, and Dan, living in the southern part of the Central Range, by the Jebusites who owned the territory surrounding and including the important city of Jerusalem. The tribes of 

Reuben and Gad, settled in the East Jordan country, were, of course, separated from all the other tribes by the Jordan River. Even in the sections where the 

Israelites settled, however, the fortified cities remained in the hands of the Canaanites.

It is difficult to say whether, as a result of the early conquest, the Israelites were better or worse off than they had been during their nomadic days. Certainly, to live a settled life meant more comfort, better opportunities for earning a living, and possibilities of moving forward to higher stages of progress. At the same time, the Israelites were faced by certain grave dangers. To begin with, they were surrounded on all sides by enemies. Not only were the Canaanites eager to force them out of their newly won territory, but the surrounding nations, the Philistines, the Edomites, the Moabites, the Ammonites, and the Midianites, were eager and ready to pounce upon them at the earliest opportunity. In addition, the Israelites, separated as they were within the country, did not find it easy to unite for purposes of defense. Besides, the teachings of 

Moses were commencing to lose their hold upon them and they were beginning to forget that they were all members of the same people. The worship of the

Canaanite Baalim was now practiced among them, for the Israelites felt that, if they were to be favored in their new home, they must worship the gods who had the power to grant favors. These gods were, of course, the gods of the Canaanites.

But the Israelites did not altogether forget the worship of Yahveh taught to them by Moses during their days of wilderness wandering. And in time of danger, when they were called upon in the name of their God to unite against the attacks of their enemies, they responded to the call. The greatest danger that threatened them soon after they settled in the country was a union of Canaanite kings who gave them battle in the Valley of Jezreel. Under the leadership of Deborah and 

Barak a number of the tribes united and succeeded in winning the important victory which decided that the 

Israelites were to be masters of Canaan. The most fertile section of the country now fell into Israelite hands, and the tribes of the north were no longer separated from those living in the central portion of the country. 

But after this important victory the Israelite tribes again forgot that they were all members of one people. 

Each tribe became interested only in its own needs and did not concern itself about the others. Only when the 

Midianites attacked the country and threatened to take away all of the Israelite possessions did some of the tribes again unite and beat off the dreaded enemy, this time under Gideon. Gradually the Israelites were beginning to see the need for union. They asked Gideon to become their king. Gideon refused but his son,

Abimelech, tried to establish a kingship after Gideon died. He was not successful, however, since the people did not approve of him, and after a short rule of only three years, he was assassinated.

Thus, for some time after the conquest, the Israelites lived through a very difficult period of their history. If it had not been for the worship of Yahveh which still had its center in the city of Shiloh, the Israelites might in time have disappeared altogether as a separate people. But the worship of Yahveh helped to remind the Israelites of the union of the tribes which had been brought about by Moses. In times of real danger, therefore, it was still possible for the Judges to rally large numbers of them to the common cause. In this way was Israel able to keep alive, even though conditions in the country were very much against them, thus making it difficult to keep the tribes together.

CHAPTER V TEACHING TOPIC V How Did Israel Become a United 

Kingdom?

Topic V treats of Israel's struggle for union, a movement which finally culminated in the establishment of the monarchy under Saul and David. Entering the land of Canaan, probably by devious routes and at various times, the Israelites in all likelihood lost much of the sense of unity engendered by Moses. EXPLORATION: INTRODUCING THE STUDY OF TOPIC V 

The teaching of Topic V, which treats of Israel's struggle for union, can probably be motivated by drawing an analogy with a somewhat similar struggle that took place in the United States in the period immediately following the Revolutionary War. While the war with 

Great Britain was in progress, the colonies remained united in order that independence from the mother country might be won. Once the war was over the states became absorbed in their own local interests and concerned themselves but little with one another's welfare. The central government, under the Articles of 

Confederation, was weak. It could not guarantee for instance that commercial agreements entered into with European countries would be fulfilled. Nor could it force the states to pay the national debt of some $150,- 000,000 which had been contracted during the war. 

In fact, it could not even levy taxes to defray the expenses of government.

The undesirable state of affairs that prevailed in the states at the close of the war demonstrated itself further in the relations existing between them. The larger states wished to become rich at the expense of their smaller and weaker neighbors. Commercial disputes arose because of widely differing policies in regard to the importation of European goods. The states were jealous of one another and passed hostile

legislation that almost resulted in civil war. These conditions rendered necessary the creation of a strong central government which would have power to enforce its demands even though these might be contrary to the interests of the individual states. The adoption of the Constitution meant the establishment of a central government strong enough to bring about the union of the thirteen separate states.

The Hebrew tribes, too, needed a central government to which they could look in time of danger. They, too, had to learn of the strength that lay in united action. 

Quarrels among the tribes and even the shedding of blood were not unknown. Absorption of the tribes in purely local interests was the prevailing state of affairs. But the Israelites realized the necessity for union only when they reaped the fruits of their mutual jealousy and separatism. Constant attacks by enemies impoverished the country and decimated its inhabitants. Even the danger of total extermination was not outside the realm of possibility. Israel began to clamor for a king. It may have taken years for the thought to ripen. It took an even longer time for the idea to become a reality. Finally, in the person of David, came the embodiment of the idea of the union of Israel.

PRESENTATION 

The Philistines were the people immediately responsible for the establishment of the Hebrew monarchy. 

The Philistines, who probably came originally from the island of Crete, were settled in the Coastal Plain. 

Here they occupied mainly five large cities which were united under a sort of federal government. The Philistines had reached a rather high level of civilization even before they entered the land of Canaan. They were already acquainted with many arts such as building, forging iron and bronze, and navigation.

The first group among the Israelites to feel the force of Philistine strength was the tribe of Dan which for many years kept up a sort of guerrilla warfare with the Philistines. The Israelite hero in these encounters

was Samson, who succeeded in making things disagreeable for the Philistines, but accomplished nothing for the Israelites that was of lasting importance to them. 

In the end, the tribe of Dan was forced to leave the Shephela and seek a new home in the extreme north of the land of Canaan.

But the Philistines did not rest content merely with forcing the Danites out of the Shephela. They began to cast envious eyes at the desirable sections of Canaan, namely, Samaria and the Valley of Jezreel. They organized a large army, proceeded to march through the Plain of Sharon, and gave battle to the Israelites at Eben-ezer. Here the gloomy, dispirited Israelite army, which at this time was lacking in leadership, met with a disastrous defeat. Even the Ark of the Covenant, which the Israelite leaders hastily brought into the battle to raise the spirits of the Israelites, was of no avail. The Philistines now became masters of the country and stationed their soldiers and military governors in all the important cities of the country. 

But the Philistine domination taught the Israelites a valuable lesson. They now began to see the importance of being a united people. They were fortunate, too, in having at this time a leader who was able to achieve what the people desired. This leader was 

Samuel, the prophet and representative of the God of Shiloh. Samuel's work consisted of teaching the Israelites the value and importance of union. The Israelites must form a united central government if they were to succeed in overthrowing the rule of the Philistines.

For a long time Samuel sought a warrior-king who would be capable of leading the Israelites in battle.

After a number of years of careful searching he finally selected Saul, of the tribe of Benjamin, as the first Israelite king. Saul proved his ability by his success in battle, first against the Ammonites and then later against the Philistines.

But Saul's successes were not of any lasting benefit. 

Toward the latter part of his reign fortune turned against him. In the first place, he quarreled with 

Samuel. Exactly why this quarrel arose we do not know. Perhaps it was because Saul did not show sufficient interest in the religion of Yahveh, or would not introduce the many reforms that Samuel demanded of him. In the second place, Saul became the victim of a mental disease, a sort of melancholy, that threw him into fits of anger and depression. And in the third place, Saul lost courage because of his jealousy of David, a young man who lived in his court and who achieved such fame that Saul saw in him a possible rival for the throne.

For all these reasons the latter part of Saul's reign was not successful. Under the leadership of a king who was mentally ill and who had very little confidence in his own ability, the Israelites could hardly hope to meet successfully the attacks of the powerful Philistines. During the last years of his life Saul no longer had the support of David who might have been of great assistance in the wars with the Philistines.

Finally, in the disastrous battle of Gilboa, Saul's army

was decisively defeated and both Saul and his brave son, Jonathan, met their death. Israel was once more under the domination of the Philistines. 

Upon Saul's death, David, returning from Philistia where he had fled from the wrath of Saul, succeeded in having himself appointed King of Judah. Saul's son, Ish-Bosheth, was, however, crowned king by the Benjaminites and the Gileadites and had his capital at Mahanaim, in the East Jordan country. For a time the country was torn by civil war, much to the satisfaction of the Philistines. Finally, however, with the assassination of Ish-Bosheth by his own soldiers, David became king of a united Israel. 

David proved to be the very type of leader that Israel needed. He immediately proceeded to free his country from the domination of the Philistines. The wars with the Philistines lasted a long time but in them David was entirely successful. The enemy was so completely subdued that henceforward they gave the Israelites no trouble. David also fought wars against other nations such as Moab, Ammon, Edom, and Damascus, and took possession of portions of their territory. Thus did David establish the empire of Israel.

David organized a regular government such as had not existed during the reign of Saul. He conquered the city of Jerusalem and made it the capital of his kingdom. To Jerusalem, also, he brought the Ark of the Covenant, and thus made the city the religious capital of the nation as well. David laid the founda-

tions for a lasting government by levying taxes, by establishing a standing army, and by appointing officials to help him in the work of government. In these numerous ways did David become the builder of the nation of Israel.

The latter part of David's reign was saddened by two unfortunate events. The first was a revolt led by his own son, Absalom, against the now aging king. 

The second sad event took place in the latter part of David's reign and was another uprising, this time led by Sheba ben Bichri. This struggle was really a rebellion of the north against the rule of the tribe of Judah. What this revolt as well as that of Absalom proved was that the nation was not yet united in any true sense. It was easy to stir up trouble among the tribes even during the reign of so successful a king as David. Fortunately, both revolts were suppressed and the Israelites were once more at peace with one another. 

David's last days were peacefully spent. As he grew older his fame increased. For he had been eminently successful in building the nation of Israel and in elevating its position among the nations of the world. 

Later ages especially appreciated the greatness of his work. He came to be looked upon not only as an ideal king and hero but also as a great poet and religious leader. In later days the kingship remained connected with the family of David, and no king ruled in Jerusalem who could not trace his relationship to David. Even after Israel was exiled, it always hoped

that it would again return to its own country and be ruled by a descendant of David, its most beloved king and greatest hero.

PRESENTATION 

In our study of biblical history up to this point, we have placed a good deal of emphasis upon such matters as Israel's conquest of Canaan, the struggle for union, the wars with numerous enemies, the quarrels between Saul and David, the revolt of Absalom against David etc. Some of us might by now be thinking that what the ancient Israelites did most of the time was fight battles or prepare for some war or other. Such a picture of the life of our ancestors in Canaan is, of course, not correct. For we must remember that only a small portion of an Israelite's time, even in ancient Canaan, was spent in fighting. During the remainder of the time he earned his living, he worshipped his God, he spent his leisure in listening to stories and in singing songs, and he did a great many other things that all people have done ever since man first appeared upon the earth. In the first topic that you studied you were told about how our ancestors lived in the desert at the time when they were nomads. In the new topic that we are about to study, Topic VI, you will learn how our ancestors lived in the land of Canaan after they had ceased their wanderings and become a settled people. 

The Israelites living in Canaan did not all earn their living in the same way. Most of them probably be-

came farmers. A great many, especially those living in southern Judah and Gilead, remained shepherds.

There were others who became craftsmen of various kinds, such as smiths, potters, tanners, etc.

The people for the most part lived in villages, but some lived in the larger towns. Houses were simple usually being made of sunbaked clay or rough stone and having only one room where both the family and the household animals found shelter. In the village the "gate" was the center of activities. There people came to buy and sell their goods or to have quarrels judged. Also, they came to hear gossip or news, or to take part in pleasure and entertainment. 

In Canaan the clans began to lose some of their importance. When the Israelites settled in the country, people who had belonged to the same clan did not always make their homes in the same place. Soon a man's neighbor became more important to him than the members of his old clan and, in time, perhaps the clan even broke up altogether. The village, too, now became more important than the tribe, and in place of the tribal leader, the village elders became the local rulers of the people. Of course, the people still had the final say in matters of great importance. But the elders, who in many cases might formerly have been the clan Patriarchs, were the people's representatives in matters that concerned the whole village. The elders also served as judges in smaller cases, the more important cases being turned over to the priests, or perhaps even to the king himself.

When the Israelites settled in Canaan they also changed their customs of holding property. We all remember that in the wilderness property was held in common by the entire clan. Thus, all people were on a basis of equality as far as wealth was concerned. In 

Canaan, however, conditions were different. Each person received his small parcel of land and worked it for his own benefit; he kept for himself the entire product of his labor. Sometimes, however, a man fell into debt and was forced to sell his land. When such a situation arose, there were always some men among the Israelites who were willing to buy up land that was for sale. In this way it soon came about that there were rich and poor people among the Israelites. The rich began to gain power, while the condition of the poor became worse and worse. Thus did equality disappear among the Israelites.

When people live together either in large or small groups, they do not always get along peacefully. Quarrels arise among them over matters of property or personal injury. Some people commit the crime of stealing, while others even go so far as to shed blood. When a quarrel or dispute arose, it had to be settled by a court over which either an elder or a priest presided. 

It is true that the Israelites had courts, laws, and judges even before they entered Canaan. But now the old laws were no longer satisfactory. Since the Israelites were living under new conditions they needed new laws. Besides, as a people grows older and wiser, the old laws are not good enough. Improvement in the

laws takes place; they became more fair and just. 

As we continue with our study of Topic VI, we shall see how Israel's laws changed for the better. 

The religious life of a people is always very important. Among the ancient Israelites this was certainly the case. Yahveh or the other gods that they worshipped meant a great deal to them. And, with the entrance of the Israelites into Canaan, their habits of worship and their ways of thinking about God probably underwent a great many changes. 

Large numbers of Israelites very likely learned from their Canaanite neighbors the worship of the agricultural gods or Baalim. Others probably continued to be loyal to the religious ideas and customs taught to them by Moses. Most of the Israelites must have believed in a kind of mixed religion that was partly 

Canaanite and partly Israelite. These very likely prayed and brought their sacrifices to Yahveh, God of Israel, and also to the Canaanite Baalim. They traveled three times a year, on Passover, on Shabuoth, and on Sukkoth, to Jerusalem, and brought the products of their harvests to the holy shrine situated there. During these pilgrimages the Israelites had merry times.

They sang, they danced, they played their musical instruments, they told stories about their famous ancestors, Abraham, Jacob, Moses, and Samson. The people got a great deal of joy out of their religious life. 

Such, in brief, was the life that our Israelite ancestors led in the land of Canaan. Some of their habits and customs remained very similar to what they had

been during nomadic times. But for the most part these habits and customs changed. There were now new ways of earning a living, new types of homes in which to live, new laws telling men how they were to act toward one another, and new methods of worshipping God. In many fields of human life the Israelites were indeed making progress. But during the period which we are studying they had not yet reached that high level of civilization which was to make them famous for centuries and centuries afterward.

b. The religion of Moses forbade the making of any images of Yahveh. c. Yahveh was looked upon as the God of all the 

Israelitic clans and as the ruler over every department of their life. d. The followers of the religion of Moses also worshipped at home and at the high places. 

In the home the father or the eldest son offered the sacrifices and led the family in prayer; at the high places the Levites were the ministers of religion. e. But the religion of Moses also had one place of worship which was considered the central shrine. During the time of David this central shrine was in Jerusalem. f. The religion of Moses also had its prophets who at first were little more than magicians or wonder workers. g. But Moses, Samuel, and Nathan are examples of a new class of prophets who were leaders of the people in teaching them right conduct. These prophets later became the defenders of the people's rights against the autocratic rule of the king. 

4. The average Israelite probably worshipped both 

Yahveh and Baal. a. Three times a year on Passover, Shabuoth, and Sukkoth, he probably went up to Jerusalem and brought his gifts to the priests and the Levites.

b. He observed his New Moons and his Sabbaths, his Rosh Hashonah, and his local holidays. c. He brought his sacrifices to the Baal who lived on the mountain near his home and he also worshipped his household spirits. d. His sacrifices were usually accompanied by singing, religious dances, games, and competitions.

F. With settled life in Canaan changes took place in other departments of Israelite life. As was to be expected most of these changes were for the better. 

1. Boys and girls received their education mostly by imitating their elders. a. The most important study for every child was that of learning an occupation. b. Boys had to learn also how to use the weapons of war. c. Only the members of the priesthood and the upper class learned how to read and write. 

2. After the settlement, when the Israelites learned how to write, many of the stories, poems, and songs which they had known for centuries were written down. 

3. Our ancestors seem to have been very fond of music, for quite a number of musical instruments are mentioned in the Bible. 

4. In sculpture and painting our ancestors accomplished but very little. 

5. Wars at this time, too, became less frequent, but

when they did take place they were very destructive. 

6. It was later in their history that the prophets began to teach the Israelites the importance of world peace.

The recitation of Topic VI should, of course, be made by each pupil on the topic in its entirety. These recitations should be brief and deal only with the high spots of the topic. While making his recitation, the pupil should have as his only guide a few brief notes jotted down on a card.

FINAL EXAMINATION 

As the last phase of the course based on Israel in Canaan, a final written examination is to be administered. This examination, like all previous tests, is prepared for the teacher and aims to test the more important concepts and ideas that, it is hoped, have been developed through the course. The examination also emphasizes what are considered to be essential facts for the proper comprehension of these ideas and concepts. The test can probably be administered in a period of from one half to three quarters of an hour.

CHAPTER VII Conclusion 

HE course in biblical history based on Israel in Canaan can undoubtedly be made exceedingly significant and stimulating. The teacher has certain initial advantages in teaching the course that makes his task easier and perhaps more pleasant than one is accustomed to associate with Sunday School work as we have known it in the past. To begin with, he has a text that has been thoughtfully and intelligently written and that meets with the requirements of good textbook writing that have been set up by reputable educators. In the second place, he has a Workbook which constitutes a real guide to the pupil in his many and varied study activities. And in the third place, this teacher's book will probably prove helpful to the teacher as a compendium of specific suggestions for procedure in handling the course in all of its many phases.

In the last analysis, however, everything depends on the teacher himself. No amount of study aids to the pupil or of helpful suggestions to the teacher will in the least way detract from the all-important and significant role which the teacher himself must assume in the educational process. Training in both subject matter and method, conscientiousness in checking pupils' work, careful and painstaking planning, adaptability in meeting untoward classroom conditions-all 111

are required if the teacher is to meet with even a fair degree of success in his teaching of this course. It cannot be urged too strongly upon the teacher that he adhere as scrupulously as possible to the procedure as outlined in this teacher's book. It is, of course, necessary to adapt the work to the particular requirements of a given school situation. The physical conditions of the classroom, the equipment in the way of books, maps, etc., the amount of homework that can normally be expected of a particular group of pupils, are factors which will, of course, need to be taken into consideration. But the procedure as such is fundamentally sound and the teacher will do well to make only such departure from it as his peculiar conditions and circumstances may dictate. It is proposed at this point to recapitulate briefly some of the more significant suggestions that have been advanced in this teacher's book. 

1. It is important to teach each unit in accordance with the procedure for the teaching of history outlined by Professor Morrison. The steps involved in the handling of the unit are exploration, presentation, assimilation, organization, and recitation. 

2. Such adherence to method should not, however, prevent the teacher from making necessary adjustments to meet the peculiar conditions of his own situation. Thus, lack of reference books may render it difficult for the teacher to stress the supplementary reading aspects of the course to the extent that might be considered desirable. Similarly, an inability on the

113 part of pupils to do any considerable amount of homework may make it necessary for the teacher to spend more class time on study, with the result that certain other aspects of the course might not receive the amount of emphasis that they deserve. No changes should, however, be introduced by the teacher that are of such nature as to strike at the very fundamentals of the course itself. 

3. A departure from the set procedure is certainly recommended in connection with the teaching of Topic I where the project of constructing a nomadic encampment would constitute the major activity during the assimilation period. The project teaching of Topic I would help launch the course in biblical history with a real display of interest and enthusiasm on the part of the pupils. 

4. The teacher should plan all aspects of his teaching with the utmost of care and precision. Considerable attention should be devoted especially to the preparation of the teacher's presentation. While the models that are included in this teacher's guide would probably serve the purpose intended, the teacher may sometimes find that telling the pupil too much of what is to follow may detract from the interest that the pupil would normally display in his reading of the text chapter. Under such circumstances it may be necessary to change the nature of the presentation and, in connection with the teaching of some topics, merely to indicate in very general terms the broad outlines of the topic about to be studied.

5. The teacher should do all in his power to see to it that the period of supervised study is properly utilized. Each study period should be carefully planned, for the teacher will need to know ahead of time what his activities for the period are to be, which pupils are to get his special attention, etc. Only in this way will the supervised study period be utilized to the full for purposes of guiding the learning process. 

6. The development of the ability to organize a unit should be a gradual process and should constitute one of the desirable outcomes of the course in biblical history. The teacher would do well to use the various types of organization suggested and thus proceed systematically in the development of the ability to outline a topic. 

7. The teacher's attention is called especially to the variety of pupil activities. The numerous devices suggested for making the work stimulating and interesting should be utilized by the teacher to the very fullest extent possible. The various types of objective test exercises, the map work, the time-lines are, of course, required activities for all of the pupils. The teacher will, in addition, place considerable emphasis on the other devices that are suggested under "Additional Activities for Good Workers" such as dramatization, debating, story-telling, drawing, etc. The utilization of all the resources that the course has to offer will undoubtedly prove a boon to the teacher in rendering the study of biblical history truly purposeful to his pupils.

115 8. Much emphasis should be placed on the matter of wide reading contacts. A subject such as history is probably mastered much more readily through extensive reading rather than through intensive study of a textbook. Even where the teacher does not have a supply of books necessary to encourage supplementary reading, a great deal can be done by having pupils bring into class such books as they can obtain from the public library, from their own home libraries, from libraries of friends, etc. Nor need the reading necessarily be confined to the books that are suggested in text and Workbook. Any related reading of an appropriate and desirable character will help to achieve the purpose that is to be served by extensive and abundant reading. 

9. The importance of Bible reading should again be emphasized in this connection. This course in biblical history offers excellent opportunities for familiarizing the pupils with a mass of biblical material. Such reading of the Bible will be much better motivated than would be the case where a special course in Bible or biblical literature is offered. The teacher should make abundant use of the opportunity here afforded to have the pupils read widely in this ancient and significant literature of the Jewish people. 

10. The teacher should at all times bear in mind the fundamental aims and purposes of the course in biblical history. It has been emphasized over and over again that the course is intended to develop in the pupil certain attitudes, understandings, and skills. At

no time should the teacher assume that it is his function to make of the pupil a storehouse of information about biblical history. Names, dates, events are important only as they contribute to some significant understanding; they should be mastered only if they serve this necessary purpose. As separate items of information they have no value. If the teacher will view his task as that of educating children rather than that of dispensing subject matter, he will not fall into the error of confusing means and ends. Thinking in terms of teaching a subject leads to emphasis on facts and information; thinking in terms of educating children leads to the development of attitudes, understandings, and skills.
