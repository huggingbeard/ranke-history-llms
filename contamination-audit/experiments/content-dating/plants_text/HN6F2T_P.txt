Dr. Reinhold Forster, the naturalist of Captain Cook's second voyage to the Pacific, brought to light the interesting fact of the similarity of all the Polynesian dialects; and the Abbe Lorenzo 

Hervas, a Spanish Jesuit of Lima, Peru, pointed out the Malay and Polynesian language affinity, and its extent over 208 degrees of longitude from Easter Island to Madagascar; common words in the Malagasy and Polynesian are wanting in the Malay, which latter language was modified by the Mother Sanskrit. 

The Malagasy speech was much changed by the introduction of African and Arabic words; the Polynesian, Malagasy and 

Malay, are apparently derived from one mother language, now missing. Due to frequent invasions of the warlike Malays, their words became engrafted on the Polynesian, but they did not, and have not materially changed it, hence without any sufficient reason, the name Malayo-Polynesian is in common use to designate a language, which is 85 per cent Polynesian.

Diligent research reveals the Polynesians derived their type of civilization from India; and also all evidence tends to prove the 

Polynesian languages have absolutely no connection with those of the American continent.

The languages of Oceanica are divided into six large groups: 

1 The Polynesian; 2 Mircronesian; 3 Melanesian or Papuan; 

4 Malagasy; 5 Malaysian; 6 Australian.

The Black race were apparently the first occupants of the 

Pacific. 

Of the languages of Malaysia, those of the Moluccas Islands approach the nearest to the Polynesian; the Micronesian languages are probably derivatives from the Malay; the Malagasy bears no resemblance to the languages of South Africa, but it has a semblance to the Tagala of the Philippines. 

The Polynesian language is an extremely ancient and primitive member of the Malay family; the Sanskrit words in the Javanese and Malay languages indicate their entrance must have been in the B. C. era. The New Zealand dialect is the most primitive of all the Polynesian languages; the Hawaiian, Tahitian and 

Marquesan are a group relatively to themselves.

The established usage or rule of all Polynesian languages isevery word and syllable must end in a vowel, no two consonants are ever heard without a vowel sound between them. In the 

Polynesian dialect or speech no distinction is made between the letters b and p, d and t, g and k, 1 and r, v and w. In Hawaiian, the consonants f and s are changed to h; ng is softened to n; k at the beginning of a word is dropped, in the middle of a word, a guttural catch or break is used; w is used for v. 

The vocabulary of the Hawaiian is richer than most other tongues of Oceanica, but it lacks abstract words and general terms; the Polynesian in its primitive stages was composed of words implying-sensations, images; later, words meaning abstract ideas were added.

In Hawaiian every day life, words are used with exactness and delicacy of perception, for example-moku, broken as a string or rope; broken as a dish, naha; broken, as a stick or long bone, haki; to fall from a height, haule; to fall horizontally, walihina; to carry on the shoulder with a stick, auamo; to carry in the hands, kaikai; hii, to carry as a child in the arms; koi, to carry on a stick between two men; haawe, to carry on the back; hali, to carry in general. Hawaiian names of natural objects are most apt and expressive, also personal words; the hair of the head, in Hawaiian the word lauoho is used, meaning the leaves of the head; milk from the breast is named waiuu, the tears of the breast; in the presence of a dead person the words pau ka luhi are used, meaning rest from weariness; also the word hala is used, meaning he or she is absent or gone away on a journey, death. A hasty condemnation of the Hawaiian language as useless, is to be deplored, it contains much that is beautiful, pathetic, poetic, and expressive.

THE POLYNESIAN CANOEMEN 

The Arabs were the great ancient navigators, explorers, and historians: they acquired their maritime skill and seamanship in the Mediterranean, Red, and Arabian seas, also in the Persian gulf. The Phoenicians received their marine training around the islands in the Persian gulf, the Mediterranean sea, the Atlantic ocean, off the coats of Spain, Portugal, and the seas around the

British isles; from Cornwall the Phoenicians carried as cargo in their ships the ores of tin; and as early as the year 2,000 B. C., according to the researches of Sir Henry Rawlinson, the archeologist, it was from this source the Egyptians obtained the tin used in making their bronze ornaments, implements, and household articles. 

The Egyptians and the Persians, owing to their dislike and dread of the ocean, never became seamen or navigators. 

The Polynesians were pre-eminently a race of excellent seamen, due to centuries of leading a seafaring life; their ancestors acquired their marine training in the bay of Bengal, off the

Malay peninsula, in the seas off Indonesia, and later in the vast Pacific ocean, which they entered in Dhows and Praus, finally sailing in large canoes with decks.

Large decked vessels, capable of accomodating 100 or more persons were in use by the Polynesian Wanderers up to about the year 1100 A. D.; in the more recent times, the art of building this type of vessel seems to have been lost or abandoned; they were built of planks, matched and well fitted together and made tight by sewing with twine made from coconut husks and other substances; the seams were also calked with resinous material making them waterproof; such a type of vessel in fleets of six

or more, carried men, women and children; supplies of fresh water, food, other stores, fishing nets, spare sails, fowl, pigs, dingo dogs, whose food was roots, vegetables-this breed of dog was eatable, did not bark or bite. Sufficient food and water, estimated to last a month, was stowed on a specially constructed supply canoe or vessel.

The Polynesian canoemen were a skilful, fearless, and successful class of navigators and explorers, equal to and exceeding the navigators of other countries in marine skill; poorly outfitted and equipped for stormy weather, yet in frail craft they pursued boldly their wanderings over a vast unknown ocean, where thirst, starvation, and possible death awaited them; speedily becoming familiar with ocean winds and currents, having a workable knowledge of the planets and fixed stars,together with their skill in seamanship and fishing; they reduced to a minimum the prospects of ocean disasters or pilikias.

On the broad expanse of the great ocean, no enemy could ambuscade them, nor was it necessary to guard against perils of this nature, because fogs were of rare occurrence, and smoke screens were unknown; hence, under all of the foregoing favorable conditions for successful ocean voyages, the bold Polynesian navigators were enabled to explore and discover many new and unknown islands in the largest ocean of our globe, the Pacific. 

In all their wanderings, the Polynesian seamen, for necessity and safety, chose the route of least resistance, the ocean; which with its currents and winds, aided by sails and paddle-power, was a motor of progress and comparative comfort, also of sustenance, providing fish, edible limu, etc.

Due to the frequent incursions of Mongoloid predatory hordes, who were crushing and crowding out the Polynesian colonists in Malaysia, about the year A. D. 100, and also in the B. C. date unknown, the Hejira of the Polynesians began into the Pacific from the west towards the east; and in the IVth century A. D., the emigrants had arrived at Rapanui, Easter island, a voyage of some 8000 miles across the Pacific. NAUTICAL SCHOOLS, WATER CLOCKS, COMPASS 

At the age of ten years, the children of the Polynesians, both boys and girls, were given instruction in navigation, steering and management of canoes, both large and small, the positions of fixed and wandering stars, the latitudes were indicated by horizontal strings stretched over a semi-circular section of a gourd; the positions of the tropical circles, north and south of the equator were shown and taught daily in the schools, also the periods of the solstices; the positions of the various groups of islands in the 

Pacific were shown on a circular board by placing small and large pebbles and varied colored shells at or about the chosen locations of the islands.

The Water Clocks of the Malays, Polynesians, and Malagasy, were made from a half section of a coconut shell or small gourd, perforated with a small circular hole for long hours or periods of from 3 to six hours; larger holes admitting more water into the floating shells or gourds on the surface of a large calabash of water, registered hourly or every two hours, when they filled

The ancient compass : Chinese navigators used a compass, composed of a magnetized needle suspended between straws floating on the surface of a level basin or box filled with water. The 

Hawaiians and other Polynesians, the Malay and Malagasy navigators, determined the cardinal points of the horizon by their bodies and arms, as follows: The navigator stood erect in the bow of the canoe, prau or dhow-facing the setting sun; this indicated to him the Komohana or west (Hawaiian speech); raising his right arm and extended hand horizontally to shoulder level from the side of his body, indicated the Akau or north; a similar position of the raised left arm and hand and fingers, indicated the Hema or south; and the back of the standing navigator, indicated the Hikina or east; this body compass of the Hawaiian navigators was an ever present, simple and effective aid to the steering of their canoes over the waters of the Pacific ocean. 

POLYNESIANS 

The Polynesians are an older race than the Malays; their manner of worship indicates that it has no connection with eastern religious ceremonies; this fact is strong evidence of their early migration.

The traditions of the Polynesians tend to prove their earliest home was towards the west, Indonesia; on enterting the Pacific they sailed and paddled from island to island. Java or Hawaiki (?) was probably only a temporary home, from which they were ejected by the Malays. It is to Java that the souls of the Polynesians return along the great road of death; the departed souls begin their journey home, which is invariably situated to the westward of all the various island groups, excepting Hawaii, which has two spirit roads of the dead, west and northwest, this latter is said to be the spirit road of the earliest Hawaiian colonists, it follows the trend of the islands on the surface of the ocean, southeast to northwest; the western, and southwest spirit roads are the highways of the departed souls of those emigrants who arrived at 

Hawaii, about the year 1000 A. D. from Tahiti and probably

Samoa.

The ancient Polynesian priests were endowed with marvelous memories; good memories were the indispensable qualification for their tenure in office; hereditary transmission was not their sole essential, it was flawless recitation of historical and genealogical facts, which have made the oral traditions of the Hawaiians as trustworthy as written legends. Io, was the Supreme being; Ra or La was the god of the sun; Tangaroa, was the god of the sea; 

Ku, Kane, Kanaloa, and Lono were names of Hawaiian gods. 

The Typical Polynesian: such as the majority of the aboriginal 

Hawaiians, have the following characteristics, light brown coffeecolor skin, very expressive brown eyes, good teeth, abundant black wavy or straight hair, cleanly, cheerful, gentle, affectionate, polite, dignified, musical, fine physical development, tall, average 5 feet 10 inches; Semi-Aryan features, a long type of skull resembling the European, also in its brain capacity. All of these distinguishing features of the Polynesian are lacking in the Malay; but the ancient Hindus and the Persians possessed most of them; it is to the former of these ancient races that the primal Polynesians can be traced, hence their identification in the Pacific should present no problem which cannot be solved. The intermixing of the Polynesian blood with the Papuan and Melanesian, is responsible for an inferior type of the race. 

Rapanui, Easter island: marked on Mendana's map of 1595, as El Lobo (the wolf), at the date of this Spanish navigator's discovery of the South Marquesa islands; on Easter Sunday in 

1722, the Dutch Admiral Jacob Roggeveen visited the island and named it Easter.

This island is an elevated rugged rocky plateau, famed for its large carved stone images, six to thirty feet long, they are prehistoric sculptures. The inhabitants of the island today are 

Polynesians, whose ancestors came from the Marquesa islands, some 1,800 miles to the northwest.

Great numbers of wooden tablets bearing hieroglyphic figures have been discovered on the island; in the year 1886 an old male resident, at the request of some visiting scientists from the University of Lima, Peru, deciphered one of the tablets, evidently a declamation or chant of some ancient priest: "In that happy land, that beautiful land which was governed by the Gods from heaven, where Romaha lived before with his beloved Hangaroa; who

lived in the water when (the season) was cold, and where the black and white pointed spider (the sun's rays shining through the dark and white clouds in a wintry sky) would have climbed to heaven, but was prevented by the falling snow and freezing cold."

This unique narrative probably relates to a former home in India, in the north near the Himalaya mountains, also to Ramachandra, the seventh incarnation of Vishnu, the Polynesian Romaha; also to Tangaroa, the sea god; the black and white spider can be and probably is the period of the winter solstice, as seen by an observer in the nothern hemisphere; and similar to 

Hawaiian astronomy-Ke alanui polohiwa a ke Kuukuu-the black luminous orbit of the spider. (The parentheses in this narrative are inserted by the Author).

The ruins at Lele are very imposing, and appear to have been erected by some pre-historic race. VOYAGES OF HAWAIILOA Hawaii the Great, Hawaiiloa or Hawa-ii-loa or Kekowa-o- 

Hawaii, the alleged discoverer and colonizer of the Hawaiian islands, was born and lived on the eastern coast of Kapa-kapa-ua, the land where his forefathers dwelt before him; which land was also named Ka Aina-kai-Mele-Mele-a Kane or the land of the yellow or handsome sea; this land also had the name of Hawaiikua-uli-kai-00 or Hawaii with the verdant hills and dotted sea. 

Kapa-kapa-ua was a subdivision of the large continent of Eastern Kahiki, and situated on the western part of the continent called Ku-i-lalo or Honua-ku-i-lalo, the Southern Land, renowned for its warlike and savage people; whilst to the west was another continent or large land, Kahiki-moe, or Western Kahiki. 

From the land of his birth, Hawa-ii-loa, who was the youngest of twelve sons, set sail and steered eastward crossing the sea Moanakai-maokioki, the spotted or many colored sea, also named the Moana-kai-popolo, the blue or dark green sea, whose shore line of sloping land extended inland to the volcanoes. 

Hawaiiloa made several voyages between Kapa-kapa-ua and Hawaii, as well as other voyages to the extreme south-to, i ka mole o ka honua; and to a western land where dwelt a people

with turned up eyes, "Ka Lahui maka-lilio"; traveling over this land north and westward he arrived at a large country, Kuahewa-hewa. On his return from this country he brought two white men, who afterwards married Hawaiian women. 

Language items: About the year 300 B. C., the ancient language of the Hindus, the Sanskrit, had become a dead language, its successor Hindustani had taken its place. A Hindu means literally a dweller on the banks of the river Sindhu, the Sanskrit name for the river Indus.

In the Hawaiian language there are many words introduced from Sanskrit, many of these words carry a phallic meaning or suggestion, most of the Polynesian petroglyphs also bear a phallic suggestion, such as those printed in the issue of the Honolulu Advertiser of December 7, 1926, which the Author of this book interpreted, the key word of the glyphs was Procreation. 

In the Hawaiian language is found the word kanaka, a man; in Sanskrit it is janaka, the letters k and j being interchangeable; jan, means to beget; jan joined to aka, causing hence a man, a father; the word kuli in Hawaiian means a deaf or stupid person; in Hindustani the word kuli, means a lowly person, a laborer (kulee); coolie in English means an East Indian or Chinese workman.

MISCELLANEOUS 

The Kilokilo Hoku or Astrologers of the Hawaiians, named the northern limit of the sun in the ecliptic-Ke alanui polohiwa a Kane, the black shining road of Kane. 

The southern limit of the sun in the ecliptic was known as— Ke ala nui polohiwa a Kanaloa, the black shining road of Kanaloa. 

The Equator was named-Ka ala ula a ke Kuukuu, the red or bright road of the Spider-also Ke ala i ka piko o Wakea, the road to the navel of Wakea.

The period of the winter solstice was named-Ke ala polohiwa a ke Kuukuu, the black shining road of the Spider-also Ke ala keokeo a ke Kuukuu, the white road of the Spider.

The Hawaiian year was divided into two seasons-Hooilo, the rainy season, extending from the 20th of November to the 20th of May; the Kau or Tau, the dry season, extended from May 

20th to November the 20th.

The Planets were known as the Na Hoku Aea, the wandering stars; the fixed stars were named Na Hoku Paa.

ANCIENT BELIEFS OF THE HAWAIIANS 

The great road of death, along which the spirits of the dead return to their homeland runs northwest in the Hawaiian islands, is named-Ke Alanui o ka poe Make. 

The west or sunset is named-He Ala nui o ka Make-The great road of death, or the road upon which the spirits of the dead return home to the land of their forefathers. 

The west is also known as—Ke Ala nui maaweula a Kanaloa— The much traveled road of Kanaloa. 

The west is identified with the setting sun-Kaulana a Kanethe resting place of Kane.

The west is Komohana where the sun lies down to sleep. 

The west is the place of Kane nee-nee-the moving or departing sun, hence the sun is Kane.

The east is the great highway of Kane-He Ala nui hele a Kane.

The east is also named-Ke Ala-ula a Kane-The bright road of Kane, the dawning, the morning light.

The sun cannot escape from Hawaii, his house is named Haleakala a vast extinct crater on the island of Maui, whose walls are 

10,000 feet high and its circumference 20 miles; whenever the

sun becomes unruly and tries to get away, the great god Mowee or Maui proceeds to lasso him and pulls him back; the sun has an eyeball which he keeps constantly on the islands, through its pupil-Ka-onohi-o-ka-la; Hawaii also provides a bed in Komohana for the sun, here he lies down to sleep; no other country in the universe provided such comfortable surroundings for the sun as the ancient Hawaiians.

HAWAIIAN SPORTS AND GAMES 

Games, for betting purposes, athletic sports, boxing or mokomoko was the most popular; wrestling or hakoko; kukini or foot races were also popular. 

SURF RIDERS 

Maika; used in bowling, a round polished stone disc about three inches in diameter was used in bowling, on a level track some three feet wide and half a mile long well smoothed, was necessary for playing the game; at intervals of every forty yards on the track two sticks were placed about six inches apart; the one who could bowl the stone the farthest was the winner of the contest. A similar game in which small darts or javelins were used, instead of the maika stones was another popular pastime. 

The Holua; was much practised and popular; a narrow sledge,

called a papa holua, was used for sliding down a hill covered with a coarse stringy grass named pili. The most practised sport was surf-swimming by means of a board, about 8 feet long and 2 feet wide on which the swimmer rode the crest of the incoming breakers with great speed. 

All Polynesians excel as swimmers, as early as 3 years the child learns to swim; today Hawaiian children, and the children of the many foreign races domiciled in the Hawaiian islands are excellent swimmers; daily the sea is used for hours by the children and adults for playing games which are usually played on land. 

The temperature of the ocean around Hawaii seldom is below 70 degrees, this permits of long periods in the sea without harm; hence the Hawaiians are named the "Amphibians”. 

Feats of endurance under water, three minutes and over, and long ocean swims are numerous in the various channels between the islands, strong currents, the under-tow and man-eating sharks do however, imperil the swimmer. North of the 18th parallel the current around the islands generally sets southwest urged on by the northeast trade winds; in the winter season the equatorial current sets eastward especially in southwest storms. 

The Hawaiians do not fear the sea, if there is a disaster and loss of a vessel, the navigator is censured for his lack of skill. 

AN EPIC OF THE SEA 

The channel which separates the island of Hawaii from its westerly neighbor the island of Maui is named Alenuihaha, its width is some 35 miles, the set of the current is south by west, and when the trade wind from the northeast blows strongly, the speed of the current is 4 to 5 miles an hour. At the hour of noon on Sunday, May 10, 1840, the Hawaiian schooner "Keola" about 40 tons burden, caught unprepared in a sudden squall, capsized and foundered eight miles off Upolu point, the most northern cape of the island of Hawaii, at this position the strong current carries the swimmer away from the nearest land, the 

Kohala coast.

THE AHULAU OKUU 

A description of a mysterious malady which ravaged Hawaii in the year 1804, and named by the Hawaiian people the Ahulau Okuu; the former word ahulau means, a pestilence; okuu, a verb, means, to sit up because no comfort is afforded on lying down, due to pain in the belly.

Owing to the intense pains in their bellies and cramps in their legs, those who had the new disease could neither stand up nor lie down in comfort; to obtain some relief from pain the afflicted ones sat on their hams, with their bodies bowed forward, clasping their bellies; or sat on the ground with their legs extended and their bodies bent over in front, thin discharges like sour starch water flowed frequently from the bowels, some vomited continuously; their faces and bodies became blue and cold like the dead, their eyes grew small and sank into the head; the sick craved for water, and died quickly and in large numbers; speedy burial was impossible, so graves were scraped in the sand near the shores of the sea to receive the corpses. 

The sick and those who nursed them, ate baked sweet potatoes, the well people were too weary to seek the taro to make poi. It happened often, those who were not affected with the disease and were cooking, suddenly were attacked by the “ahulau okuu” and died quickly before the potatoes were baked. (The foregoing lucid statement of a rapidly fatal epidemic disease is typical of malignant Asiatic Cholera. Author.)

For four months the so called new disease ravaged all the islands, and is said to have caused 22,000 deaths on the island of Oahu alone, where the King Kamehameha I was then living, he was stricken with the Ahulau Okuu, but recovered. Cholera was apparently an unknown disease to the Hawaiians; China an endemic seat of Cholera was visited in 1787 by Chief Keawe 

Kaiana, and three other Hawaiians who remained there three months; other vessels had contact between Hawaii and China in the Sandal wood trade, but there was no history of Cholera being carried to Hawaii; the Russian ships Don and Neva of 

Admiral Krusenstern's fleet visited Hawaii in June 1804, (see page 34).

Cholera germs enter the system of man through the mouth,

from polluted water or milk or food; a carrier may retain the germs for months, his bowel discharges can infect others. On 

August 22, 1895, a Chinese passenger from Canton, landed in Honolulu, from the steamer "Belgic", the steamer's bilge water was pumped into the harbor, decaying fruit and vegetables were also dumped overboard; from all these sources there later resulted 

88 cases of Asiatic Cholera in the city of Honolulu, causing 63 deaths; again in the year 1911, on February 23, the disease was introduced to our city.

EARLY BRITISH RESIDENTS 

In the year 1790, in the month of March, two American vessels the "Eleanor", and the "Fair American", were sailing in Hawaiian waters off the Kona coast, seeking to engage in trade with the Hawaiians; Kameeiamoku, a high chief, with a fleet of canoes boarded the "Fair American", in the guise of traders; and whilst the captain and crew of the vessel were off guard, the Hawaiians suddenly attacked them, killing the captain and all the crew excepting the mate Isaac Davis; John Young, the boatswain of the "Eleanor" who was on shore at Kealakekua, was also captured and kept a prisoner by the order of Kamehameha. 

Young and Davis were kindly treated, given valuable lands, and elevated to the rank of chiefs; from their knowledge of carpentry, boat-building, iron work, and the use of firearms, they became valuable aids to Kamehameha. They were his ever present advisers and companions and the Hawaiian people held them in respect and admiration. The late Queen Emma, wife of Kamehameha IV, was a grand-daughter of John Young. 

Isaac Davis, was a native of Milfordhaven, Pembrokeshire, Wales; he died in Honolulu, April 1810, allegedly from poison. 

John Young was born in Liverpool, Lancashire, England, in the year 1759, he died in Honolulu, on December 16, 1835, aged

THE GREAT HISTORIAN DAVID MALO 

David Malo, (1793-1853) was born in Keauhou, North Kona,

Island of Hawaii, about the year 1793; the coastal village of 

Keauhou is about nine miles north of Ka-awa-loa, where England's greatest and best navigator was slain on Sunday morning about 8 a. m., February 14, 1779. Kona was also the birthplace of the historians Kamakau, father and son, and Pilipo (Philip) known as the "Lion", an orator, who publicly told a certain royal personage "Thou art no chief, thou art no king, you are the tool of haole schemers"-this blunt speech deeply stung the Royal one, so to even up, when Pilipo was a candidate for election to the Legislature, 150 soldiers of the royal army were transported to Kona just before the date of the election, and their votes defeated Pilipo.

Unheralded, unsung, forgotten, Malo the historian of ancient and modern Hawaiian history, in person was tall, slim, with a long, lean face, very high forehead, intellectual and refined cast of features, an unusual type of Polynesian face; he was endowed with a prodigious memory, knew more about ancient Hawaiian history and folklore, than any other Hawaiian at the period in which he lived and wrote, or even up to the present day. Few 

Hawaiians engage or have engaged in literary pursuits; hence to encounter a writer of Hawaiian history is a great treat, the ancient Hawaiians not having a written language had to memorize their history by oral traditions. Malo having been born and lived many years in the district of North Kona, was eminently qualified to describe two highly important epochs, the arrival of the Spaniards in 1527, and the re-discovery of Hawaii by Cook's ships.

In the year 1823, at the age of thirty years, Malo began to attend school at Lahaina, Maui; his instructor the Rev. William

Richards of the American Mission, speedily recognized the intelligent and receptive mind of his pupil; he easily out-distanced all the other students, and made rapid progress in his various studies, he could easily read and write English, but was not a fluent speaker of it, he spoke the language with difficulty. In 1831, a high school was established at Lahainaluna, Malo was one of the first students to enter it, at the age of 38 years; in 1834, he

began to write in the Hawaiian language, a History of Ancient Hawaii, it was completed and published in 1838; this book is a classic, and another work on Hawaiian Antiquities, stand as a monument to his memory today. 

HOSTILITY TOWARDS FOREIGNERS 

In the year 1845, the Hawaiian people began to agitate and protest against the encroachment of the foreign residents on their rights; on the Island of Maui, David Malo became the chosen leader of a large faction of discontented Hawaiians who petitioned 

King Kamehameha III, to enforce the following requests: 

1. To dismiss all foreigners from Government offices, excepting Mr. Richards, Minister of Public Instruction. 

2. Not to permit the naturalization of any more foreigners.

About 350 had been naturalized previous to the above year, 1845. 

3. Not to sell any more land (Government) to foreigners.

THE FLEET OF SAAVEDRA 

On October 31, 1527, Admiral Alvarado de Saavedra sailed from Zacatula, Mexico, for the Moluccas, spice islands; the fleet was struck by a heavy south west Kona storm, west of Kauai and scattered, two of the smaller vessels disappeared and were never heard from, the large ship the galeon weathered the storm and sailed on alone to Manila. The Hawaiians fixed the time of the wreck of the Konaliloha in Kona in the reign of the aforementioned King Kealiiokaloa, which corresponds to and corroborates the known date of the loss of the two ships of Saavedra's squadron in November or December 1527. 

The Florida was Admiral Saavedra's flagship and carried a crew of fifty men-the caravel St. Diego (phonetic Iago, St. 

James) carried a crew of forty-five men and several Nunswithout a doubt this vessel was the one lost in the Kona surf; the name of the captain was Louis de Cardosa or known to the

Hawaiians as Kukana-loa, big Susa; the smallest vessel of the fleet was the Corvette Espiritu Santo (Holy Ghost) in command of Pedro Fuentes, with a crew of fifteen men. No other 

Europeans but the Spaniards were navigating the Pacific at this date, 1527.

Malo predicted the doom of his race; due to the influx of strangers, their business ability, greed, and bringing in their train diseases, intoxicating liquors, he branded Stephenson a devil in human form, cursed by all respectable Hawaiians for introducing rum making into the islands, with consequent demoralization of the people, causing them to acquire drunken habits, gambling, laziness, incitation to crimes of violence, etc., the end being misery,

poverty, disease and death. Under the title of "The Great Black 

Ghoul", he describes the ravages of the dreadful phagedenic venereal disease, Kaukau, Tertiary Syphilis, (the Companion of Love) first introduced into the Hawaiian islands by the crews of 

Cook's ships.

Malo flays Cook for his lack of care, in not preventing the diseased sailors and marines of the crews, from having shore leave; the disease they carried ashore caused over 12,000 deaths on the island of Hawaii, in the first twelve years after Cook's death; Malo also charges the Commanders of the Resolution and 

Discovery with murder, by killing more than 100 defenseless

Hawaiians in the fight at Kaawaloa, when Cook was slain. 

Population of the islands at the date of Cook's arrival, 1778, was estimated by Malo at 360,000, and by islands as follows: 

Hawaii, 165,000; Maui, Molokai, Lanai, 90,000; Oahu, 65,000;

Kauai, 40,000. By a census taken in the year 1823, the population had decreased to 142,000; these figures show a decrease of 

218,000 within a period of forty-five years.

David Malo's death was hastened by sorrow, shame and property losses caused by the dissolute and drunken habits of his wife; she repeatedly deserted him and lived in the town of Lahaina, the then capital city of the islands, associating with the gambling and drinking element, augmented by the semi-criminal and lawless crews from the fleet of whaling ships at anchor off Lahaina. 

Malo died on October 21, 1853; at his own request, his body was buried on the summit of the hill Paupau, 2,254 feet elevation, mauka of the Lahainaluna school; hence, from his lonely grave the spirit of the dead historian looks down upon the scene of his earthly labors.

Where else can be found an aboriginal historian equal to Malo? 

After a few years of instruction in an alien language, begun at the age of 30 years, he wrote a history of his own country, in its own language. No Samoan, Tongan, Maori, Tahitian, Malay,

Malagasy, Javanese, Filipino, or Indian has ever accomplished what Malo did. The Aztec Indians alone, approach the genius of Malo; the Spanish friars in fifteen years taught them to become excellent writers and Latinists, in later years Rincon, an Indian, wrote the best grammar in the Aztec tongue; also the Aztecs,

Valeriano and Ixtlilxochitl (Ixt-lilexo-chittel) wrote excellent histories of ancient Mexico.

THE PROWESS OF HAWAIIAN WARRIORS 

When Captain James Cook fell into knee-deep water at Kaawaloa landing, the nearby Hawaiian warriors charged with their spears of Kauila wood through the musket fire of the English marines standing near Cook and slew four of them; yet the pinnace (a boat with 8 oarsmen) not more than five or six yards distant from the beach, with a lieutenant and 13 marines, made no attempt to recover the bodies nor Cook's body. Under the heavy fire of the cannon and musket fire from the Resolution and Discovery, the Hawaiian warriors withdrew inland carrying off their dead Chiefs (30) and 70 others then the shore was deserted-and for four hours (until noon) the bodies of the dead,

Cook's and the marines, were allowed to bake in the sun, whilst the ship's boats with heavily armed crews remained inactive near the shore, evidently fearing a new charge and attack from the Hawaiians-finally about noon, unmolested, the Hawaiian warriors carried off the bodies inland. In England an official inquiry brought to light the aforementioned disgraceful display of cowardice; and so intense was the public indignation, that Captain 

Clerk, of the Discovery, died from the effects of worry and censure, aggravated by his former ill health. 

When Calderon landed at Kau, Hawaii, in the year 1627, he was astonished on viewing the large stature and warlike appearance of the Hawaiian warriors (Indians), and fearing an attack from them, he made a pretense of waiting for supplies of provisions, and having completed the repairs to the rudder of the "Santa Maria", he sailed away by stealth in the night, and when out of sight of Hawaii (Isla de Mesa), at daybreak Calderon's officers and crew held a Te Deum Laudamus service, giving thanks to God for aiding their escape from the giant cannibals. 

At Waimea, Kauai, in the year 1816, the Russians began the erection of an alleged storehouse, for trading and storing merchandise, thinking to deceive the Hawaiians; however the presence of embrasures in the walls and also the raising of the Russian flag caused suspicion. Kamehameha I, heard of the acts of the 

Russians, and sent orders for the dismantling of the fort and the ejection of its occupants-these orders were fearlessly carried out by the former warriors of King Kaumualii. 

When the United States of America was forced into declaring war on Germany in April, 1917, the former warlike spirit of the Hawaiians reappeared, they did not wait to be drafted, they enlisted voluntarily, without any delay or reservation. 

THE MENTAWI 

The Mentawi islands lie some 70 miles off the west coast of the great island of Sumatra. There are 21 islands, four large and seventeen small. All are of volcanic formation. They lie off shore near the base of the celebrated mountain Indrapura (City of Indra), 12,500 feet elevation, now a quiescent volcano. According to the belief of the Indian peoples, it is the abode of Indra,

the Fire god of the Indo-Aryan peoples, a King god of the Middle 

Realm, the fire of the air, the lightning.

The four largest islands of the group are Siberut, Sikitan or Sipora, North and South Paggi. They are surrounded by coral reefs and volcanic shoals, difficult of access due to a huge ever-prevalent surf. The area of all the islands is 1224 square miles, less than one-fifth the area of the Hawaiian group. Their latitude is from 1 degree South to 3½ degrees North; their 

Longitude is 100 degrees East.

The inhabitants of the Mentawi islands, in features, habits, customs and language, have a distinct and remarkable affinity with the Polynesians. They are almost a pure blooded race of 

Aryan stock, and are the only survivors of an eastward emigration of an Indo-Aryan people who antedate the Malay peoples in Sumatra, the Battas and others. The Mentawis have not fused nor mixed their blood with other Malay races. As a rule miscegenation, if any, is rare. 

Resembling the Hawaiians, they adorn their heads and necks with leis or wreaths of flowers and leaves, and wear pink and white coral ornaments. Their food is fish and sago derived from the pith of several species of palms. The Javanese and Malay word for sago is sagu. The stature of the Mentawis is from five feet to five feet, six inches.

THE RUM KING ARRIVES 

William Stephenson, an Englishman known as the Rum King, a native of Barrow-in-Furness, a seaport in northwest Lancashire, England; a convict, a lifer confined in Millbank penitentiary, Westminster, London, for heinous crimes, was deported to Botany bay, New South Wales, Australia; he escaped and landed in 

Honolulu about the year 1796. This man was the first distiller of alcoholic liquors in Hawaii, and he taught the Hawaiians the art of distillation. The too frequent use and abuse of alcoholic drinks has been responsible for sapping the lives of many of the Hawaiians.

Stephenson took up residence in Manoa valley, established a distillery there, made rum from sugar-cane, also other strong alcoholic drinks from rice, bananas, ti root, and potatoes. He was an expert distiller, had learnt the art in Kingston, Jamaica, West Indies; his alcoholic products were of pleasant taste and strength and were in great demand; the Hawaiians unused to alcoholic beverages became speedily intoxicated; also the chiefs so craved for the new drinks, that they engaged Stephenson to build stills in their homes; within a year, more than 100 stills were in operation in Honolulu alone; orgies, drunkenness and riotous conduct spread over Oahu and the other islands; drunken men and women slept alongside the roads, cultivation of food products, taro and potatoes almost ceased, famine threatened and so great became the demoralization of the people, that in the year 1800, Kamehameha I, the king, who himself had been a drinker of alcoholic liquors, and feeling ill-effects from the stupefying action of the drinks-ceased drinking, sent for his bodyguards and high chiefs, and ordered them to tabu all drinking of strong liquors, and destroy all the stills they could locate. 

Nemesis, the goddess of vengeance arrived on the scene; 

Stephenson, wealthy in money and lands, lost all through gambling and thefts; he died in Manoa valley about the year 1822.

ADMIRAL ADAM IVAN KRUSENSTERN 

(1770-1846) Russian navigator, explorer and scientist; served and trained with the British navy, from 1793 to 1799. In the year 1802, Russia purchased 2 ships from England, whose names were changed to the Neva and the Don; after refitting and being provisioned, the ships sailed from Kronstadt in August 1803 on a voyage to the Pacific; they surveyed the Marquesa islands, and on June 13, 1804 arrived at Hawaii, and made an intensive study of island products, resources, fisheries and harbors. Krusenstern discovered an islet 800 miles southwest of Kauai. Did these 

Russian ships introduce Cholera to Honolulu harbor in 1804, the Ahulau okuu? It is not known the exact date when this new disease began on Oahu-but the two Russian ships left an epidemic disease at the Marquesa islands, also a Samoan historian

states that about forty years prior to 1844, a pestilence resembling Cholera prevailed in Samoa, from which thousands of the people died.

In the year 1801, Krusenstern's ships had formed part of Admiral Nelson's armada, blockading the French fleet in Aboukir bay, Egypt, a seat of endemic Cholera-many of the crews of the English fleet had died from this disease.

HISTORY OF PORTUGAL 

Portugal, formerly Portingal, the Portus Galorum of the

Romans, was a part of the ancient province of Lusitania; it has a frontage on the Atlantic of 400 miles; it has a population of 

6,600,000; its area is 35,490 square miles, hence it is larger than the State of Maine, whose area is 33,040 square miles. 

Lisbon, Lisboa, or Olisipo of the ancient Lusitanians, is the capital city, and its latitude is 39° North; its population is about 

515,000, it is built on seven hills, and therefore has a resemblance to the city of Rome; it is seven miles from the ocean, and stands on the right or north bank of the river Tagus.

The estuary is one and one-half miles wide, but inland, above the city, to the eastward, the river widens into a large tidal lake, four to eight miles wide and eleven miles long, with deep water in the lake and through its narrow entrance; these great natural advantages make Lisbon harbor one of the finest and safest in the world for vessels of light or deep draught; it would afford a safe and commodious anchorage for the United States fleet, which visited Honolulu harbor, its roadstead, and Pearl Harbor, in the month of May, 1935.

Lisbon, in the 16th century became one of the intellectual centers of Europe; poets, historians, philosophers and artists, all flocked to it; it was the home of Camoes, the noted Portuguese poet, before his banishment to Goa, India. 

In the year 1580, Spain conquered Portugal, and held the country in subjection until 1640, when the Portuguese regained their freedom by a revolution. In 1910, Portugal overthrew the monarchy and became a republic. 

ANGLO-PORTUGUESE ALLIANCE 

From the latter part of the 17th century, all British admirals and captains who had visited Lisbon and anchored in its harbor, uniformly urged upon the British admiralty the desirableness of obtaining from Portugal the exclusive control of Lisbon harbor, in case of war breaking out between England and any continental power. Portugal on being approached was only too willing to

make a Treaty with Britain granting the privileges desired; hence there has been an "Offensive and Defensive Alliance" existing for many years.

THE UNITED STATES OF BRAZIL 

Brazil, formerly the largest and most valuable possession of the Colonial Empire of Portugal, became an independent monarchy in the year 1822. This was the only monarchy that ever existed on the American continent.

Napoleon I, was responsible for the establishing of this Monarchy; in 1807 a French army invaded Portugal and took possession of the country; John VI, the king, and other members of the Braganza family fled to Brazil on November 29, 1807, from Lisbon, arriving at Bahia, January 21, 1808.

By the decree of 1815, the Portuguese sovereignty assumed the title of "The United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil, and Algarve". On September 7, 1822, Brazil declared its independence of Portugal on the plain of Ypiranja, near the city of San Paulo, southern Brazil; a monument commemorates this event. The son of King John, Dom Pedro, was proclaimed Emperor of Brazil. 

On November 15, 1889, Brazil became a Republic, modeled on the Constitution of the United States of America; the then Emperor, Dom Pedro II, was pensioned and taken by a warship to Lisbon.

THE DAWN OF EXPLORATION 

The age of discovery began in the 15th and 16th centuries, during this period Portugal became a great maritime power, and was the first European nation to send out vessels to explore the Oceans, and seek for supposedly inaccessible islands and continents. Portugal initiated the search for a sea route to India, and in the year 1498 began to trade with that country.

FAMOUS MEN OF PORTUGAL 

Prince Henry of Portugal, younger son of King John I, of 

Portugal, born in Oporto, March 4, 1394, surnamed "The

Navigator", became celebrated as a munificent patron of navigators and explorers; it was due to his genius and foresight, that 

Portugal was launched on a career of exploration and discovery; he was also one of the famous generals of the army of his country.

Every year he paid for the supplies and crews of several caravels (Spanish, caravela, a large sloop of war); his expeditions discovered the island of Madeira in 1419; the Azores in 1431; 

Gil Eannes was the first of Prince Henry's captains to round Cape Bojador, on the northwest coast of Africa, and discover the coast of Senegal in 1433; in 1441 Cape Blanco was rounded; in 1446 the Cape Verde islands were discovered by Antonio de Nola, also in 1455 Captain Cadamosto reached the Gambia river. 

He was born in Venice 1432, died there 1477.

Prince Henry lived at Sagres, a desolate spot, where he would have seclusion and freedom from visitors. Here he established a school for navigators and explorers, and those who were already qualified established themselves at Sagres and awaited appointment to sea duty.

The Prince denied himself all luxuries, and led a Spartan life devoted to his chosen work; he died at Sagres, November 13, 1460; but the stimulus and inspiration he had given to maritime enterprises continued after his death, and resulted in the building up of a Portuguese Empire in Brazil and India. 

Prince Henry also sent out expeditions seeking to discover the kingdom of Prester John, a fabulous Christian Monarch, who was believed in the 12th century to have conquered the Mussulmans, and established a great empire in Abyssinia, or beyond 

Armenia or Persia. 

Bartholomeu Dias, (?1450-?1500), sailed from Lisbon in 1487 with two small vessels, Captain Infante commanded the accompanying ship; the fleet went south following the coast of Africa, and discovered the Cape of Good Hope.

Vasco da Gama, (1460-1524), descendent of a high born family, at Simes, Portugal; became a valiant and renowned

soldier; fought in wars against Castile, and later a bold and intrepid navigator.

He was chosen to command an expedition to India, and sailed. from Lisbon on July 8, 1497, with a fleet of four ships; he followed the course of Bartholomeu Dias along the west African coast, and four months later the ships rounded the Cape of Good Hope. On Christmas day the coast of Natal was sighted, hence the name given to the land (dies natalis); sailing along the east coast of Africa, Delagoa bay, Quillimane and Mozambique were discovered. Early in January, 1498, Malindi was reached, here the fleet made repairs and took on stores; a pilot was obtained from the local Indian merchants, and steering eastward Da Gama arrived at Calicut on the Malabar coast of India, on May 20, 1498. after a voyage of 23 days. He spent several months at this port, observing the possibilities of establishing a Portuguese colony to gain control of the rich Indian trade; but the Rajah (Prince) of Calicut showed hostility to Da Gama, so he fought his way out of the harbor, and sailed home to Portugal, arriving at 

Lisbon on September 8, 1499. To avenge the insults he had received from the Rajah of Calicut, and to punish him for the killing of Portuguese workers in the factory, which Pedro Alvares Cabral had established at Calicut in 1501, Da Gama in 1502, was given the title of Admiral of India, made commander of ten well armed ships, which sailed from Lisbon for Calicut; on the voyage, colonies were founded at Mozambique and Sofala. 

On arriving at Calicut, Da Gama promptly destroyed the

Rajah's fleet and bombarded the town; proceeding to Cochin, he succeeded in collecting rich cargoes then he sailed for home arriving in Lisbon, December, 1503.

Da Gama's great ability as a navigator and statesman was now established, and he was given the titles of Count Vidigueira and Admiral of the Indies. He remained inactive for 20 years, then in 1524 he was appointed Viceroy of India and dispatched with a fleet to India to rectify abuses in the administration of the Portuguese Asian colonies. Da Gama died soon after his arrival at Cochin, December 24, 1524; he had opened up a valuable trade route to India, that later enriched Portugal, and placed this country in the ranks of one of the foremost maritime

nations of Europe, and brought about the colonization of the Far East, by opening its commerce to the western world. 

Pedro Alvares Cabral, (1460-1525). In January, 1500, he sailed from Lisbon with 13 vessels on a voyage to India; his course was set too far to the westward, hence he accidentally discovered the coast of Brazil, near Bahia, on April 22, 1500. 

Affonso d' Albuquerque, (1453-1515), surnamed "The Great", and the "Portuguese Mars", was born at Alhandra, near Lisbon, and died at sea near Goa, India.

He became a celebrated navigator and conquistador, the founder of the Portuguese Empire in the East. He commanded an expedition to India in 1503; and returned in 1504. A second flotilla under his command conquered Goa in 1510, the whole coast of Malabar, Ceylon, the Sunda islands, the peninsula of Malacca and the island of Ormuz, at the entrance to the gulf of Persia. 

The Shah of Persia demanded tribute for the occupation of 

Ormuz; in reply Albuquerque delivered bullets and swords to the Shah's ambassador-stating "This is Portugal's coin for tribute." He maintained strict military discipline in the conquered possessions, and made the Portuguese name dreaded and respected in the East.

The kings of Siam and Pegu sought his alliance. Albuquerque was energetic, far seeing, wise, humane, equitable; he was feared, but his virtues greatly impressed the Indian peoples. 

Antonio d' Abreu, Portuguese navigator, discovered the northern coast of the great island of New Guinea, in 1511. 

Admiral Diogo Lopes de Sequeira, a companion of Magellan, in 1509 reached Banda and Amboyna, longitude 130° East, returning to Portugal in 1512.

Admiral Pedro Luiz da Rocha, on a voyage to the Moluccas, in 1527, sailed easterly and discovered the western Caroline islands, and named them in honor of Admiral Sequeira, the "Sequeira" islands.

In the year 1686, Admiral Francisco de Lazeano, of the Spanish navy, renamed the islands, Caroline, in honor of Charles 

II, of Spain. 

Admiral Count Ferdinand Magalhaes, (1480-1521), known to the English speaking people as Magellan, a Portuguese nobleman,

was born at Sabarosa, Portugal, in the year 1480. The title of 

Count in continental Europe, in the order of precedence, ranks as an English Earl.

At the age of 24 years Magellan enlisted in the Portuguese navy, as a volunteer for the Indian voyage of the first Portuguese 

Viceroy Francisco d' Almeida; on arriving at Calicut the fleet made necessary repairs, took on cargo and returned home; but 

Magellan remained behind; in 1509 he and Admiral Sequeira, with other vessels sailed eastward through the Strait of Malacca, the Java sea, to Banda and Amboyna, observing the great value of the Spice islands, and loading a cargo of nutmegs and cloves (worth $6 a pound), Magellan returned home to Portugal in 1512, round the Cape of Good Hope. The "Portuguese Mars" Affonso d' Albuquerque, died in 1515, leaving a vacancy in the Viceroyship of the Portuguese Eastern Empire. 

Vasco da Gama and Magellan were on the inactive list. Though both were capable and able men with a thorough knowledge of 

Indian affairs, neither was appointed. Then Magellan seeing no opportunity for preferment, resigned from the Portuguese navy, left Portugal and traveled to Madrid. The career of 

Magellan in the service of Spain will be described later.

Luiz Vaz de Camoes, celebrated Portuguese poet, was born in

Lisbon, 1525, and died there 1579. He studied at the University of Coimbra, and was talented. His love for Catharina d' Atyada caused him much trouble; she was a lady at the Court, and disputes and duels with other lovers of this lady, caused Camoes to be exiled to Santarem.

From despair he became a soldier, and served in the Portuguese fleet sent against Morocco; on the battle fields he composed poetry, and danger stimulated his genius and animated his courage; before the town of Ceuta, under siege, an arrow penetrated his right eye, and deprived him of its sight; he received no recognition for his bravery, and thus embittered he embarked for India in 1553. His powerful imagination was excited by the heroic deeds of his countrymen, so he celebrated their glory in an Epic.

Camoes, displeased with the abuses of the Government of India, wrote a satire; for this offence he was banished from Goa to 

Macao. Soon after he was removed to the Moluccas, where his decree of banishment was removed and Camoes was returned to Macao, and appointed to a petty government office; the Lusiad, his chief poem was composed during his exile. 

Camoes was recalled from banishment, and on the way home, was shipwrecked at Mekon, Cochin, China, but saved himself by swimming, holding in one hand above the water, the manuscript of his poem. Landing at Goa, India, new persecutions met him, he was charged with embezzlement of funds whilst in office at 

Macao, and finally arrived at Lisbon in 1569.

The young King Sebastian, of Portugal, accepted the dedication of his Epic, and it appeared in two editions in 1572. Later

the king was killed in battle, and Camoes lost his patron and friend, and all hope of further assistance. His Indian slave begged in the streets of Lisbon at night, in order to prevent his master from starving. In his misery Camoes wrote other lyric poems, nevertheless, this brilliant hero of Portuguese literature, the ornament of his country, died in a hospital alone and neglected. His work not appreciated during his life, created a furor after his death in 1579, and a splendid monument to his memory was erected in 1596.

Luiz Vaz de Camoes, has chosen the first voyage to India, of Da Gama, for the subject of his masterpiece "Os Lusiadas”, a magnificent Epic poem-the national glory of the Portuguese people appears in every form, which an imaginative mind could suggest. Epic, as it is the outstanding masterpiece of the Portuguese literature, and also one of the greatest epics of the modern worldit is an Epic in ten cantos, containing 1,102, eight line stanzas, and has been translated into 15 different languages; there are nine published versions in English.

PORTUGUESE WERE DISCOVERERS OF AMERICA By John Jardim 

Once more the discussion is brought before the public: Who was the first discoverer of North America?

In the issue of December 2, 1932, of the Jornal Portugues, a dispatch from Seville, Spain, states the claim is made, in the year 1452 a Portuguese, Pedro Vasques da Fronteira had knowledge of the existence of America. Now comes the historian Corteson, who brings him to the front in order that My Lady Confusion can make as much fun as she likes with our historians. 

Neglect from our rulers, the immense greatness of our maritime discoveries, and the jealousy of Spain, were responsible for the discovery of the new continent not being credited to those who truly deserved it.

Portugal, fated by her geographical location, by the daring of a handful of fearless mariners, having at their head the adventurous and enlightened genius Prince Henry, the navigator, tore the darkness of the ocean, and prepared the world for new worlds. 

American history states that Christopher Columbus was the first navigator to see the shores of America, his description was vague; he is stated to be a Genoese trained in the Portuguese Naval school. The whole new American generation have believed this, hence only positive facts can dispel the erroneous idea of a man, who forgetting the teachings of his childhood and the origin of his blood, assumed a puzzling name, which the solvers of charades. have endeavored to explain in various ways. 

That erudite investigator, Major Santos Ferreira, has placed before us, that Christopher Columbus was one Salvador Gonsalves Zarco, a grandchild of the first grantee of the Island of Madeira, and tells us that he never believed the son of a wool carder or weaver of Genoa or Savona, long before the discovery of America, could receive letters addressed to him by King John II, in one of which he is called "our special friend." Again, speaking of his marriage with the daughter of Bartholomew Perestrelo, of the

Island of Porto Santo, named Filipa Monis, who through her mother, Dona Isabel Monis de Melo, was related to the Braganzas, and therefore to the mother of D. Manuel I, and to whom John 

Esmeraldo, a Fleming of noble blood, would not have given hospitality for quite a time in his Funchal palace, nor would he have invited him to become the godfather of his second son, had he not been related to the nobility, all this having happened many years before his sailing to America, and before his decoration with the titles of Admiral, Viceroy and Knight of the Golden Spur.

In the city of Funchal there is a street named Esmeraldo, and even today there is to be seen, one of the windows of a house where for many years resided the man who is called Christopher Columbus; no doubt can be entertained, but that Columbus was a Portuguese, most probably a native of Madeira, son of Helen 

Gonsalves Camara, the eldest daughter of John Gonsalves Zarco, the first Captain-grantee of Funchal.

Modern historians competing for national honors, often offer incomplete and non-conclusive facts, which have to be noted complaisantly for the elucidation of the riddle. 

First, we have the Genoese, who, traveling through villages and along coastlines, offer some thirty localities as the birthplace of him, who even now bears the title of Discoverer of America. 

Next we have the Spaniards, who have endeavored carefully to prove Don Christopher Colon was neither Genoese or something like one, but a native of Galicia. Finally the Portuguese historians have their turn. What have these to say?

Forty years before the discovery of the North American continent, the Portuguese were already established in the land of Santa Cruz, which we now call Brazil, where they had built sugar factories; and twenty-nine years before the first voyage of Columbus, other Portuguese navigators were familiar with the existence of the new continent, North America. Amongst other navigators, the following had been there-Corte Real, father and sons, Alvaro Martins Homen, as well as the unfortunate Affonso 

Sanches, who, on his return from America, meeting in Madeira with Christopher Columbus, was there cowardly murdered, and robbed of important papers connected with the discovery of the

new continent; which then the adventurer proposed to undertake, having with high treason, offered his services to Spain. 

What were the reasons which caused Columbus to hide his family name? The best and most notable investigators inform us that Columbus addressed himself to King John II, with proposals to be officially known as the discoverer of the new lands. 

His stipulations and services not having been accepted, he turned enemy of the King, and joined the partisans of the Duke of Bragança, Don Fernando, who just then was conspiring against the royal sovereignty. The conspiracy was discovered, and the 

Duke was executed on the scaffold, at Evora, June 21, 1483, the rest of the conspirators saved their lives by escaping to 

Castile, France and England. 

About the same time, another conspiracy was discovered, which also had a fatal ending; on August 23, 1484, at Setubal, John II himself slew the Duke of Vizeu, the chief of the conspiracy. As a malediction the fugitives were persecuted everywhere, the exiles had recourse to the ancient strategem, change of names in order to hide their nationality. In proof of the supposition that 

Columbus was an accomplice in one of these conspiracies, we have a letter written by John II on March 30, 1488, addressed to Christopher Columbus, calling him "our special friend at Seville", in which letter he said not to be afraid to come to Portugal, giving orders to his police, that Columbus was "not to be arrested, accused, summoned nor molested for any cause or crime."

By what we have said above, one sees clearly that the alleged discoverer of America was a Portuguese in the service of Spain, similar to Fernando Magalhaes Faleiro (Magellan), John Serrao,

Duarte Barbosa, and others. The names which Columbus gave to the discovered lands, were names of Portuguese places as: 

Rio de Belem, Santa Maria de Belem, Santiago and Santa Maria,

Cabo Verde, Iha das Cabras (Goats Island), Vale do Paraiso (Valley of Paradise), Santa Cruz, San Miguel, and othersamongst them numbers of places in Alentejo, Madeira, and Porto 

Santo. 

Herman Columbus, biographer and historian of his father, affirms that Columbus desired his origin and native country to be

unknown and uncertain. Even the very Castilian family, of 

Donna Beatriz Enriquez de Cordova, his sweetheart, did not know where he came from.

The coat-of-arms which Columbus made for himself is very significative and conclusive: a field with five anchors, arranged like the "quinas" of the five escutcheons in the banner of Portugal. 

Patrocinio Ribeiro tells us, before this revealing proof: "This symbol number of the five anchors corresponding to the five "quinas" and the five "escutcheons" of our glorious banner, reveals the mystery that Columbus was a Portuguese, a graduate from Prince Henry's naval academy, because only a Portuguese would look for a symbolical analogy of the epic flag of the "Quinas."

SPAIN SPAIN THE GREATEST AND MOST ENLIGHTENED POWER-1500-1640 

At this date, A. D.

When Count Ferdinand Magellan arrived at Madrid, he made known his desire to offer his services to the Spanish government, and being of noble blood, dignified, of distinguished bearing, handsome and courteous to all, he immediately made a great impression on the haughty Spanish courtiers; they brought him before

Magellan full of enthusiasm, a facile and convincing speaker, and due to his eight years residence in the East Indies, he was fully competent to discuss the Indian trade, and its great possibilities for obtaining cargoes of great value. Magellan speedily became a great favorite at the Spanish court and of the King.

The plans of Magellan for enriching Spain, by establishing a western trade route to India and China, met with the speedy approval of the Spanish Marine authorities. A fleet of five armed vessels was fitted out, and Magellan was appointed Commander.

On August 10, 1519, the fleet left Seville, a tidal city on the south bank of the river Guadalquiver, some 90 miles inland from the sea, and passed down the river to the sea-port San Lucar de 

Barrameda, leaving this port on September 20, 1519. Magellan's flagship was named the "Trinidad", and the five vessels of his fleet were manned by 270 men, of many nationalities. 

Two hundred and sixty-eight names have been preserved, and only 237 of these received pay; those who did not were aristocrats and gentlemen adventurers who volunteered their services, such was Antonio Pigâfetta, the historian of the voyage, a native of Vicenza, Italy; out of the 270 men, the Spaniards were in the majority, 188; some 37 were Portuguese; 30 were Italians, mostly Genoese; 

19 French, one English and one German.

On October 21, 1520, Magellan discovered the Atlantic entrance to the strait which bears his name; it is 360 miles in length, and the fleet took 16 days to reach the Pacific entrance. 

The current races through some of the "Narrows" in the strait towards the Pacific, at a speed of 14 to 17 miles an hour.

On November 9, 1520, Magellan's fleet passed through the Pacific entrance of the strait, and in a sheltered bay to the north the vessels anchored, where they were then given a general overhauling, and two of the ships were abandoned as unseaworthy. 

On November 28, 1520, Magellan with the three remaining ships sailed on a northwesterly course over the great ocean, to which he gave the name "Pacific", on account of its calmness and balmy winds; later the Friars named it "The Sea of our Lady of Loretto." 

After a voyage of 98 days from the Magellan strait, on March 

6, 1521, Magellan sighted a group of islands in Latitude 15°

North, and Longitude 161° East of the meridian of Ferro, which he named "Las Islas de las Velas Latinas; Islands of the Lateen 

Sails"; because the sea became dotted with numbers of outrigger canoes with lateen sails, which met the ships of the fleet; later the islands became known as the Ladrones, islands of Thieves, a grave and offensive name for the islanders, then Chamorros.

Magellan's pioneer voyage across the Pacific was most remarkable, it traversed an ocean space of nearly 10,000 miles, without the sighting of any of the numerous groups of islands located near the route of the ships, but there were no lofty volcanic mountains, such as the Hawaiian islands have, near Magellan's course, only the low coral islands of Micronesia, the Marshall, Gilbert and Carolines, etc.

Whilst crossing the Pacific, the crews and officers of Magellan's ships suffered from starvation, disease, dysentery and scurvy, which daily took their toll of deaths; since leaving Spain more than 130 of the crews of the ships had perished, and the remainder were in a precarious state of health.

Antonio Pigâfetta, the historian of the voyage has written a graphic account of it. He relates the dreadful hardships which were encountered, and the heroic conduct of the officers and crews of the fleet.

Some of the officers, prostrated by disease and starvation, besought Magellan to turn the ships back to Spain, otherwise all would die-his reply was, "Be of good cheer and wait a few days more, we still have food, we can eat the ropes, the rigging and the sails."

Pigâfetta writes, "For 98 days, after leaving the south American coast, we sailed day after day over a vast unknown ocean, receiving no comfort from God or man; our plight was desperate in the extreme, we were starving, disease, and death was rampant amongst our officers and men, dysentery, fevers, and the deadly scurvy were our constant companions; our drinking water was foul and ill-smelling, rotting fish and biscuits, which were no longer biscuits, being flour dust mixed with maggots, and the urine of rats and mice, were our daily fare; ox hides thrown overboard, and dragged along-side of the ship for days, softened them, and when cooked and eaten with sawdust, afforded a meal which lessened the pangs of our hunger; a lean rat was eagerly bought for one gold ducat-the coin of a Duke-worth about 9 English shillings.

Leaving the Ladrone islands and steering a westerly course, Magellan sighted the island of Samar, one of the eastern islands of the Philippine group, on March 16, 1521.

On April 27, 1521, whilst aiding a friendly chief on Mactan island, off the eastern coast of Cebu island, Magellan, fighting in a rear guard action, was ambushed and slain by the natives of Mactan. He was 41 years of age. To Magellan belongs the honor of being the "First Circumnavigator"; in the year 1509, he and 

Admiral Sequeira of the Portuguese navy, after sailing through the strait of Malacca, arrived at Longitude 130° East in the sea of Banda-on his western voyage from the Ladrones to Samar, Magellan, crossed the same degree of Longitude 130°, and passed six degrees over, to Longitude 124° at Mactan island.

The Second Circumnavigator: John Sebastian del Cano (15401595) born in Guetaria, Guipuzcoa, one of the Basque provinces.

He was one of Magellan's Lieutenants (Teniente), and commander of the caravel Victoria, the only remaining seaworthy vessel of Magellan's fleet in Manila harbor. 

Del Cano, sailed from the Philippines homeward bound for

Spain, on December 21, 1521, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and arrived at Cadiz, Spain, on September 6, 1522; thus completing the circle of the world in 2 years, 11 months, and 16 days -Del Cano was the first circumnavigator of our globe in one continuous voyage. Out of the original complement of 270 men of Magellan's fleet, only 31 persons returned on the Victoria, but four survivors from the Trinidad appeared later, a total of 35 men returned to Spain.

The Third Circumnavigator: Sir Francis Drake (1540-1595), born in the village of Crowndale, near Tavistock, Devonshire, England. In 1572, accompanied by 18 men and 30 Indians, Drake crossed on foot the isthmus of Panama, saw the Pacific ocean, and fervently asked of God, "To grant him his desire, and permit him to sail in this ocean." 

On December 3, 1577, Drake sailed from Plymouth, England, on a voyage to the Pacific, with a fleet of 5 small vessels, to attack the Spanish colonies on the west coast of the American continent, Chile, Peru, Panama, and Mexico. In his flagship the "Pelican", later renamed the Golden "Hind", and with a crew of only 90 men, Drake attacked and captured many Spanish galeons in the ports of Lima, Callao, Panama, and Acapulco, and collected an immense booty. He sailed north as far as the 48th parallel, the adjacent American coast he named New Albion, known today as the States of Washington and Oregon; leaving the American waters, Drake sailed across the Pacific to the Spice islands, arriving there on November 4, 1579, reached Java, March 11, 1580, arrived home at Plymouth, on September 26, 1580; a period of 2 years, 9 months, and 23 days had elapsed since the date of his leaving this same port. Drake died in the West Indies on

The Spanish marine authorities especially cautioned the commanders of their vessels to prevent the English and Dutch obtaining any details of their discoveries. Portugal and Spain, and in part Italy, worked together under the covenants of the treaty of Tordesillas; the discoveries by each one were known to the other. 

The Dutch commanders, Houtmann, Van Noort, George Speil bergen, and others; the English sea robbers, Drake, Cavendish in the 16th century and Anson in the 18th captured and plundered more than 40 Spanish vessels, both large and small in the Pacific alone. DUPLICATE NAMES 

In the Western Pacific the name Los Monjes is applied to three rocky islets of the Northern Ladrones or Marianas; it is never used as a group name; nearby islands of the Ladrones are 

La Vezina (the cousin or neighbor); La Desgraciada, the Misfortune; Las Urracas, the Magpies; and Los Pajaros, the Birdsall of the foregoing names have been applied to other islands of the seas; however the name Los Monjes (the Monks) has been given as a group name of Hawaii, and also as one of the islands of the Islas de Mesa group, another name for the Hawaiian islands. The marvellous transformation of the Los Monjes of the

The claim of Dahlgren, that the Los Monjes islets of the northern Ladrones are the same as the Los Monjes islands, placed by the Spanish cartographers with Longitude 142 degrees West of Greenwich passing through the center of the group; is tantamount to stating, the Farallones, rocky islets some thirty miles west of San Francisco, are part of the Hawaiian Islands. Elisee Reclus, French historian states: Captain Samuel Wallis, of the English navy, in the year 1766 and in 1767, at Tahiti, was the first navigator, and not Cook, to determine Longitudes in the south Pacific by means of Lunar observations; and thereafter errors made by the early Spanish navigators of 1,000 miles or more, were eliminated. Mariners no longer beat about on the ocean for weeks, and even months, in search of Islands already reported and discovered by their predecessors. The Augustinian 

Friar, Andres de Urdaneta, determined the Eastern route across the Pacific from Asia to America, by reasoning that the Atlantic and Pacific had the same wind and weather above 35 degrees North, or about the limit of the Trade wind belt; this belief and guess proved correct, for in the year 1565 the Eastern voyage of the galeons from Manila, on the parallel of 35 degrees to the 

California coast was made in 125 days.

This new course, and the Western route from Mexico to the Philippines was carefully surveyed by the Spanish pilots, and the galeons followed them for 200 years; rarely diverging therefrom, except in storms, fogs and contrary winds; so closely was this beaten track adhered to, that few discoveries were made to the north or south of it; nevertheless, the indications of Islands are figured on early Spanish charts in the region occupied by the 

Hawaiian Islands. 

The Hawaiian islands are in the center of the belt of the northeast trade winds; hence the Spanish galeons must have passed in close proximity to them on their eastern course from Manila to the Mexican coast, for more than thirty years, 1530 to 1565; on this route the galeons, caravels and corvettes encountered strong head winds and rough battering seas, which caused the ships to leak, become unseaworthy, and many foundered; the duration of the voyage from Asia to the Mexican coast took from six months to even a whole year.

The captains of the galeons accepted Urdaneta's proposal to set their course eastward above the trade wind belt, and finding this route feasible, effective and speedier, the middle trade wind course near the Hawaiian islands was immediately abandoned. 

The presence or absence of volcanic activity in Haleakala, Hualalai, Mauna Loa and Kilauea, would be an important aid

ADMIRAL GEORGE ANSON, R.N. 

George Anson (1697-1762), elevated to the English Peerage, then known as Lord Anson, was born in Shugborough, Staffordshire, England, (the shire of pottery manufacture and Bass' Pale 

Ale, 6%) on April 23, 1697.

In 1737 Anson was appointed commander of the 60 guns frigate the Centurion; he later became a circumnavigator. In 1740 he sailed from Plymouth, England, in the Centurion, now his flagship, and five other vessels, on a voyage to the Pacific, to attack and harass the colonies of Spain, in Chile, Peru, Panama, and 

Mexico; Anson was not a freebooter or pirate, England and

Spain being then engaged in war. 

The complement of Anson's fleet was 961 men and officers; 

Cape Horn was rounded with only three ships, the two other vessels failed to pass the Cape, they also had become unseaworthy, and were driven back by continuous storms, they later returned to Plymouth in safety. Another ship the "Wager" was wrecked off the Chilean coast, reducing the fleet to three ships, two of these were leaking badly, their sails had been blown away, and with the crews so reduced by scurvy and deaths, that in June 

1741, only 335 able bodied men remained; so for safety the two leaking and unsafe ships were abandoned and burnt, their crews being collected on the Centurion.

Anson sailed north along the west coast of South America, attacked and defeated the Spaniards, and sunk their ships in the ports of Chile, Peru, Panama and Mexico, collecting immense booty; calmly and deliberately he plotted and surveyed the Mexican harbors, the Spaniards fearing to attack him; finally Anson sailed across the Pacific to the Ladrone islands, made careful surveys of this group; then hawk-like awaited the coming of the rich 

Spanish galeons bound to Manila from Mexico, which usually arrived in the month of June. (Galeon, a large Spanish ship with lofty stem and stern; English spelling is Galleon; a great galley, a long low built ship, anciently propelled by slaves in

Anson, lay in wait for weeks, off the island of Samar, P. I., near Cape Espiritu Santo (Holy Ghost), and in the early afternoon of June 20, 1743, a long expected galeon loomed up on the eastern horizon, it proved to be of large tonnage, and named 

"La Nuestra Senora de Covadonga", Our Lady of Covadonga. 

For months the officers of the Centurion had daily drilled the crew in musketry practice, and heavy cannon; the capture of the galeon, with 550* men, an armed merchantman only, was easily accomplished by the Centurion's crew, 50% of whom were under 25 years of age, which fact much chagrined the mature sailors of the galeon after their surrender, to a lot of boys, as they called the English crew.

In this naval battle, the captain of the Covadonga, Jeronymo Monteiro, a Portuguese, was severely wounded, 57 of the crew were killed, and 84 wounded, before the Spaniards surrendered. 

The British lost 2 men killed, a lieutenant and 16 men wounded. The captured galeon yielded an immense booty, valuable secrets of Spanish naval history, numbers of charts, also the famous so called Islas de Mesa chart, on which Anson and his officers saw for the first time this group of islands, which Cook and his officers proved to be 10 Spanish equatorial degrees** too far to the Eastward of their true position, and re-named them the Sandwich islands. Who is responsible for the alterations and erasures in the original log and description of the voyage of Admiral Ruy Lopez de Villalobo in the year 1542, with John Diego Gaetano acting as Pilot?

It can be safely stated that even today the Spanish archives lack systematic classification, hence what documents are on file or missing is not known to the officials in charge, search for the map of the survey of the route from Manila to Guam, which took two years to complete, was missing for 200 years; it was listed as filed in the Madrid archives-it was found hidden in Manila;

The Moluccas were discovered by Captain Antonio d'Abreu, an able Portuguese navigator, in 1511, he also discovered New 

Guinea the same year.

After Magellan's arrival at Manila, in 1521, two of his ships arrived at the Moluccas late in November of the same year, piloted by Arab pilots.

Spain claimed these islands under the terms of the treaty of

Tordesillas, made between Portugal and Spain in 1494; later, in the year 1528 the Portuguese government purchased the Spice islands from Spain for the sum of 350,000 gold ducats-a ducat —the coin of a Duke, value in United States coinage $1.80, total sum paid to Spain, $630,000.

The Dutch East India Company was incorporated in 1602, and by force and skilful diplomacy, the Portuguese were eliminated from the Spice islands, and by the year 1675 the Dutch had become the masters of the whole Moluccas group, and their ascendency continued until 1749, then declined.

THE SPANISH GALEONS 

The Spanish galeons plied the Pacific between Mexico and 

Manila from 1527 to 1811-a period of 284 years; in the 17th century the galeons were known as the Royal Naos (ships) of Manila, most of them were built at Cavite; those built in the middle of the 16th century were small, about 100 tons burden with crews of about 50 men, increasing in size each decade to 300, 600, and in the year 1589 to 700 tons-in the year 1614, to 1000 tons; in 1746 the "El Rosario" had a tonnage of 1700-the

great galeon "Sanctissima Trinidad" captured by Admiral Cornish in 1762, had a displacement of 2000 tons, and carried a crew and passengers of 800 persons.

The personnel of the galeons varied, generally it comprised a captain, or captain-general, three pilots or navigating lieutenants, surgeon, chaplain, purser or contador, mates, coast-pilot or piloto practico, boatswain, carpenters, divers, veedors or inspectors of stores, maestros d'agua fresca in charge of fresh water. 

The daily position of the galeon from the port of departure was given in latitude only, and in marine leagues, and was determined by measuring the altitude of the sun at midday-this reckoning gave fairly accurate results, the first mention of the chronometer for calculating the longitude of the galeons was in the year 1663.

THE ALLEGED STOLEN CUTTER 

Cutter: A ship's boat built for speed with sharp bows that cut the water, provided with one mast, mainsail and jib. 

At 5:30 o'clock in the morning of Sunday, February 14, 1779,

the cutter of the Discovery was missing from the buoy astern of the ship, to which it had been made fast the previous evening. 

Captain Cook was immediately notified, the news angered him. 

He immediately ordered Lieutenant Molesworth Philips to assemble nine marines and accompany him ashore, armed with muskets and hangers (hanger, that which hangs; a short broad sword, curved near the point); on arriving at the landing, Cook at the head of his marine escort, marched into the village of Kaawaloa, seeking King Kalaniopuu, then over seventy years of age. 

Most assuredly 7 o'clock in the morning is an unusual hour to visit, even a Hawaiian king, with a military escort of heavily armed marines; Cook invited the king to accompany him on board the Resolution, the king was willing, but his high chiefs and

At the time of Cook's arrival, the Hawaiian people were not a savage, rude race, without any refinement; undoubtedly they had a higher grade of civilization, than other colonies of the Polynesian race. WHAT DID THE PACKAGE OF HIAPO CONTAIN? 

(1) Both hands of Cook, entire. (2) The skull, no fracture, the scalp separated, the face bones missing. (3) The scalp, with the hair upon it cut short, and the ears adhering. (4) The bones of the arms, with the skin of the forearms hanging to them. (5) 

The thigh and leg bones joined together, but without the feet; the ligaments of the joints entire. On Sunday, February 21,

1779, Hiapo delivered the remaining bones of Captain Cook's body, also the barrels of his shotgun, and shoes. 

In the Sunday afternoon of the same day, Cook's remains were buried in the bay of Kealakekua, but inasmuch as the Hawaiians never buried their dead in the sea, this incident was looked upon by them as a forerunner of some future disaster falling upon the people, hence the curse of Cook was anxiously awaited. 

To correct a wrong impression: It has been stated, that the 

Hawaiian warriors surrounding the dead body of Cook lying on the rocky shore, passed daggers from one to the other, and stabbed the corpse repeatedly; as a matter of fact, what appeared to the marines in the boats some 150 feet off the beach, to be stabbing motions, was in reality rapid movements cutting off the shining brass or gold buttons of Cook's uniform; his body surrounded by an excited and rapidly moving crowd of warriors presented an imperfect view to the marines. 

Cook always visited ashore in full dress uniform, was neatly attired, took pride in his appearance, when he sought to find King Kalaniopuu; the double-breasted frock coat worn by Cook was adorned with 12 gold buttons on each side, 32 buttons in all with 8 on the cuffs.

Cruelty to the dead was never an appendage practised by Hawaiians; to their minds the possession of a button from the clothing of a great personage like Lono and famous warrior, was an Aumakua or talisman of great value and power, guarding its owner or bearer from evil spirits and death.

CANNIBALISM: Cannibal, an eater of human flesh; a

corruption of the word caribales; in English, Caribbees the native name of the West India islanders, who ate human flesh; also perhaps derived from the Latin, canis a dog, expressive of their character.

At the date of Cook's arrival the excellent teeth of the Hawaiians was so noticeable, that it received marked comment from the officers of the Resolution and Discovery, even today the teeth of the Hawaiians can be distinguished from those of other races, by certain perculiarities. 

One of the chief indications of the eater of human flesh is one who files or has filed his front incisor teeth to sharp points; there is no record in ancient history of the Hawaiians ever sharpening their teeth with files.

On a previous page it has been stated, Captain Cook thought the Hawaiians were eaters of human flesh; his reasons for so thinking were, when a native visited the Resolution with a small package, he was asked what it contained, he became confused and agitated; its contents were a small piece of human flesh about two inches in length, to be used for fish-bait. Cook then asked the native if his people were eaters of human flesh and ate each other-a look of fear and horror appeared on the man's face; he answered, "We eat those of our enemies slain in battle." (Not understanding the exact meaning) the native meant, "We immolate the prisoners captured in battle to our war-god." The practise of Kamehameha I was to placate his war god Kukailimoku by the sacrifice of prisoners.

Later, Cook wandering around on foot at Waimea, Kauai, entered a Heiau, accompanied by one of its priests; saw blood on the surface of a large stone, and along side, covered with kapa lay the recently killed body of a man-Cook spoke immediately to Captain King-"Why, these people are cannibals", whereas the body of the man was to be used as a sacrificial offering to the god or gods of the Heiau or Temple.

Captain King distinctly states on pages 132-4 of Volume III, Cook's Third Voyage to the Pacific, that the Natives of Hawaii were not cannibals.

The practice of the Kalmucks, Tartars and the Caribs was to

eat the flesh of the bodies of their enemies killed in battle, symbolic of intense hatred, not on account of hunger.

The Resolution lay at anchor in Kealakekua bay, January 18, 1779. An aged priest named Koa came on board and saluted 

Captain Cook with great veneration and respect, mingled with reverence and awe; he placed a red kapa cloak over Cook's shoulders, then recited a long prayer, and offered a pig. 

Next, the priest accompanied Cook ashore and on landing, all the people immediately lay flat on the ground face downwardthe priest then conducted Cook to the Sacred Heiau or Temple of Lono (a Hawaiian idol god of inferior status) (Ku was the idol king god of the Hawaiian pantheon) north of the village by the sea, Napoopoo, he was then led inside the Heiau into the innermost precincts of the holy of holies, and halted in front of the sacred idols-there and then Cook was invested with a red kapa gown; lengthy prayers were addressed to him by two High priests, after which his face and hands were anointed with a sacred face cream, made from chewed coconut meat; Cook was then offered steamed fish, imu baked pork, and awa chewed into a liquid of human spittle, as a drink. 

All the aforementioned ceremonial rites were to initiate and install Captain Cook as the INCARNATION of the Hawaiian god Lono.

About the year 1785, the three volumes of Cook's Third Voyage, were placed before the public; at first all seemed satisfactory, the record of events as written were accepted; gradually the story of the secret censorship of the British Admiralty came to light, and why it should be necessary; why all the crew had been subject to search, and their private notes and papers taken from them and destroyed; public distrust became rampant, the pendulum of censure swung towards Cook and the Earl of Sandwich, the latter was then the most detested and unpopular man in England and Cook had been under his protection, his protege; popular dislike and disgust was vented on Cook's conduct in the heiau at

Captain James Cook (1728-1779) was born on October 27, 1728, in Marton, a village in the North Riding of Yorkshire, England, near the river Tees, whose dales (dal, a valley, Teutonic) were noted for its tall men, descendants of raiding Vikings-the 

Dales, Dugdales, Goodales, Lauderdales, Tisdales, etc.. James

Cook was tall, stood 6 feet, 234 inches; he had dark brown hair, Kaawaloa-The Scene of Cook's Death, and His Monument and very dark gray flashing eyes, the Scotch blood of the Stuarts was in his veins. He inherited his love for adventure and the sea from his Viking ancestors. He was killed in his 51st yearhis relics are or were on view in the Chelsea Hospital Museum, London, S. W. 

Salient incidents in Cook's life occurred on the Sabbath; his day of fate. Sunday, January 18, 1778, he arrived in Hawaiian

waters his first visit. Sunday, January 17, 1779, his ships anchored in Kealakekua bay, second visit. Sunday, February 14, 1779, about 8 a. m., he was slain at Kaawaloa, whilst rashly attempting to kidnap King Kalaniopuu, whose body-guards, Chief Kalaemano struck Cook on the head with a war-club, and Chief Nuaa stabbed him by a sideways thrust in the back with a dagger ; 

Cook fought off his assailants with the butt of his gun, but they finally subdued him by forcibly submerging his head until his breathing ceased. Surgeon Samwell, of the Resolution, found no fracture of Cook's skull nor broken bones of his limbs. 

Cook was a master swordsman, and had he had his sword as a weapon, and not a double-barreled shot gun, he might have cut down his attackers and reached the beach in safety; both Lieutenant Molesworth Philipps and John Ledyard, the American marine, cut down all the Hawaiian warriors who opposed them. By sheer weight of numbers of men they were forced off the rocky shore into the sea; both Philipps and Ledyard were skilled swordsmen.

NO MEMORIAL TO COOK; HIS OFFICERS UNREWARDED 

The British Admiralty is mainly responsible, or was, for the neglect and lack of appreciation by the English people of the valuable work of Cook, and his officers in the Pacific Ocean. 

The mask of deceit which the government censors applied to the actions and conduct of Cook and the crews of his ships, was at first not perceptible to the thinking public of England; but little by little the stories related by the garrulous sailors and petty officers became known, and placed a different construction on the alleged facts which had lead up to the death of Cook, and thereby convincing the public at large of the deceit which had been practised on them by the Admiralty. 

Why did not Cook receive more generous treatment during his lifetime from the government and the nation? In part, this question is solved-Cook, still a Lieutenant at the age of 40 years; his First voyage to the Pacific covered a period, from August 25, 1768, to July 12, 1771. He was promoted to be Commander this same year-As Commander Cook, his Second voyage began

on July 13, 1772, and ended on July 29, 1775. On August 9, 1775, Cook was advanced to the rank of Captain. The fatal Third and last voyage of the now Captain Cook, began on July 12, 1776. 

Without strong influence at the sessions of the Lords of the British Admiralty, promotion in former times was exasperatingly slow. Cook encountered jealousy in the navy, efficiency versus influence, the latter always won promotion first. He was a plebeian, not even a graduate from the British Annapolis, and the universally detested, First Lord of the Admiralty, the Earl of 

Sandwich was his (Cook's) patron. The navy, like the navies of all countries was permeated with cliques, even Sir Hugh Palliser, Cook's true and staunch ally, was also disliked by many influential admirals and other officers holding high commands-even Cook's tragic end did not alter the hostility towards him, held during his lifetime, hence no posthumous promotion to admiral or monument.

THE MAHIOLE: FEATHER HELMET 

As a head-dress the feather cap, and the feather cape, were worn by kings and high chiefs of Hawaii prior to the time of King Umi's reign, about the years 1480-1520 A. D.; the elevated crest in the center of the Hawaiian helmet as seen today, and the feather cloak, were without a doubt acquired and copied from the steel helmets of the Spaniards of the 16th century, worn by high ranking officers of the Spanish army and navy; the head gear worn by the Spanish soldiers and marines was a flat uncrested helmet made of iron or Cordovan leather. 

The Wallis islanders, the Tongans, Tahitians, and the Maories, wore feather caps, but after the Spaniards discovered Tahiti in the year 1606, probably the crested feather helmet came to be worn by the kings and high chiefs of those islands; the brilliant red coloring of the feathers in the Tongan capes and head-dress surpass the Hawaiian.

ONE OF CAPTAIN COOK'S LAMBS 

The Mutiny of the Bounty, is a well known story of the sea, the number of its readers rivals those of the romantic narratives of Sinbad the Sailor, and Robinson Crusoe.

The cause of the mutiny was the harsh and brutal treatment inflicted upon the crew, by the commander of the ship Lieutenant Bligh, who had served as a midshipman with Cook on his third and last voyage to the Pacific; after finishing his studies in sea-

manship and navigation, Bligh graduated into a hard-boiled commander, and was sarcastically named and known as "One of Cook's Lambs." 

His Majesty's ship Bounty sailed from Portsmouth, England, on the 28th of November, 1787, on a voyage to Tahiti, seeking young breadfruit shoots, which the British Government was desirous of introducing into the West Indies, in order to augment the food supply of the plantation laborers. 

In order to gain the goodwill and friendship of the Indians (Tahitians) the Bounty carried merchandise, suitable for gifts, such as nails, beads, knives, mirrors, ironware and cotton goods, etc. Emphasis was placed on the orders given to Bligh-To treat the Tahitians with kindness and consideration, to avoid giving any provocation or friction which might lead to a quarrel, such as led to the killing of Captain Cook in the Sandwich Islands. 

After the Bounty had sailed from Tahiti homeward bound, under the lead of one, Christian, a member of the crew, the plot of the mutineers which had been in the hatching, came to maturity. 

Lieutenant Bligh, all his officers, other members of the crew who had refused to join in the mutiny, a total of 18 persons, were captured without bloodshed, and forcibly driven into one of the ship's boats, and cut adrift from the ship. 

Bligh set sail to the west, and with the aid of a compass alone, arrived at the island of Timor in Malaysia, after a voyage of more than 3,600 miles, without the loss of a man; a truly remarkable marine accomplishment.

MARINE JOURNAL, HISTORY AND GEOGRAPHY BARCELONA, SPAIN, A. D. 1710 

A Narrative, from the Journal of Commander Ricardo Cal-

deron, of a voyage between Callao, Peru, and Acapulco, Mexico, in 1626-1627; Vice-Admiral Pedro Alvares de Pilger, Commandant of the Port of Callao. (Speilbergen, page 70, Burney, II, page 340).

ISLAS DE MESA 

We sailed from Callao, Peru, Latitude 12° South, on October 

25, 1626, bound for Acapulco, New Spain, on the Santa Maria, with a crew of 68 officers and men. The Caravel Carmen accompanied us. We encountered head winds and heavy seas on our course NW by North; the Carmen sprung a leak, listing heavily to port, and had to put back; we went on alone, with a damaged rudder which hindered our tacking to starboard (estribor). Day after day the wind would shift from NE to E, hence we were blown west.

On the 60th day out, December 24, we crossed the line (Ecuador), our meridian of Longitude was about 130°, but of this we were uncertain. On the 75th day we had reached the parallel of Acapulco, 16° North, this day was January 8, 1627, but due to a westerly current and lack of wind we made little progress N-Easterly. 

On the 16th day of the month of January, 1627, the officer of the midnight watch, Mingo d'Apella, sounded the alarm, "All hands on deck." We saw a most magnificent sight, the sky was aflame, the night was like midday, it was a great Volcan burning in the sky, high up above the clouds, distant some seven leagues on the starboard beam. The wind had fallen to calm, the air was filling with surphur smoke, fine dust covered the deck, and at midday the darkness became like the night, intense heat, suffocating fumes, and terrible explosions followed rapidly in succession.

Where could we be, we feared, we prayed to God, the deck was covered with kneeling men, we drifted slowly north, no land was visible thru the dense smoke, now we could see no fire. Our fear continued all night, as the noise from the Volcan increased, near sundown next day a fresh cooling wind from the north sprung up, in answer to our prayers, and we sailed rapidly due south out of danger; but the next morning the wind changed to

the South West (Sud-Oeste), and aided by the current we were again carried towards the island; but it was now on our port bow, and open water was visible to starboard, for this we steeredwe were now on the East (Este) side of the island. At noon the wind fell to calm, the current carried us inshore rapidly, the ship being about ½ a league from the land, with a low promontory (promontorio) on our port (Babor) beam extending south for about a league; we sounded, found no bottom at 80 fathoms (brazas).

When we came closer to the shore, the land appeared as an inferno of heaped up black rocks, a desert-a desolate barren place-devoid of vegetation or verdure, saving a few coconut trees, growing here and there amongst the rocks, near the shore. We now observed groups of Indians, moving about amongst the rocks; at four bells in the afternoon watch, the ship had drifted to less than 4 mile (500 Spanish yards) from the land; we now dropped our spare anchor, it found no bottom at 50 fathoms, but served to slow down the drift of the ship ashore. 

We lowered our boats, hoping, if the Indians were friendly, to fill up our water casks, and also obtain a supply of fruits, vegetables, yams or potatoes. We took with us as interpreter a sailor, a native of the eastern Sequeira Islands, known today as the Caroline Islands (1710). Landing was difficult owing to the surf, we finally found a sheltered cove; near the shore on a level patch of ground, we saw some 50 or more Indians, armed with long and short spears; they drew away from the beach as we approached, making no chatter or noise, like the other aborigines we had formerly met on other islands in the Pacific; these 

Indians seated themselves on the rocks, remaining quiet, but carefully observing us. They were naked, save for their loin cloths, had no footwear. We laid down our guns and swords, then 

Lieutenant Garcia, our tallest man, walked towards the Indians with one of our marines bearing some nails, biscuits, and three small knives, a chisel, two broken swords, and a metal cup; the marine placed all these articles on the ground, then returned to us. Our Lieutenant held his hands aloft, making movements of welcome and invitation, to come for our presents; but these Indians remained aloof, dignified and silent-on their standing up,

we could not but gaze at their lofty stature, splendid physique and graceful deportment.

Hawaii is an ancient name, Cook changed it to Sandwich, later the good old name Hawaii came into use and it has come to stay forever.

We all returned to the ship, expecting to come again next day to have further intercourse with these interesting people, but at late evening, the wind began to blow fresh from the north, which rapidly carried us away from the land, so we hove up our dragging anchor, sailing and drifting all night. With favoring wind we set a course for New Spain; held a service, thanking God for his mercies, and our deliverance from the dread perils of the volcan, and the warlike and formidable Indians, whom we much feared had gone inland, only to return with re-enforcements. SPANISH NAMES OF THE HAWAIIAN ISLANDS 

The ancient Spanish navigators almost invariably gave apt names to their discoveries in the 16th century in the Pacific ocean. The earliest or first navigator Saavedra, named the 

Hawaiian group the Archipelago de Volcans, not naming each island; another navigator used the name Los Volcans, and La 

Farfana; next comes the name, Los Monjes, joined with La 

Vezina (the neighbor or cousin), and La Desgraciada (the unfortunate).

Now comes Gaetano or some other navigator, who names the islands the Islas de Mesa, which includes the Los Monjes, La Roca Partida, Olloa, and La Desgraciada. Mendana is credited with his name only applied to the group. 

A resident in Hawaii, be he a native or foreigner, is better fitted for analyzing the Spanish names given to the various islands (especially, if he or she has a knowledge of the language of 

Castile) than others who have no such qualification. 

1. Hawaii, the largest island of the group, is the La Mesa ; or La Mira, the Wonder, an interjectional noun, the former name means, the Table. At sea level, from a distance of fifty miles or less off Cape Kumukahi, the most easterly point of the island of Hawaii, when the clouds blanket to their bases the great mountains, Mauna Loa, Mauna Kea, Hualalei and the

Kohala range, the great mass of land extending from Upolu point or cape, the northwest cape of the island, to Cape Ka Lae its most southern, appears and gives the impression to the eye of being flat or plane-like, gradually sloping from the seashore upwards to the cloud hidden mountains, where appears the end of the land.

On the afternoon of May 2, 1883, the appearance of the island presented to the officers and crew of the S. S. Abergeldie, then steaming 75 miles northeast of Cape Kumukahi, was a large flat mass of land, utterly unlike the island of Hawaii as shown in the north Pacific sailing instructions. The steamer was stopped, soundings showed no bottom at 500 fathoms; as no member of the ship's company had ever been in Hawaiian waters, consternation reigned supreme. The haze over the convex ocean added to the deception. The island was the La Mesa of the Spanish navi-

gators, or Hawaii as it is known today; and may have been seen under the very same atmospheric conditions, at the time of its discovery by the early Spanish navigators-hence they gave it the name of La Mesa, the table or flat plane-like island. 

2. Maui, is probably the Los Monjes of the Spanish navigators, (El monje, the monk; Los Monjes, the monks or friars; 

La Monja, the nun; Las Monjas, the nuns) the crater of Haleakala, a huge mass, towers some 10,000 feet into the sky, its crest is continuously covered with white fleecy clouds, like the shaven poll of a monk. To the westward some 30 miles distant, is the Eka mountain 6,000 feet, its top crested with white clouds, like Haleakala; hence these two cloud-capped mountains of Maui, resembling the shaven polls of two monks, account for the name, Los Monjes. 

3. La Desgraciada, the unfortunate, is probably the island of 

Oahu; on some of the Spanish charts it represents the island of 

Maui; why the name unfortunate was given to the island is not revealed in any of the logs of the early Spanish navigators to our knowledge; conjecturally it may be an occurrence such as loss of a ship, man overboard, sails carried away. On the 

Mexican chart annexed to this booklet, the name La Desgraciada is placed on the island between Roca Partida and Olloa, which indicates Oahu and Desgraciada are the same. (Early Spanish charts name the Ladrone islets, the Recifes Ladrones-the Reefs of the Ladrones; of the eleven islets of the

The extreme northerly islets include the Los Monjes, small rocky peaks of 500 feet elevation; great prominence was given to them because they were the sought landfall from Manila -a guide to the galleons bound on the eastern route to Mexico.)

4. La Roca Partida, the split or broken rocky island; Molokai probably, with its miles of lofty fault scarps; another island of the same name belongs to the Revilla group.

JOHN PAUL JONES 

An American naval officer, alleged to have been born in the year 1747, in Scotland; has had more than six biographers, yet each one of them differs in the more important details of his life. Why the said Paul saw fit to add to his name the surname, Jones, a typical Welsh name, has not been convincingly explained, and they all so admit.

The British naval authorities scathingly denounced Jones as a pirate, traitor, deserter and renegade. In his 26th year, then living in the State of Virginia, 1773, he joined the U. S. Navy as John Paul Jones, perhaps to hide his identity as a deserter from the British Navy-he was appointed as a First Lieutenant to the thirty gun frigate Alfred.

With the rank of Commodore, in command of the Bonhomme 

Richard on September 23, 1779, Paul Jones defeated and captured the Serapis, a large British vessel, superior in guns and men. 

He occupies a foremost position amongst American naval heroes; he later resigned from the American navy, and entered the naval service of France, but soon became dissatisfied with his rank, pay, and treatment by his brother officers he then sought service with Russia, but jealousy and indifference from the Russian officials caused him to return to Paris, France. He died there on July 18, 1792, aged 45 years, a victim of kidney and heart disease, lovingly nursed by his sweetheart Aimée de Telison.

Paul Jones, when questioned in both France and Russia about his nationality, stated "I am first, last and forever an American citizen, and as such I shall die; the English denounce me as a pirate and deserter-I owe them no allegiance."

The impression made by Paul Jones on his fellow officers was

that he was irritable, quarrelsome, over-jealous of his reputation, boastful, and querulous-in appearance, he was coarse featured, a typical Keltic face, of slim build, round shouldered, some 5 feet, 7 inches tall-yet withal he was pleasant mannered and attractive to strangers.

On March 31, 1905, the body of Paul Jones was exhumed from its location in Paris, then brought to the United States of America, and laid to rest in Annapolis, in one of the Naval Academy buildings, one hundred and thirteen years after his death; at this date the kinks in the tail of the British lion had loosened, the scream of the American eagle had ceased, its feathers also were unruffled, and the bones of the fighting Welshman, by name, finally rested in peace.

JEBEL-AL-TARIQ: GIBRALTAR 

Gibraltar is named after the Arab chief, Tariq-ibn-Zaid, and is a corruption of Jebel-al-Tariq; Jebel means "mount" in the 

Arabic language, hence Mount Tariq. This Arab chief built a fortress on the promontory in the year 711 A. D., soon after the 

Moors or Arabs had invaded and conquered Spain. 

The promontory is situated at the extreme southern end of Spain, and on the eastern or Mediterranean side stands the rock of Gibraltar, and the strait of the same name which separates Spain from Africa; the promontory is composed of primary grey marble, its length is 234 miles, breadth 3/4 mile, elevation 1439 feet, the area of Gibraltar is 17% miles, and it and Abyla, in 

Morocco, near Ceuta, are the classical "Pillars of Hercules", crowned with silver columns by the Phoenician mariners. 

SIEGES OF GIBRALTAR 

First: Gibraltar was captured from Spain on July 24, 1704, by a combined British and Dutch force, under the command of

Sir George Rooke, and the Prince of Hess-Darmstadt in the service of Archduke Charles of Austria. It was then named, and is well named "The Key of the Mediterranean". By the treaty of Utrecht in 1713, Gibraltar was ceded to Britain. 

Second: Beseiged by the Spaniards and French in 1704-5, without success.

Third: Again attacked by the Spaniards in 1726, also failed. 

Fourth: The attack was begun by the Spaniards and French in the year 1779; this siege lasted 3 years, 7 months and 12 days, and also failed. The seige began on June 21, 1779; the strength of the besieged force under General Elliot was 5,382 men; twice the fortress was almost reduced to starvation, but relief was given the garrison by Admirals Rodney and Derby.

In 1782, the Duke of Crillon took command of naval and land forces of Spain and France. Additional batteries on the land side, and ten enormous floating batteries were built by the Chevalier d'Arcon; covered boats to disembark 40,000 men were also prepared. The attack began on September 8, 1782, and continued to the 13th. The floating batteries, covered with layers of raw ox hides, were set on fire and destroyed by the British gunners using red hot cannon balls, and the beseigers were repulsed with a loss of 2,000 men killed and 3,500 wounded; the British casualties were 16 killed and 68 wounded.

John Bull has held on to Gibraltar for 231 years, and his doing so is a thorn in the side of Spain; and having the most powerful navy in Europe, he will keep it. From present day appearances the millennium of peace is far off, the work of the World Court and the League of Nations is of small value.

SYPHILIS RAVAGES EUROPE 

The sailors of the ships of Columbus on their return from the West Indies carried with them to the city of Barcelona a new disease, which spread rapidly to the inhabitants, especially amongst those living in the slums and brothels bordering on the water front. The chief victims were soldiers and sailors who had indulged in the worship of Venus, hence the new disease was given the name of the venereal or sexual plague. 

In the early part of the 16th century, Charles the VIII, king of (1494) France, declared war on Ferdinand of Naples, Italy, and in the army of this latter monarch, there was a large number of Spaniards, soldiers, with their accompanying wives and sweethearts, who were aiding Ferdinand to defend Naples, which was besieged by the French army. In order to lessen the drain on the food supply of the beleaguered garrison, the Spaniards drove out their women companions, who then sought refuge amongst the French invaders. Within a brief period, the "sexual plague" -Syphilis, became very prevalent amongst the French soldiers and their officers, the disease seriously impeded the military effectiveness of the army; hence the French besiegers were compelled to raise the siege of Naples, and began a retreat through Italy, spreading the "sexual plague" far and wide amongst the inhabitants of the country villages and towns, where it assumed epidemic proportions.

A DREADFUL AFFLICTION 

The victims of this newly imported disease presented a deplorable sight-their faces, limbs and bodies were covered with foul copper colored sores, varying in size from a half inch

diameter to three inches. Treatment was of little aid-hence the infected ones were herded together and shunned by their comrades, and these victims in their misery prayed for relief or death. Other diseased persons had sores which were hard, like the bark of trees, but only on certain parts of their bodies, the forehead, neck, chest, and buttocks; so painful, itching and burning were these indurated sores, that the afflicted ones tore themselves with their nails. Other victims had numerous vesicles and pustules—and many had thick warty growths like plugs or horns on the face, ears, nose and scalp, which burst with a foul odor. 

THE TOCSIN OF FEAR 

Syphilis continued to spread all over Europe from the precincts of the hovel to the throne, and great alarm filled the minds of all those in contact with the disease. Eight thousand Swiss soldiers who enlisted under the banner of the French king Charles the VIII, were so severely scourged by syhilis, that only 148 survived to return to the Swiss capital, Berne, but its gates were closed and strictly guarded to prevent the entrance of this infected residue.

As the disease kept spreading and increasing from year to year and lost none of its virulency, its cause became a subject of general discussion. "The stars were blamed, because Jupiter had been attacked by the evil-doing planets Mars and Saturn, and conquered; also the battle between these stars had begun in the bounds of the constellation Scorpio-which extend from October 24 to November 22—whose sphere of influence includes the genital organs."

Various nations were denounced as being responsible for infecting others. The French soldiers who were allegedly infected from the Italians named it the "Mal de Naples"-the Italians however, returned the accusation and named the disease "Malum 

Francorum" the Portuguese named it the Spanish disease "Mal de Castilla"-the Spaniards named it "Mal Gallico", the Gallic or French disease; the Poles called it the "German disease"; the

Russians named it the "Polish disease"; the Persians knew it as the "Turkish disease".

About the year 1530, Fracastoro wrote a poem in Latin, which was published in Venice, under the heading-Syphilis, sive Morbus Gallicus. The word Syphilis, derived from two Greek words, means, "The Companion of Love." Investigators have claimed, that 

Syphilis was known to the Romans, Greeks, Egyptians, Persians, Babylonians and the Indians.

By the end of the 16th century, the disease had been carried to Africa, Malay States, China and Japan. At this epoch, due to the lack of sexual morality, syphilis spread rapidly; the daughters of Eve, known to be spreaders of the disease, were branded on the forehead; the public baths and markets were notorious for their unsanitary conditions; the common bath tub with its unchanged and polluted water, caused the disease to become almost epidemic, as at Brunn. History records men, women, young boys and girls, remained for hours together in the baths, eating, drinking during periods when bands played music.

In the 16th century, mercury was the remedy used for treating leprosy, hence as syphilis in some respects resembled the former disease, mercurials became a remedy for syphilis; but its unskilful administration caused intense salivation, caries and necrosis of bones; hence the alleged remedy and cure-all produced symptoms worse than the disease. As much as three and four pints of saliva was expectorated daily by the unfortunate victims of syphilis, until welcome death ended their sufferings ! 

A TAME VOLCANO-KILAUEA-MOLTEN LAVA LAKE DESTRUCTIVE FORCES OF NATURE 

Earthquakes, Tidal Waves, Volcanic Eruptions 

The Lisbon Earthquake: With no premonitory disturbance, about 7 o'clock in the morning of November 1, 1755, an earth-

quake of greater severity than had ever before been experiencd, rocked the city of Lisbon and the surrounding country, reducing almost instantly, the greater part of the city to a mass of ruins; fires speedily broke out and consumed the fallen debris and those buildings which had withstood the shock of the quake, and completed the work of ruin. 

It took ten years to remove the debris from the streets and fires smoldered in the ruins for months; the victims of the quake were crushed to death by the fallen houses and stores, and those who were mortally injured and unable to escape were cremated. The ruins of the fallen buildings filled the streets thirty feet high, hence rescue was impossible; for weeks the earth continued to quake, accompanied by terrifying rumblings and thunder-like claps. 

In 1755, Lisbon was a populous city; when the shock of the quake was felt and the nearby buildings crashed, the panic-stricken people rushed pell-mell for safety to the river bank of the Tagus, and crowded the quays and jetties; almost immediately a great tidal wave 40 feet high swept over the occupants, men, women and children. The recoil of the wave carried the assembled crowds into deep water, also a large section of the river bank broke away, augmenting the speed and height of the turbulent water forming a deep valley; the quakes had opened great fissures in the bed of the river causing whirlpools on the surface of the water, and into their vortex the masses of struggling people were drawn and sucked down into the depths of the whirlpoolsdisappearing forever.

The Lisbon disaster was one of the greatest tragedies of modern times, and the most dreadful of all the recorded earthquakes; the exact loss of life is not known, but a conservative estimate places it near 38,000 souls.

Portugal when in trouble has always appealed to Britain, and it did so in 1640, 1755, 1807, etc., hence on a call for aid in the 

1755 quake, prompt response was made, a committee of engineers and geologists were dispatched to Lisbon. 

A partial report on the earthquake by Daniel Sharpe, geologist, follows: 

1. No building on the Blue clay escaped destruction.

2. None or any on the Tertiary deposit escaped serious injury. 

3. All buildings on the Hippurite limestone and basalt were not damaged. The line where the earthquake ceased to be destructive, corresponded exactly, with the boundary of the Tertiary deposits.

The shock of the earthquake was felt from northern Scotland to southeast Asia; yet in the year 1927, in the month of January the quake demon returned to Portugal; strong shocks terrified the people compelling them to abandon their homes and camp in the fields and streets of the cities.

Asia: The center of the world's volcanic activity is located in the islands surrounding the sea of Java-the great island of Sumatra with a length of 1,100 miles, and 250 miles in width, has 66 volcanoes, some active, many dormant; and the island of Java has almost as many.

In the Pacific ocean, the placement on its surface of many of its groups of islands, of volcanic origin, is from northwest to southeast- -some have a trend west to east-a few north and south. The trend of the Hawaiian Islands on the ocean's surface is from northwest to southeast, and the lava ejected from its volcanoes assumes two forms cindery and liquid molten metallic. 

The former is named by the Hawaiians a'a (ah ah), meaning burnt, broken, rough earth-hence cinders; the latter form, the molten lava, cools as a leaden colored smooth mass or magmais named pa-hoe-hoe (pa, solid or hard; hoe, patted or beaten).

KRAKATUA or KRAKATAU or KRAKATOA 

Krakatau is or was a small 9 miles area islet, in the 14 miles wide Strait of Sunda, which separates Java from Sumatra; its volcanic cone had a base circumference of one mile, and an elevation of 2623 feet; tourists and local visitors from Batavia, the chief city of Java, made weekly trips to view Krakatau. The volcano, with ever present fire, was supposed to be dormant, and no loss of life had ever occurred amongst the thousands of visitors who came to see the crater.

About the hour of 4:30 o'clock on Sunday morning, August 

26, 1883, the volcano of Krakatau hitherto active, but insignificant, suddenly burst into explosive activity which speedily alarmed the whole world. The whole of the volcanic peak was blown away as well as two-thirds of the sloping portion of the island; the ocean floor sunk to 1000 feet. There was no visible sun, dense darkness prevailed at Batavia, 100 miles distant, lamps had to be used at mid-day; terrific explosions occurred at short intervals until

August 28th; a tidal wave 120 feet high submerged the villages on each side of the Strait of Sunda, drowning some 36,000 people, only one man survived, the lighthouse keeper; the huge wave swept over the lantern.

The ashes and other ejected material from Krakatau covered and destroyed the surrounding forests for 50 miles distant by 3 p. m.; the activity of the volcano continued until the 28th of 

August; it was estimated the ejected ashes reached a height of 17 miles, they became diffused all around the world, and became visible in the Hawaiian atmosphere on October 4, 1883, 40 days after its ejection from Krakatau. The illumination of the volcanic dust, high amongst the clouds, by the setting sun, produced the uncanny effect known as the "After Glow."

The sounds from the incessant explosion in the crater of Krakatau resembled the firing of heavy guns, and were audible at 

Rodriguez Island 3000 miles distant, at Bankok 1413 miles, Philippines 1450 miles, and at Ceylon 2058 miles.

Tidal waves of two kinds swept over the ocean; first, long waves at periods of an hour; second, shorter but higher waves with briefer intervals. The mareograph at Cape Horn, 7,818 miles distant, registered the first long wave, 10 hours after the first explosion. 

Dreaded Volcanoes: Gunong Api (always active) is a solitary cone in the Banda sea, elevation 16,200 feet. 

Papandajan: located in West Java, elevation 8,744 feet. It is named the Forge or Foundry, on account of the incessant hammering, clattering noises which stun the ear; it has also ejected enormous masses of blue hot mud for miles destroying vegetation, men, women and children, houses, crops, and as a side issue deadly gases (carbon monoxide) killing humans, birds and beasts.
