For example, the Mother Cathedral of Etchmiadzin - built in the form of a giant cross (cruciform) surrounded by walls in a square area, with five cupolas, one in the center and four on each arm of the cross is still in active use as the seat of the Catholicos of all Armenians. The basic structure of the church of St. Hripsime has reached our days almost intact. The church of Tekor, a pagan temple before it was remodeled and a dome was added. has likewise retained its original plan.
Many such examples could be cited if an attempt were made to list all of the pagan temples converted into Christian churches during those early historic days.
Between the 3rd and the 5th centuries A.D., Armenia enjoyed a comparatively peaceful and constructive period. In addition to architectural achievements, the invention of the Armenian alphabet also enabled the people to create for themselves a most enviable literature and culture..
This period, however, was not completely uninterrupted.
Byzantium, and especially Persia, which resented the presence of a Christian state adjacent to her dominions, took full advantage of all opportunities to attack Armenia and to ruin much that Armenians had built, in their struggle over mastery of Armenia.
Yet, in spite of these obstructions the Armenian genius continued to flourish.
Toward the end of the 5th and the beginning of the 6th centuries, architecture in Armenia reached even newer heights when Catholicos Hovanness directed the construction of the Church of Avan. The plan for this church was not entirely new in concept, but since it had been almost forgotten during the passing centuries, it constituted a turning point.
The actual renaissance of Armenian archite ture, though, took place in the 7th century, when the threat of the Persians having subsided considerably, the Armenians found themselves able to give full expression to their talents. Even the invasion of the Arabs, who went as far as to raze to the ground the city of Tvin, did not deter this progress. Oddly enough, it seemed as if the Armenians were able to find a way of life with the Arabs while they had not been able to do so with either the Byzantines or the Persians.
In 618, Catholicos Yezr directed the rebuilding of the magnificent church of St. Hripsime. In 640 Catholicos Nerses sponsored the erection of the church of Zvartnozt, a magnificent masterpiece of church architecture in all of Christondom.
By the end of the 9th century, rab pressure subsided.: The Armenians now had the field all to themselves. Under the wise leadership of the Bagratunis of Ani and the Ardzrounis of Van, culture made unprecedented strides Architecture which had flourished even when the country was overrun by the invaders now, in the 9th and 10th centuries, reached an all time high. In the Bagratuni capital of Ani alone hundreds of churches were erected one more beautiful than the other until in time the city became to be known as "the city of the 1001 churches." Numerous churches, palaces and other public edifices were also erected in Van.
Unfortunately, this era lasted but 300 years. Ani, besides being an architectural masterpiece, was also a great cultural and commercial center, rightfully deserving of the title of "the jewel of the Middle Ages." Surrounded by avaricious peoples, it could not long hope to remain untouched. And in eight subsequent sieges aimed at its possession, it was left in ruins.
It would be impossible, of course, to give a full accounting of all the churches, bath houses, palaces, bridges, and high walls of which Ani boasted. In the following pages a sketchy mention will be given only of the more important edifices.
Yet it should not be forgotten that Ani, though but the "Queen of Ruins" today, was once the foremost example of Armenian creativeness.
In addition to Ani, the constructive activities of the Bagratunis also spread as far and as wide as possible — in the greater part of the Araratian province, and a large sec- - tion of Greater Armenia, such as Lori, Kars, Ardahan, Karabagh and Nakhitchevan.
In the 10th century two very famous monastery-churches were built in the province of Lori. These, the monasteries of Sanahin and Haghpat, soon became centers of high culture and remained as such for three centuries. The same may also be said about a number of other monasteries where the torch of high national culture was kept ablaze as late as in the 14th century.
Following the downfall of the Bagratuni Dynasty, the architectural progress of the Armenians and their efforts to further embellish their fatherland came to an end. Yet, large numbers of Armenians now dispersed in many corners of Europe and Asia Minor, continued their efforts on behalf of their national arts.
Even today, this effort is being continued. Armenians faithful to their religion and its high principles, are true to their heritage. They are not only actively building in their so-called fatherland, which now groans under the most barbaric form of oppression, but also in every corner of the world where they are ever waiting for the day when Armenia will be free once again.
THE MOTHER CATHEDRAL OF ETCHMIADZIN The Mother Cathedral of Etchmiadzin has attracted the attention of the entire civilized world since its erection many centuries ago, because of its great age, the influence it has had on Armenian architecture in general, and as well as for having been the preferred seat of the Catholicos of all Armenians.
The Cathedral is situated in old Vagharshapat about 10 miles from Erivan.
There are many popular legends regarding the site of the Cathedral, some of which go as far back as Noah's Ark.
The most important of these legends has come down to us from the pen of the famous 4th century chronicler Agatangelos. We are assured by him, that St. Krikor Loossavoritch had a vision one night that Jesus Christs descended from heaven and bade St. Krikor to erect on the spot of the visitation His Mother Church, calling it Etchmiadzin.
As mentioned before, the basic structure of the Cathedral is a giant cross surrounded by walls in a square area.
It is approximately 180 ft. long, 168 ft. wide and 125 ft.
high.
But it should not be imagined that this ancient cathedral, as it is today, is exactly as it was when St. Krikor built. Over the many years many changes have been made. In fact, today, it is in no respect the outward pattern of the original building.
During the Armeno-Persian wars in 452 A.D., the use of Etchmiadzin as the sea or the Catholicos was interrupted temporarily.
It was put back into use again around 618 when Catholicos Gomidas renovated the Cathedral and replaced its wooden cupola with one of stone.
In spite of all the changes Etchmiadzin has undergone, it would be correct to assume that it represents, as one of the oldest churches of the Christian world, the model for most of all churches built later on on the basic structure of a cross.
Now, it is interesting in this connection to study the name Etchmiadzin which has been given to the place where Gregory is said to have seen Christ.
Etchmiadzin is composed of the contracted Armenian word "Etchnel" (to descend) and the full "Miadzin," which means "the Only Begotten." The word Etchmiadzin then, literally translated, is: "The place of the Descent of the Only Begotten." Following the construction of the Mother Cathedral by St. Gregory, the name of the community in which it is located was changed from "Vagharshabat," to "Etchmiadzin." It is to be noted too that King Tiridates the Great, King of Armenia, gave of his treasures freely to construct the Mother Cathedral.

THE MOTHER CATHEDRAL OF ETCHMIADZIN THE FAMED CHURCH AT TEKOR The village of Tekor, or Tikor, is located on the Akhourian River, hard by the once great Armenian city of Ani, and at the foot of an imposing chain of mountains. At Tekor is found the ruins of an ancient Armenian Christian church which is considered one of the most shining examples of the great Armenian art of building. The church, taking its name from the village in which it is located, is known to many scholars as the "Church of Tekor." The Church of Tekor is especially famed because its central chapel is known to have been the first Christian church surmounted by a cupola in history. This important fact has brought to light through the researches of the great Armenian architect Thoramanian, and Prof. Nicholas Marr.
The origins of the great Tekor Church are absolutely unknown. The remains carry no inscriptions. But scholars Thoramanian and Alishan, after examining the ruins and doing intensive excavations, suggested with a good deal of logic that the Church was originally a heathen place of worship. It is believed that the Church was remodelled after the plan of THE TEKOR CHURCH the Cathedral at Etchmiadzin in the Fifth Century through the orders of the Catholicos Sahag Kamsarakan.
Only added research at the site of the old church will solve the riddle it represents.

THE FAMED CHURCH OF ST. HRIPSIME One of the most famous edifices in Armenia is named for St. Hripsime, the famed virgin who was martyred during the early period of Armneian Christianity. This church is located THE CHURCH OF ST. HRIPSIME (The Southwest Side) about 1000 meters away from the Mother Cathedral at Etchmiadzin.
One of the amazing things about St. Hripsime's is that the basic structure of the church seen today is almost as it was when built under the sponsorship of St. Gregory the Illuminator in the closing years of the Third century. It was renovated by St. Sahak in 396, and received its final form under Catholicos Komitas, in 618. A magnificent bell tower, topped by a characteristic cupola, was added in the 17th century.

Despite its basically narrow size, St. Hripsime's gives an illusion of grandeur, and is an impressive structure. Lynch, the English traveler, said that of all the churches he had seen anywhere, St. Hripsime's had impressed him the most.
THE ANCIENT CHURCH OF ST. KAYANE As it may be known, most of the old churches of Armenia were named after early Armenian saints. This tradition has been followed throughout the course of Christian Armenia. Today, many of our churches here in America bear titles memoralizing the life and sacrifices of our early Christian Fathers and other Christian martyrs and figures.
The Church of St. Kayane was named after the virgin maiden Kayane. It is found in that section of Armenia known as "Airarat," which is known world wide as "Ararat." This beautiful edifice stands to the East of the Etchmiadzin motherchurch. The early Armenian historian Agatangelos relates that the original edifice was built on the spot where Kayane herself was martyred by the Armenian heathens who resented ner Christian preachments in the country.
St. Kayane's was built in the latter part of the Third century, again under the orders of St. Gregory the Illuminator.
When in 630, Catholicos Yezr of the Armenians observed the crumbling state of the old edifice, he ordered the edifice pulled down lock, stock and barrel, and reconstructed from the ground up. It is said that the new church was even more beautiful and stately than the original model.
Various improvements and additions were executed on the church up to Eighteenth century.
THE EXQUISITE ARMENIAN CHURCH AT ZVARTNOTZ One of the most interesting and original works of Armenian architectural art is the circular church at Zvartnotz, a community standing about 4 kilometers from Etchmiadzin, where the Armenian Mother Cathedral is located.

The Zvartnotz Church was first erected about 640 A. D.
Scholars say that its lines show the influence of Arab, Persian and Byzantine art on Armenia, but there is nevertheless much at Zvartnotz that is purely Armenian in inspiration.
Responsible for its erection was the famed Nerses Catholicos the Constructor. Its architect was a talented man by the name of Hovhan.
Unfortunately, only ruins of this once great church stand today. Accurate reproductions of how the proud church once looked were done by the architect Thoramanian. The outside of the church was exquisitely done with the circular facades modulated by beautiful arches pierced by small slot-like windows. The Church was in three tiers, the topmost of which was a magnificent characteristic Armenian cupola. A row of circular windows served to let in light and at the same time add to the beauty of the edifice. Thoramanian exclaims that the circular form of the building and its characteristics breathe of the creation of Armenians, and is an exceptional piece of work.
Alas that such a beautiful church is today in ruins!
THE EXQUISITELY BUILT CHURCH ON ISLAND OF AKHTAMAR One of the most wonderfully built specimens of the art of the early Armenian builder rests on the famed island of Akhtamar, which is found in Lake Van. This is perhaps the most exquisitely decorated of Armenian churches.
The Church, called Holy Cross Church was built in 915-921, under the orders and patronage of pious King Kakik, a scion of the monarchial Armenian royal family of the Bagratids who, at that time, ruled that general area of Armenia though surrounded by a sea of Arabs. The architect is known to have been an artisan named Manuel.
Holy Cross of Akhtamar is ornamented beautifully on the facades with lacey-like vines and figures of men and beasts. Bethe CHURCH OF AGHTAMAR (The Northwest Side) cause Armenian Church buildings were usually austere, that is they were not decorated very much by the builders, Akhtamar, with its glorious ornaments cut into the stone, enjoys a unique place in the story of Armenian architecture.
Many scholars have asked why King Kakik built such a noble church out on an island separated many miles from the mainland. Many answers have been advanced, the most sensible of which are the following. The Church, being on an island, would be more or less protected from surprise assault by enemy forces by the waters of Lake Van, which then would act like a moat, and the island, a castle. Another suggestion: Kakik wanted a place where he and the Armenian monks could retire from the tumults of the world and engage in pious meditations. At any rate, whatever the reasons for building Holy Cross on Akhtamar, the fact remains that the Church played an important role in Armenian ecclesiastic history after its construction. At one time, it is said, it served as the center of all Armenian Christian activity when Etchmiadzin was suppressed by invaders. At another time, a reigning bishop of Akhtamar declared his "independence" of Etchmiadzin and set up a "Catholicosate" of his own which continued for some time.
The noble Church of the Holy Cross on Akhtamar is so wonderfully located on the island that when one leaves the mainland by boat to travel to Akhtamar, the very first glimpse of the historic island is the sight of the magnificent cupola of Holy Cross' standing forth like a beacon.
THE MOTHER CHURCH IN THE CITY OF ANI-ST. ASTVADZADZIN'S If Etchmiadzin is the Cathedral City of the Armenians, Ani was the City of Churches. History relates, and archaeological remains prove, that this once great city, the capital of Armenia, and the commercial and cultural center of the Middle and Near East, harbored more than a thousand "churches." Now of, course, one must see in the word "churches" something else than the vision of the usual large and flourishing edifices we associate with that word today. Actually, a goodly number of the "churches" at old Ani were more accurately chapels, small places of worship into which the wayfarer could enter and pay his devotions to the Maker.
- - - One would ask why so many places of worship in one city? The answer is found in the old Armenian documents.
Those accounts say that wealthy Armenians nobles, merchants, etc., were accustomed to underwrite the erection of "churches" as a sort of physical manifestation of their piety, and in the wish that the Maker might consider them worthy of entrance to heaven when once death came. Many of the churches at Ani were built under the sponsorship of such people.
Perhaps the greatest real church at Ani was the famed Mother Cathedral of St. Astvadzadzin (the word Astvadzadzin is used to designate the Mother of God.) This church was built in the Tenth century during the most flourishing period of the Armenian Bagratid (Pakradouni) dynasty of kings.
Responsible for its construction was King Kakig, the same monarch, who, we will recall, built Holy Cross, at Akhtamar.
The plan of the church was generally rectangular at base.
High walls with attached columns on the facade were surmounted by sloping roofs, while a truly magnificent Armenian cupola stood majestically over the entire mass. The windows are mere slits in the wall, while the portals are narrow and low. The whole structure is one of the marvels of the Armenian art of architecture. One of the portals was used exclusively by the King and his family.
So perfect and substantial was the Church that through the years it is said not a single alteration was made. This wonderful structure is now in partial ruins.
Scholars say that St. Astvadzadzin's is a perfect example of Armenian influence on Roman-Gothic architecture.

ABOUT THE OLD CHURCH AT SANAHIN The revered church of Sanahin is found hard by that village which bears the same name. It is found today along the railroad that connects Tiflis, Georgia, with Erivan, the capital of Soviet Armenia.
Sanahin is really a Monastery, though its architecture is characteristically of the Armenian church type. It is divided into nine component parts.
One of the sections is a library, while there are three distinct churches or chapels - St. Krikor's, St. Asdvadzadin, and St. Amenaprgitch. Each of these churches has its own distinct vestibule chambers. The other two areas are the "Magistros Jemaran" and the beifry structure.
Sanahin was constructed under the auspices of King Ashod Bagratuni in 957. For many years it served as a center for Armenian learning and retention of Armenian culture, and is therefore justly famed.
The really astonishing thing about this monastery or church is that it is the result of many years of additions and remodeling attempts, yet it accords a feeling of general harmony that deludes one into believing that it was thrown up originally as it now stands. It would seem that all the princes through the ages who patronized Sanahin operated after a master plan calculated to preserve the essential oneness and harmony of structure.

Strangely enough, however, scholars are in agreement that each addition to the original Sanahin represents a picture of the state of Armenian architectural learning at the moment of the addition. The entire progressive category of Armenian building art is represented in Sanahin. There are windows there with pointed lintels, characteristic horse-shoe arches, attached false columns, and even bas-reliefs, each of which was perfected by Armenian architects as the years advanced.
A noted French scientist, after viewing Sanahin, wrote: "I like Armenian architecture most of all because it is inherently a logical art of building. It is the work of masters. It is the work of a cultured and intelligent people." THE CHURCH OF ST. GREGORY THE ILLUMINATOR AT ANI The church of St. Gregory is found in the eastern precincts of Ani, hard by the Akhourian River. Much smaller than the Mother Church at Ani, St. Gregory's is more accurately a chapel.
St. Gregory has but one portal for entering and leaving, that located on the western side of the structure. According to testimony of the inscriptions found on the pile, St. Gregory's was built either in the Twelfth or Thirteenth centuries, A.D. Without much doubt, it was one of the last religious edifices thrown up in the "city of churches," as Ani was known.
Because of that, at least at the time of the first World War, St.
Gregory's was in much better condition than the semi-ruined churches elsewhere in Ani.
St. Gregory's is distinguished by the beautiful bas-reliefs found externally and internally on its walls. Generally speaking these bas-reliefs depict phases in the life of Christ.
The Frenchman Texier said of these bas-reliefs: "Interestingly enough, these bas-reliefs are not the work of one man.
They are not even the work of men living in the same area.
This is proved by the character of the sculptures themselves.

Where some panels are coarsely and inartistically done, others are exeted masterfully. Some of the panels are so exquisite that they could very well have ornamented any church in Europe." CONCLUSION It is difficult to say which nation has contributed what in the knowledge of architecture. However, the most learned authorities in the field of architecture recognize the great contribution of the Armenians, who have given a great deal to the three great architectural styles: the Romanesque, the Gothic, and the Moorish. Perhaps the first "Romanesque" church in the world was St. Hripsime's. Many of the characteristics of Romanesque architecture were found in Armenia long before they were used in Europe. After seeing the sketches of the great Italian architects of the 15th and 16th centuries, and especially those of Leonardo da Vinci, it is impossible to believe that they had not been acquainted with Armenian forms and styles. Indeed, the greatest cathedral in the West, St.
Peter's at Rome, like the greatest in the East. St. Sophia's in Constantinople, is believed to have originated in the Armenian mind.
The Armenian churches used Gothic arches and other characteristics of the Gothic style before they were developed in Europe. The influence of the Armenians in this respect has been traced to France, Germany and even northern Ireland.
The Arabs, Moors and Spanish also owe much to Armenian architecture. The horseshoe arch so characteristic of their style was first found in Armenia. Beside this influence in Moslem countries, North Africa and Spain, a remote relationship can be traced even to the Spanish colonies of the new world.


THE ARMENIAN CASE AND THE WESTERN POWERS It has been said often: "Our views in regard to the Soviet are, of course, negative. Nevertheless, it seems that the so-called Western Democracy has been idealized a bit too much. There is a tendency to whitewash its shady sides.
"The Soviet will never bring salvation to the world, of course. Yet it will not be denied that the West too is deeply immersed in material gain. There is no hope of the West, especially for the Armenian Case as long as she is committed to the task of bolstering up the Turk.
"How will it all end, is unknown. Democracy seems equally risky in my opinion. What sort of democracy is that which denies the right of others to live or at least is indifferent to it? As to the United Nations, that is only a plaything in the hands of the big nations, as was the case of the League of Nations in its time." If such mentality were not quite prevalent, it would not, of course, be necessary to take the pains of discussing it, unfortunately, however, many others have, and still do express similar thoughts quite frequently. That is the reason why such thoughts should be subjected to a critical examination.
First of all, it is gratifying to note that during the past three to four years there has been a perceptible increase in the number of those who no longer entertain any illusions in regard to the Soviet nor put any trust in her. If during the last World War it was possible to entertain any illusions about the Soviet's potential peaceful reformation, or to cherish hopes of their cooperation in the establishment of world peace and in the solution of peoples' just causes, one would have to be politically blind today to entertain longer such illusions and hopes. Today, for any politically conscious Armenian it is never pardonable to expect any good from a Soviet victory.
It is a signal bit of good fortune that, after many bitter experiments, freedom-loving Armenians together with the world have at last understood that much of the truth.
If hitherto in numerous newspaper articles it has been tried to tear the mask of the Soviet's infernal reality, it has been done so, first of all, because that reality, due to the Iron Curtain, has been impenetrable, inaccessible and hazy to most Armenians of the Dispersion. What is worse, this unknown reality, thanks to the intensive propaganda of the Communists and their fellow travelers, has been completcly transfigured and represented in rosy colors.
On the other hand, if there has been comparatively less insistence on the shady sides of Western Democracy, that is because the Armenians of the Dispersion who live in those democracies are well acquainted with those deficiencies. Moreover, no matter how shocking or revolting these failures may be, they shrivel and become dim when compared to the absolute darkness which prevails within the Iron Curtain.
Plainly, it is not necessary to whitewash the shady aspects of Western Democracy, but these aspects appear white when contrasted with the black reality of the Soviet regime.
The magnitude of Western shortcomings diminishes also when it is compared with the brighter aspects of Western Democracy..
Can, for example, any critic of the West, in the whole history of mankind, show a single country who was capable of extending tens of billions of dollars in aid to those nations who are struggling for their existence and their freedom as has done with such astounding and admirable generosity, that great western Democracy, the United States of America?
This fact becomes all the more striking and worthy of admiration when it is considered that the Soviet Government, which in the opinion of many "is not immersed in materialistic gain," and "does not deny the right of others to live," did not extend one cent for the reconstruction of those countries which had been ravaged by the last war, whether inside or outside the Iron Curtain, did not lift a finger for the suffering multitudes of the world, and did not appropriate one cent for any humanitarian aim, not even excepting the orphaned children concerning whom she had received so many appeals.
The critics of the West completely ignore the fact that today the world is divided into two hostile camps-the Soviet Union and the anti-Soviet world—that the fate of all nations depends upon the outcome of that fight. The nature of this fight is such that it is impossible for any nation, especially for a small and helpless nation like the Armenians, to try to straddle two horses or to play with unrealisitc and precarious notions of neutrality. It is true that even now there are some nations which are playing with the idea of neutrality, but sooner or later these too will be forced to choose one or the other of these camps. At all events, the Armenians cannot rely upon those who play neutral, or follow their example.
The critics say that there is no hope from the West, especially for the Armenian Case. Why? Because the West embarked on the road to strengthen Turkey.
Is it true, however, that there is no hope for Armenians from the West? If this were true, then on whom shall they rely? The critics themselves rejected the Soviet. And since there is no third power on whom Armenians might rely for the solution of their case they would be doomed to absolute despair if they followed their advice and rejected the West as well.
Fortunately the views of the critics are not correct, and that, for the following reasons: First, in the present world situation only a Western victory will enable all the nations, great or small, to insure a free and autonomous life, whereas a Soviet victory will bring them complete and permanent slavery.
Secondly, only through a Western victory can the mass of the Armenian people now residing in Soviet Armenia free itself from the Soviet slavery and have ar oportunity for a free and independent life in its historic fatherland.

Thirdly, the critics forget that: a-The thing called the Armenian Case, under the present circumstances, first of all is a question of a free and independent Armenia; the question of a united Armenia is a remote aim, at least for the time being.
b-The creation of a free and independent Armenia is possible only in the instance of a Soviet defeat and a Western victory.
c-That the creation of a united Armenia is possible only when the Armenians already possess a free and independent state.
d-That it is futile to expect of the Soviet even the creation of a united Armenia when we know that her plenipotentiary in the United Nations promised Kars and Ardahan, historic Armenian territories, to the Georgians, and not to the Armenians.
e-That the bolstering up of the Turk by the West is not an aim but only a means in the life and death struggle waged by the West against the Soviet.
f-That Turkey on the side of the Allies can be restrained more easily and can be rendered less dangerous for the Armenians than if she were the Soviet's ally.
g-And finally, only after smashing the Soviet menace will the way be paved for the solution of the Armenian Case. Once Armenia becomes free and independent, the Free West, America in particular, will feel more free to bear down on the Turk to do justice to the Armenians in regard to their historic claims.
It is highly curious that critics not only disparage reliance on the West, but even consider the idea dangerous. Why? Because, as they affirm "the West has embarked on a policy of bolstering up the Turk." However, the strengthening of Turkey, as stated, is not an aim either for the West or the United States in particular, but is only a means of containing the Soviet menace to the world, if not to remove that menace. The moment the Soviet menace is removed, neither the West nor the United States shall have anything to gain by bolstering up or aggrandizing the Turk.
It also should be borne in mind that, once the Soviet menace is removed, Turkey, a comparatively small power, shall be in need of outside aid and will not be in a position to play a decisive role in the general solution of post-war problems.
Finally, it must not be forgotten that after the overthrow of the Soviet dictatorship, the world shall never be able to live without international organization, something like the present United Nations but far more powerful, and the principal aim of that organization shall be to preserve the world peace and peacefully to solve, or assist in the solution of international conflicts. It cannot be imagined that an organization of this sort will be indifferent to the Armeno-Turkish territorial dispute, especially after the creation of a free and independent Armenian state.
If critics have certain valid grounds for their displeasure of the United Nations, they have no right on the other hand to say that it is a play thing in the hands of the big powers. The defense of Iran in 1946-the expulsion of Soviet forces from Azerbaijan - the solution of the Israeli question in 1948, and the armed intervention in behalf of Korea in 1950 are living proof that the United Nations is not a plaything in the hands of big powers, and that, to a certain extent, it is capable of defending the rights of small and weak nations.
It is true that that organization still is hackled by the endless Soviet vetoes and her sabotage. It is true that, in certain respects, it still is a faulty and weak organization. Yet it is capable of being converted into a powerful organization to preserve the world peace once the Soviet obstructionism is permanently removed and a series of important revisions are introduced in its constitution and by-laws.
The West no doubt is nct perfect, it has many faults and many shady aspects, but it also has a clear consciousness of these shortcomings and is constantly and quite successfully trying to remove them by peaceful means. As a matter of fact, the very nature of Western Democracy is conducive to peaceful evolution in all matters, be they social, economic, political or spiritual, whereas, on the other hand, the Soviet tyranny is incapable of such peaceful evolution. To be able to preserve its existence it has steadily become a greater menace to mankind.
Gone are the days when freedom-loving Armenians could prosecute their Case independently of other nations' cases.
Today a condition has been brought about in the world when all the nations great or small, free or unfree, are intricately bound together in their struggle for existance and the preservation of their freedom. Today, before anything else and more than anything else, there is one general cause, that the whole world might be free, and that each nation, great or small, might be free to determine its way of life as an equal member of the human family, the alternative being enslavement by the Soviet, as is the case with many helpless nations including the mass of the Armenian people who live under the Soviet regime.
The first of these conditions can be created only when the Soviet tyranny is overthrown and Western Democracy emerges triumphant from the present world struggle. The second condition can ensue only after a Soviet victory.
Theoretically, there is one more condition which may prevail and that is the division of the world into two hostile camps the Soviet, and the anti-Soviet worlds, as is the case just now, provided they can live side by side temporarily. But such a modus vivendi cannot, and never shall be a peaceful coexistence, the nature of the Soviet regime will never tolerate such a condition. A permanent peace can be established only by the victory of one or the other of these two antagonistic camps.
To say, therefore, that to rely on the West not only is useless but it is dangerous for the Armenians means failure to comprehend the true meaning of what is going on in the world at present. For if today the Armenians or other nations of kindered fate shall ever hope to be free and independent someday and to recover their historic territories, they can expect the realization of such hopes only after a decisive victory of Western Democracy. Only in a free world, and in the company of mankind which has been emancipated from the Soviet nightmare, can the Armenians ever hope to realize their supreme political aim of a free united and independent homeland.

And though decorative uses of light and dark stones in stripes were common in the earliest days, to be followed later on by mural paitings and even portraits.
Large size paintings did not become common until very late, and for centuries Armenian artists were forced to devote their talents to the painting of religious illustrations and marginal paintings, called illuminations.
The lot of the modern Armenian painter is doubtless a difficult one. He cannot follow a specific school of painting which bears the Armenian trademark; he must express his talent and attempt competition on an international level.
Yet, amazing as it may seem, though confronted with so many obstacles, in the past two hundred years, Armenian artists have not only begun to bring their share to the world of painting but have already captured for themselves a prominent niche among those of other nations.
Artists such as Ayvazovsky, Fetvadjian, Katchadourian are not only famous in Armenian life, but are even more respected by the non-Armenian art loving world. Their lives, of which we shall speak in the following pages, have not only made many aware and appreciative of Armenian talent and the creativeness, but have also become a living inspiration to all present Armenian artists, who may, even at this moment, be struggling to transfer their creative drive to a piece of canvass, realizing full well that they can give only their best.
HOVANNES AYVAZOVSKY (1817-1900) Hovannes Ayvazovsky, the world famous Armenian marine painter, who in his life time captured the admiration of art lovers and still continues to demand the respect of connoisseurs the world over, was born in Theodosia, Crimea, on January 7, 1817.
It has been related that the love for art in Ayvazovsky became evident very early in life. In fact, he was yet a mere boy of a few years of age when he would amaze members of his family and neighbors by his highly successful attempts to record nature in her varying moods.

He was only about 12 years old when he began to attend the secondary school of Sinferopol, from whence he was transferred to the Art Academy of St. Petersburg when only 16 years of age. At the Academy of St. Petersburg he studied diligently and became a star pupil of Tanner, he famed French marine painter.
After graduation from the Academy in the late 1830's, Ayvazovsky entered the period of his life which would bring him acclaim after acclaim, and would enable him to reach the highest level in the artistic world of his times.
Even while in school, he exhibited some of his paintings and won prizes, which actually meant a great deal more since thus he received the recognition of the Russian government, the latter supporting his every project thereon.
Soon after his initial success in Russia he embarked on an extensive tour of European countries, lasting nearly ten years, during which time he visited as much of Europe and studied all of its natural beauties as was physically possible.
The success he had been able to achieve in Russia soon followed him to Europe. And when he returned to his birthplace in 1847, he had been conferred the title of "Professor." The next few years he spent putting on canvasses his memories of Europe as well as cultivating his marine painting technique, a branch in which he was destined to excell one day.
In 1875 Ayvazovsky, upon the invitation of Napoleon III and Empress Eugenie of France, travelled to Paris and exhibited his paintings, and was rewarded by receiving the Order of the Legion of Honor. This was followed by similar invitations and rewards by other monarchs of Europe, and aslo from the Sultan of Turkey, whose reception of Ayvazovsky in Constantinople was a memorable event.
His grateful compatriots and patrons of art from many lands celebrated Ayvazovsky's 50th anniversary as a master of the arts in 1888, in which year the St. Petersburg Academy of Arts also elected him an honorary member and ordered gold medals of honor cut bearing the portrait of Ayvazovsky.
 Ayvazovsky died of old age on April 19, 1900, in his birthplace and was laid to rest in the church of St. Sarkis.
In his life time he produced over 5000 paintings, which today have found their rightful places in all the leading museums of the world or are part of priceless private collections.
He had over 100 exhibitions, the proceeds of which he invariably dedicated to charity.
As to his paintings, beside the hundreds of works immortalizing the Black Sea, a feat which once made Professor Vladisslavlef say that the name of the Black Sea should be changed to "Ivan Ayvazovsky Sea," he has also given many memorable impressions from the tortorous life of his compatriots, their historic glories as well as biblical passages. His foremost canvasses are considered to be "The Creation of the World," "The Deluge," "Armenian Massacres in Trebizond," "Byron Arriving at St. Lazarus," "The Wreck," "Christ on the Sea," "The Sea at Night," and others.
It has been considered bad taste to praise any one's work over-enthusiastically, lest it pass the limitations of logic. However, in Ayvazovsky's case it would be correct to say that no amount of praise can be enough.
He is one of the brightest stars in the Armenian artistic constellation which shall continue to shine forever.

 Fetvadjian's Armenological and archaeological activities began in 1900. In 1919, Fetvadjian was sent to Paris by the Government of the Republic of Armenia, to undertake the preparation of Armenian postage stamps. While in Europe, in answer to many requests, he consented to exhibit part of his architectural studies, creating a great stir in French and English scientific and intellectual circles by introducing to them for the first time the beauties of Armenian art.
In 1922, after the fall of the Armenian Republic, Fetvadjian came to the United States, where he lived and worked until his death on October 7, 1947.
He gave various exhibitions of his works while in the United States and always received great acclaim.

RUINS OF AN ANCIENT PALACE By FETVADJIAN SARKIS KATCHADOURIAN (1889 1947) Sarkis Katchadourian, the painter, was born in Malatia, Turkish Armenia, and died in Paris, March 4, 1947, at the age of 58.
Katchadourian was a genuine artist and a patriot. It may be said that his deep feeling for his people, which was manifested in many of his works, came to full maturity when he, at an early age, entered the Sanassarian Institute in Erzeroum, from which he was graduated in 1905. While at Sanassarian the school trustees noted that he possessed unusual talent in art, so following his graduation they sent him to Italy so that he might develop his genius there. Upon graduation from the Rome Academy of Arts, Katchadourian went on to France where he plunged into further study in painting. Following the completion of his training, in 1909, the young artist returned to Malatia where he served as an art instructor in the local Armenian School for one year, before transfering in the same capacity to Sanassarian.
In France again in 1913 to refresh his studies, Katchadourian was spared from the ensuing Turkish deportations which destroyed over one million of his kinsmen. He later wandered through the metropolises of the civilized world and painted wherever he went-in water color and oil-portraying nature and human life and, still later, the color and opulence of the East.
Sarkis Katchadourian moved to the Caucasus in 1915 and was present, in 1919, in Independent Armenia, to the cultural growth of which state he contributed immensely.
Early in 1920, Katchadourian met his countrymen and colleague Arshak Fetvadjian in London, where the dies of Armenia's postal stamps and currency had been cut after designs rendered by Fetvadjian. Khatchadourian volunteered to take the final products with him to Erivan, but the tragic news of the destruction of the Republic put an end to his mission.

One of the important branches of Armenian culture is Armenian music. Inspite of the insufficient bits of information which have come down to us, we shall try to give a succinct idea of the various phases of its development down to our tim, based upon information which is at our disposal.
THE PRE-CHRISTIAN ERA What little information we possess on this period comes down to us from Greek, Persian and Assyrian sources. Before the birth of Christ Tigranes the Great invited from Greece students, scientists, artists, entertainers, and converted his royal court into a veritable cultural center. His idea was to revive culture in Armenia. He founded the Armenian theater.
The most significant development of his reign, however, was the religious rituals which were observed with great pomp.
Several times in the year, and especially on the first of the Month of Navassart (the Armenian new year) great festivals were held, attended by minstrels, actors, poets and troubadors from all regions of Armenia. Undoubtedly, during these festivals a great role was reserved to music. The festival dedicated to the God Tir, the patron of poetry, was climaxed with huge bonfires around which the people indulged in songs and group dances.
However, this and also the information that the story tellers of the times, who were also composers of songs, used instruments such as, the flute, the lute, the lyre, the tambourine, the harp, the trumpet, the cymbals etc. are all the information now available regarding that period.
THE CHRISTIAN ERA Our first information about this period comes to us from St. Sahak Pharthev concerning whom the Armenian historian Lazar of Pharbe writes: "He was versed in the letters of music (notes) and expatiated on the arts of philosophy." Aristakes Lastivertzi speaks of the existance of musical notes which probably were introduced from Greece since it is in Greece that we first meet those musical symbols.
Unfortunately no written proof has come down to us from this period. The first punctuated gospel (in scroll) dated 887 A. D., is now, in the Lazareff Institute of Moscow, while the first gospel written with the signs of versification (now in Etchmiadzin) was written in 998. The Armenian chroniclers of the fifth century, Koryoun and Agathangelos, speak of spiritual hymns which were sung during the holy mass. Even Lazar Pharbetzi speaks of the hymn entitled "Sourb Astvadz❞ (Holy God), while Sebeos mentions "Park i Bartzouns" (Glory in the Highest). Thereafter, in the 9th century, during the period of the Bagradit Dynasty, cultural life in Armenia received great impetus, largely through the churchmen, in the monasteries and the schools. It was a period of great literary, chronological, religious, scientific, and artistic outpouring.
A new impetus was given to Armenian church music and the theater. Nerses the Graceful being well versed both in poetry and music enriched the treasury of Armenian music with his beautiful sharakans.
Another significant development of this era was the emergence of laymen in literature and music. They soon became a great factor in the promotion of popular music, and though little has come down to us in regard to secular music and the theater, historians tell us that they were very popular although the clergy violently persecuted both.

This lack of information does not surprise us, since, as mentioned above, the clergy were opposed to secular music; and historians, who were mostly churchmen, naturally avoided mention of any kind of music which was not approved by the church.
Speaking of Khachatour Taronatzi, Kirakos Gandzaketzi the 12th century chronicler writes: "He brought notes from the east, gave body to the tunes, spread it in our land, and taught it to many." But he does not say where he brought the notes from. The important thing is that contemporary intellectuals properly evaluated music and worked hard to put it on a solid foundation. These notes could not accurately satisfy all the requirements of composition in point of range, pitch, and the separation of the measures. At best they could approximate the tune. But in the course of time even this little was lost until an historical event forced the Armenians to think about ways and means to put their music on solid foundation.
To save their music from eventual oblivion in 1818 the Greeks of Istanbul held a conference and decided to create a new system of notes. By this means they meant to recapture their church music from the whims of fate and oblivion. This event spurred the Armenians to give a little thought to their music. Baba Hambartzoum Limunjian, a church scribe (17681839), taking advantage of old Armenian notes, invented a new system of composition with Arabic nomenclature. He was familiar with eastern music and highly versed in the Greek system of notes.
Catholicos Gevork IV, the founder of the Gevorkian Jemaran in Etchmiadzin, who was a musician in his own right, was another man who took great pains to save the Armenian music which had been the object of improvisations, according to individual whims and very often in the vein of Turkish sharkies.
From Istanbul he invited N. Tashjian, himself sang all the sharakans, the Mass, and the hymns of the Church Codex, while Tashjian put them to notes. This was the first attempt to compose and to standardize the Armenian music.

Much later, in 1896, Makar Yekmalian, a serious scholar who had studied at the St. Petersburg Conservatory of Music, put the Armenian music to European notes for male trios and quartets, and for the first time the Armenian church music was harmonized in accordance with the demands of the times.
Without doubt, Yekmalian's work was a great labor and triumph. But a basic fault weakened the work. Yekmalian tried to make the harmony as simple as possible, because such was the requirement of the spirit of Arab and Persian music, of which, in his opinion, Armenian music is a part.
Later, the Great Komitas not only refuted this theory, but his composition of the Mass purged the Armenian music of its Arabic and Persian influence which, due to various political factors, had been introduced into Armenian music.
Yekmalian's work is greatly influenced by the form of the Russian church music; but with Komitas the harmony is more THE GREAT KOMITAS VARTABED (1869-1935) genuine and more true to the spirit and the nature of the Armenian songs. In the composition of the songs, in purging the music from foreign influence, and in harmonization, Komitas had in mind most of all the Armenian spirit. Besides the Masses of Yekmalian and Komitas we have another Mass by the present Catholicos Gevorg VI who is a scholarly musician having graduated from the Leipzig Conservatory of Music.
And last year saw the publication of Father Srapion's Mass for chorus and orchestra, a volumnious work which is a new triumph.
In the Middle Ages we have already seen the emergence of secular literature and simultaneous with it a slight mention of popular music. It is inconceivable that in the 19th century, when the Armenian literature and the secular arts had their revival, music would be ignored. The progressive Armenian students who were trained in Europe could not very well fail to notice this important branch of art.
Before Komitas, Kara Murza already had begun to cultivate little songs, to organize mixed quartets and concerts. To him belongs the honor of being the first organizer of choral groups. But the honor of recapturing the Armenian popular peasant songs, of evaluating, purging, harmonizing, and composing these songs, and what is most important of all, of founding a distinct Armenian School of Music, belongs to the immortal Komitas. Every student of a conservatory can easily compose any song. That is not the essential thing. The essential thing is to restore the true pattern of the song to purge the superfluities, and to bring out the essence, qualities which require natural qualification, a strong musical instinct, an analytical mind. This is the important thing in which Komitas has succeeded in transcending, and herein lies the greatness of Komitas. It is this that has given value to Armenian music, and it is through this road that a people's art, coming out of its narrow tribal circle, occupies an honorable position among the international musical circles.
Komitas was also an erudite theorist. He had the capacity of penetrating and expounding the problems of ancient Armenian notes, the characteristic nature of Armenian music, and to give them theoretical form. In this sense we have another learned musician, Spiridon Melikian, who has made valuable research studies of Armenian music which involve theoretical and historic problems.
In collaboration with Anoushavan Ter Ghevondian, Melikian has rendered a great ethnographical service, having collected the popular songs of Shirak, Van, Sassoun, and Abaran.
Simultaneous with the emergence of Armenian lyric poetry we see the development of lyric music. The outstanding representative of this branch is Romanos Melikian who, with his "Zemroukhti Yerger" (Emerald Songs) created the Armenian lyric music. Other successful works in this field have been contributed by Gourgen Alemshah and Ara Parthevian.
The honor of founding Armenian dramatic music belongs to Arshak Choohajian. His "Arshak II" bears the imprint of Italian opera; the libretto is Armenian while the music is Western. Kara Murza made an attempt in this direction with his Opera "Shushan" (The Lilly) concerning which we know very little. But in the national sense we may call Armen Tigranian's "Anoush" an opera, which, even after its perfection in recent times, will not stand the acid test of operatic music, although it is very wel liked by the Armenians because of its popular character. As a piece of art, however, it is weak.
Unfortunately the "Anoush" of Komitas was never completed and the little he accomplished is lost.
We may rightfully consider Spendiarian's "Almaste" (The capture of Tembkaberd) as the most successful specimen of Armenian dramatic music. This work meets all the conditions of the opera. A. Ter Ghevondian has an opera called "Seda" (based on Levon Chanth's "Ancient Gods") which has not been staged to date, and unfortunately, is not printed. We cannot say much about it.
The music of the operetta is still in its formative stages.
In this field the most successful work is Choohajian's Operetta "Leblebiji Hor Hor Agha " A few years ago Haro Stepanian wrote the first Armenian comic opera based on the popular legend of "Nazar The Brave." The music of this opera is very difficult.
To Alexander Spendiarian belongs the honor of founding the Armenian symphonic music in which field he has accomplished much. The greater part of his works bear the general eastern form, but they are preeminently suited for the orchestra, beautifully instrumentalized, and very true to the spirit and the character of eastern music. In this field a shining luminary is Aram Khachatourian, internationally recognized composer. The greatest musician we have ever had, Khachatourian is the logical continuation and the elevation of Komitas. We see in his music the colors of Armenian and eastern music, with a magnificent and original development which has no precedent. Although his first works bear the imprint of Russian composers (Rimsky-Korsakov. Tchaikovsky) and the French impressionists (Ravel, Debussy), but soon he shook off their influence and adopted his own path. Equipped with a comprehensive musical preparation in the course of years he has given us symphonies, choice selections of chamber music (trios and sonatas), and ballets.
While preserving the forms of classic art, he has cultivated and developed the Armenian-eastern themes - rhythm - splendidly, with an original and modern harmonization, preeminently suited for the orchestra, so masterfully, and so beautifully, that today, from America to Europe, and as far as distant Iran, he is one of the most popular and appreciated composers.
In the dispersion, appreciable work has been, and is being done by Alan Hovhaness, Onnig Berberian, Grigor Siuni, Kanachian, young director Ohan Tourian, Parthevian, Alem Shah, Hambartzroum Berberian, Levon Grigorian, and Patmagrian, who have, and are enriching Armenian music with their creations, are trying to compose in higher form, and coming out of the narrow national confines, are trying to produce more valuable works.

In foremost conservatories of the world (Paris, New York, Brussels, Geneva, etc.) you will see such great teachers of music as Tiran Alexanian, the irreplaceable cello instructor, Professor Ivan Kalamian (Paris, America), the famous violin instructor Mischel Tzovianov (Brussels), Sheritjian (Geneva), and many others of international renown.

However, in the dispersion we must admit that there is no organized effort to encourage the Armenian music, or the artists by creating for them the possibility of making a living, to say nothing of creating, or presenting their creations. The Armenian artist is doomed to live in dire circumstances, and he works and creates driven only by his inner urge, with a realization of his duty and his debt, enduring all privations and hardships.

AHARONIAN (1866-1948) If Raffi was the great dreamer and the unlimited source of inspiration for the revolutionary generations to come, then Aharonian was the foremost exponent of the already crystalized revolutionary thought and the truest chronicler of the glorious self-sacrifice of contemporary national heroes.
Born in Iktirmava in 1866, Aharonian, the son of a humble blacksmith, rose high in life but never did he forget to put that life to the full service of his people. Destined by nature to be a writer primarily, many a time he turned a deaf ear to his inner voices, and whenever called upon he became a teacher, journalist, revolutionary worker, strategist, statesman, government representative, and leader of various delegations in pursuit of national recognition and peace.
As if his intense suffering in view of the destruction wrought upon his dreams had not been sufficient, fate played one last joke. Until his death in 1948, the lips of the master were sealed in silence, forcing him to swallow his tears and remain helpless, when he would have assuredly protested with all of his might the injustice which his people suffered.
ANI An ancient and ruined Armenian city, situated in "Turkish" Armenia very close to the border of "Soviet" Armenia, at an altitude of 4300 feet. In 961 it became the capital city of the Bagratuni Kings and thereupon grew in fame as a great cultural, educational and commercial center. It was a populous city, and was known as the "city of the 1001 churches" or "the gem of the Middle Ages" because of the numerous beautiful edifices erected under the auspices of the Bagratuni kings.
Ani was yielded to the emperor of Byzantium in 1046. Eighteen years later it was retaken by the Seljuk Turks, then five times by the Georgians between 1125 and 1209, in 1239 by the Mongols, and its ruin was completed by an earthquake in 1319.
The once majestic city of Ani is still surrounded by a double wall partly in ruins, and amongst the remains are a "patriarchal" church finished in 1010, two other churches, both of the 11th century, and a fourth built in 1215, and a palace of large size, as the sole witnesses of a glorious era long past.

Antranig was born in Shabin Karahissar and though trained for carpentry, he readily forsook his trade when early in life he devoted himself completely to the Armenian emancipatory movement upon the untimely death of his young bride.

ARA (The Beautiful) The model for brave, stalwart and virtuous manhood is embodied in Ara. It is told that Shamiram, the beautiful queen of Assyria sought his hand but Ara, in loyalty to his young and beautiful Armenian queen Nevart, refused. In a war which ensued between Assyria and Armenia as result of his refusal, Ara was slain. Shamiram mourned the loss of her beloved and honored his memory. But fearing the revenge of the Armenina people she had his corps abducted and she gave rise to the rumor that the gods had revived Ara by treating his wounds.
This legend embodies the Armenian conception of the immortality of virtue and the value of eternal struggle for national freedom. The mountains Ara and Shamiram take their names from this legend. Ara is supposed to have lived around 2000 B.C.
ARAM (1879-1919) Perhaps the greatest of organizers in contemporary Armenian revolutionary history, Aram Manoogian, baptismal name Sarkis Hovanissian, was born in Ghapan, Russian Armenia, in 1879.
Aram's revolutionary activities started so early in life that by the time he was still at the tender age of twenty-two, he had become a veteran field worker and organizer in the oil fields of Baku. The organization sensing his extraordinary talents followed his moves with great care and channeled them where most needed. He was in Kantzak, organizing popular selfdefense, during the 1903 confiscatory attempts of the Czar. Next, he was in Kars dispatching arms and fighters to Turkish Armenia. Following this, in 1904 he went to Van, where he remained until 1915, with one brief absence when he attended the ARF World Congress in Vienna. In Van, he taught school, organized the youth, formed self-defense teams, and at the most critical moment, in 1915, he led the heroic stand of Van, until the Russian army and the Armenian volunteer bands came to the rescue of the people. From 1916-1917 he was with the Armenian National Bureau in Tiflis, especially interested in the resettlement of displaced Armenians. In the year following he was sent to Erivan as the plenipotentiary of the Armenian National Council, to lay the ground work for what was later to become the Free Independent Republic of Armenia. During the Republic he served as Minister of the Interior. He died a victim of typhus on January 19, 1919, his work well started but not completed.
ARARA An insignificant hilly formation situated approximately 17 miles northwest of Jerusalem, Arara, or as it is pronounced in Arabic, Aroura, one day in 1918, became the site of a decisive and historic battle, which many justly consider to be one of the important turning points of World War I.
It was at Arara, that on September 19, 1918, Armenian volunteer legionnaires, most of them from the United States under the supreme - command of British General Allenby, bravely attacked the Turko-German lines and broke the enemy's back in the Near East.
It was an unconditional victory. The battlefield was covered with the corpses of the enemy. 75,000 were taken prisoner. Heroic Armenians had avenged in part the atrocities perpetrated against their defenseless mothers, sisters and young brides.
The Armenian losses for the day amounted to 22 soldiers.
ARARAT or MASIS - Mount Ararat, the world famous mountain mass, is the symbol of Armenia, a towering, extinct volcano 16,916 ft. over sea level higher than the highest peak in the United States. Mount Ararat, which is not in Soviet Armenia today incidentally, has actually two peaks. The largest is known as Ararat Major, the smaller, Ararat Minor. The two peaks stand about six miles from each other. Ararat Minor, also known to the Armenians as "Mount Masis," is 12,840 ft. over sea level.
According to the Bible Noah's Ark rested on "the mountains of Ararat.” ARAX The best known river in Armenia is the Araxes (Yeraskh or Arax), which has also become affectionately known to the Armenians as "Mayr Arax" (Mother Araxes). This term is used perhaps because this river flows in almost all its length in Armenia proper irrigating the land around its bed. The Arax or Araxes rises in the Bingeol Mountains about 25 miles from Erzeroum, and flows easterly through lush land, through deep gorges and valleys. Its valley, in ancient times a shallow lake, is very fertile and has often been identified with the Biblical Garden of Eden.
Arax finally joins with the Kura (Cyrus) river, and after taking in various other tributaries, the combined waters empty into the Caspian. The Arax proper is approximately 528 miles long. Its waters are fast and seldom navigable.
ARDASHES (190-159 B. C. ?) The forceful, sagacious and colorful personality of Ardashes has held the Armenian imagination for centuries. He was the first historic king of Greater Armenia and the famous founder of the Artaxiad Dynasty thus affecting the first unification of Armenia. He was a brave soldier, a wise monarch, and a sincere patriot. Through fighting, astute diplomacy, and wise ruling, he was able to extend greatly the boundaries of Armenia. He entertained in his court the famous Carthaginian general, Hannibal, after the latter was banished to Asia by the Romans, and upon his suggestion he built a fortress city, Ardashad, on the Araxes at the foot of Karabagh, and made it his capital. The shapeless ruins of this city are still to be seen near the village of Khorvirab, about nineteen miles to the south of Erivan.
Himself a great lover of Hellenic culture, Ardashes sponsored and spread Greek art throughout his kingdom to such an extent that his reign came to be known as the Golden Age of that period.
Students of history remember Ardashes as the brave warrior king who conquered a nation called the Alans, and then fell in love with the beautiful daughter of the King of Alans, Satenik, around whom Armenian minstrels have woven many a beautiful legend.
About their wedding they say: "A shower of gold fell Over the marriage of Ardashes; Pearls rained On Satenik's wedding." ARMEN GARO (1873-1923) Karekin Pasdermajian, popularly known as Armen Garo, was the scion of the famed Pasdermajian family of Erzeroum. He was educated at his native Sanassarian and at the Nancy School of Agriculture in France. But, instead of practicing his newly acquired science in his homeland, he was easily led into another. life's career as soon as he was made aware of the intense suffering of his people in the hands of the oppressor. The once aristocratic youth changed overnight into a most determined revolutionist.
In 1896 he was one of the heroic handful who attacked the Ottoman Bank in broad daylight. Exiled from Turkey, he next took refuge in Geneva until 1900 when he journeyed to Tiflis where in the days of the Armenotartar wars he organized the defense of the entire area. Following the declaration of the Turkish Constitution he represented Erzeroum in the Turkish parliament from 1910-1914. With the advent of World War I he played a very important role in the formation of Armenian volunteer regiments. In 1917 he journeyed to the United States as a special emissary of good will appointed by the Armenian National Bureau. From 1920 until the fall of the Republic he was the first diplomatic representative of Armenia in Washington. After the Sovietization of Armenia Armen Garo left America for Switzerland for new assignments, but now he was a broken man. Already a sick man, his deep grief only intensified his illness.
Armen Garo who had worked so selflessly for the emancipation of his fatherland, died in Geneva on March 27, 1923, without seeing his beloved independent Armenia.
ARSEN (1885-1941) Arsen Mikaelian, the noted revolutionary worker and intellectual, was born in Norshen, Karabagh. His baptismal name was Arsen Ter Hovanessian, and he was the son of a patriotic and well-educated family, his father being a devoted educator.
Rebellious by nature, Arsen's educational life took him through various schools, including the famed Gevorgian Jemaran, until he graduated from the law school of the University of St. Petersburg · Pleasant in manner, he was a born leader and organizer. In 1912, a year after graduation, he was already entrusted responsible duties in the Federation, which movement he had joined at the turn of the century.
In 1914 he was instructed to go to Turkish Armenia to prepare a memorandum on conditions of the Armenian people and the situation of the organized forces of the Federation. But the advent of World War I forced him to move to Europe from where he proceeded to the United States. After four years in the U. S. as a field worker, he went to Paris to join the national convention as delegate of the Armenian-Americans, in 1919, and later to Armenia, where he remained until the fall of the Republic.

In 1921 he was back in the U. S. once again where for the following twenty years he served his organization as field worker, editor, member of the Central Committee, Secretary and delegate to the Federation's general conventions.
His premature and unexpected death from pneumonia again found Arsen at his post. He had just arrived in South America as field worker and editor of the "Armenia" when he fell a victim of the dread disease.
BAROOIR (870 B. C. ?) A legendary patriarch or king of Armenia, descendent of Haig, Barooir, according to tradition was a brave man who in his day allied himself with Kiaksar, the prince of Media, and invading Babylonia occupied the capital city of Nineveh.
CHRISTOPHER (1859-1905) Christopher Mikaelian, born in Upper Akoulis near Kakhitchevan in 1859, was one of the three founders of the ARF. Upon graduation from his native school he assumed the position of teacher, but while teaching reading and writing, he would incorporate in his classes the elements of military drill and marksmanship.
His colorful, adventurous. and completely selfless life took him through years when he was subjected to imprisonment, persecutions and came close to loosing his life. But the man who had organized the ARF almost singlehandedly, never evaded the call of duty, even to the extent of preparing and testing bombs with which his organization had planned to punish the Red Sultan. And he was engaged in these experiments on the Hill of Vidosh, Bulgaria, on March 17, 1905, when a bomb exploded prematurely and a piece of shrapnel imbedded itself in a heart which had palpitated for the Armenian people alone.
Christopher was a revolutionary in the exalted sense, revolutionary in thought and action.
DAGHLIAN (1921-1945) Harry K. Daghlian, the atomic scientist, was born on May 4, 1921, in Waterbury, Conn.. and was the first American scientist to die as a result of the mysterious burns caused by fissionable materials during the atomic bomb experiments of World War II.
A very serious young man, Harry had devoted his life very early to the service of mankind through science. And it was actually in patriotic and humanitarian service that he lost his life at 24 years of age.
He was a brilliant student and was educated at the Mass. Institute of Technology and Purdue University, until he was called by the Manhattan District in 1943, to work with the team of scientists which succeeded in harnessing atomic power.
His death followed an accident while experimenting with fissionable material. He sacrificed himself to save his fellow scientists.
As a last measure of devotion he allowed doctors attending him in his last painful days to experiment on him in an effort to find a cure for atomic poisoning.
ERIVAN The city of Erivan, founded by King Yervant of Armenia, and known as Yervantavan in ancient times, has played a historic role in Armenian life as a major city and also many times as the capital city of Armenia.
Situated on the Araratian Plateau, the City of Erivan is nearly 2500 feet above sea level and enjoys the full view of the majestic mountains of Ararat and Arakadz. It is bordered on the east by the river Hraztan and is approximately 10 miles from Etchmiadzin and 12 miles from the Araxes.
The climate of Erivan is very healthful and it is a great fruit bearing center.
Erivan was the capital of the Free and Independent Republic of Armenia from 1918 to 1920, and is the so-called capital of the present Soviet Republic of Armenia.
Not far from the modern city of Erivan may be found historic sites such as the ruins of Ardashad, the palace of Karni, the small hill of Khor Virab, the remains of ancient Armavir where once stood the holy forest of poplars, a foremost religious center in pagan Armenian days, and Tvin, which is but a village today.
GAIDZAG (1875-1904) Gaidzag, baptismal name Vagharshag Baghdassarian, was a native of Moush. Little is known about his youth except that he was a true product of his times, that is he was introduced to the ideals of national emancipation while still in his teens, and that he embraced those ideals until his death.
He fought alternately with Arabo and Aghpuyr Serop, until in 1904 he was given command of the "Djokhk" (Hell) cavalry detachment, which later in the same year played a major role in the uprising of Sassoun.
Gaidzag was killed in battle on July 7, 1904, as he led his men into a counter-attack. He was only 29 years of age.
HAIG The greatest of Armenian legendary heroes, the arch-patriarch, believed to have lived around 2350 B.C., was Haig, the son of Torkom. He is considered to be the founder of the Armenian nation, a mighty bowman, the conqueror of Bel, the Babylonian king, who strived to subjugate all people.
The founding of the first Armenian political state is embodied in the legend of Haig, and in his initial act of rebellion against oppression is found the inspiration for all Armenians never to bow their heads when their independence is threatened.
Born in Kantzak, in 1882, he was educated in various schools in his native land and abroad, and the experience gathered during his early journeys played a great role in his future activities.
It may be said, and without exaggeration, that from 1902 when he began to organize his schoolmates, until his death in 1940, Jamalian remained in the avant guard of the organization.
He was the organizer and spokesman for the Armenian Students Association in Europe; he was a delegate to a great number of regional and international ARF conventions; from time to time he was a field worker, editor, theoretician, underground organizer, lecturer, writer, volunteer fighter; he was a member of the Armenian delegation which met with Russian emissaries in Riga, after the February 18 uprising in Armenia; he was a field worker-atlarge, member of the ARF Bureau, and a foremost exponent of close cooperation between Caucasian nations.
He died on December 27, 1940, a victim of pneumonia, leaving the realization of his dreams to the generations to come.

KEVORK CHAVOOSH (1871-1907) Kevork Chavoosh, the hero of Dalvorig, the lion of Sassoun, was born in 1871 in the province of Daron. It has been related that his first and only love was his gun, which he obtained very early in life, always keep at the service of his tortured people, and which he did not abandon until his tragic and untimely death in 1907.
He met Arabo, the famed guerilla fighter, when he was still in school, and this initial meeting resulted in young Kevork's decision to serve selflessly.
From 1890 until the uprising of Sassoun in 1904, Kevork Chavoosh, rushed from one heroic battle to the next. He saw action in Dalvorig. Fought side by side with Mihran Damadian, Mourad the Huntchakian, Aghpuyr Serop and Kourken. Lived through the horrors of a Turkish prison from which he escaped, and at last, as Antranig's chief of staff, fought for three weeks in Sassoun.
Following the fall of Sassoun, not wanting to leave the people of Sassoun unprotected, Kevork chose to stay behind when most of the Armenian fighters withdrew to the Caucasus. The years which followed were most trying. He not only sustained the attacks of the enemy, distributed food to and maintained order in the populace but he had to be ever watchful of the traitors who went as far as to attempt poisoning him.
He was killed on April 17, 1907, a victim of betrayal, thus bringing to a close an amazing era of eighteen years of revolutionary activity and more than forty battles.
MOURAD (1874-1918) Mourad Khrimian, the beloved "Sebastatzi Mourad" of contemporary revolutionary fame, was born in humble surroundings in the village of Govdoun, the province of Sebastia.
He had had no formal schooling and it was claimed that his most treasured belongings were his fast horse and trusty gun.
He was a legendary figure; a natural leader and a firm disciplinarian.
His complete presence of mind under all circumstances would amaze his volunteers, and his commands, seemingly given at the spur of a moment, never failed to produce the expected result, as they were born of experience and farsightedness.
His name became famous very early while he still fought as a foot soldier in various volunteer bands; and that fame grew as he participated in the revolt of Sassoun, played a major role on the Turkish front in the pre-Independence days as the commander of one of the volunteer regiments, and reached its peak in 1918, when Mourad single handedly led the defense of Baku, in which struggle he also lost his life.
Mourad died before he had a chance to see Free Armenia. Yet he died happy, certain that his efforts had not been wasted and that they opened the way to a younger generation to enjoy that which he had only dreamed of.
MUSA DAGH Musa Dagh, a mountain in the north of Alexandretta overlooking the Mediterranean, had doubtless little or no meaning for most Armenians until August, 1915.
It was in that month that 4500 Armenian men, women and children from the seven villages lying at the foot of this rocky rise, confronted by complete annihiliation at the hands of the Turks, chose to die fighting and took their positions on the sides of the mountain.
This heroic act of self-defense by a handful of Armenians against numerous Turkish regulars, which had already been chronicled and immortalized by Franz Werfel in his historical novel; "The Forty Days of Musa Dagh," not only proved to the world once again that Armenians know how to die for their freedom, but also impressed the foreign powers to such an extent that when after 51 days the Armenians were forced to raise the flag of surrender, they intervened and enabled those on the mountain to be evacuated safely.
Today there are no Armenians who do not know or remember the name of Musa Dagh. In it they find a living inspiration.
NAVASSART The ancient Armenian New Year's Day was called the Day of Navassart.
This holiday was observed as a rule in the late summer of early fall, when the work in the fields would be over and the happy people would gather to give thanks to their gods and offer them sacrifices for better crops in the year to come.
It has been told that the Day was celebrated with great pomp and ceremony. Armenians from all levels of life would gather in predesignated areas and would participate, without distinction of social standing, in the elaborate pageantry designed for the day, including musical, literary, athletic contests, various games for the children and the ladies, and of course, unlimited feasting with the best and sweetest of Armenian wines to quench the thirst of the contenders.
RAFFI (1835-1888) Hagop Melik Hagopian, who lives in the hearts of all Armenians as "Raffi," was the most beloved and widely read Armenian writer of his day. A man of varied talents, Raffi was novelist, poet, historian, journalist, and political writer. Though he was singularly successful in all fields of literature he is best known today as the father of the Armenian novel.
Raffi's entire life was devoted to the propagation of Armenian nationalistic spirit, to agitation against the despotic rule of the Turk over his countrymen, to weaving a spell of beauty and genius eagerly indulged in by people everywhere.
His literary activity began in earnest in 1858 and continued intense and unabated for thirty years. His dire financial state, however, forced him into extracurricular activities as well. He was at times a salesman in a clothing store, correspondent to various Armenian periodicals, and teacher.
Raffi was a genius fired by the sparks of profound patriotism. He died on April 25, 1888, before he could enjoy the fruits of the deep impression he had been able to leave upon the youth of his day.
Among the most appreciative devotees of Raffi may be found Kamar Katiba, another great Armenian writer, who said of Raffi: "He awakened national feeling while others merely beat the drums Michael Varantian called him "the Dante of Armenians," and Prof. Abeghian declared that he "showed the path of liberty." ROSDOM (1867-1919) Rosdom, his real name Stepan Zorian, was the most colorful member of the triumvirate which founded the ARF. He was the son of a wealthy patriarchal family and was born in 1867, in Tzghna.
It would be impossible to describe his almost cinematic life in a few lines.
He was always in motion and bubbling activity. He was an amazing man; shabby, his exterior unkempt, his speech artless, he wrote with difficulty and effort. But anyone who lived with him for a while could not help being enchanted by the magnitude of his soul. He was an agronomist by education, but was a teacher, field worker, editor, type-setter, mail clerk, and revolutionary, whenever someone was needed to fill those posts. He was destined to be the untiring arm of the ARF, and he knew that in order to keep that arm in motion he would himself have to set the supreme example of self-sacrifice before he could have the right to demand the same from others.
He died in Tiflis on January 18, 1919, a victim of typhus fever. He lived for Armenia, for the conception of Armenia's freedom, yet died, as Christopher and Zavarian, without having seen his fatherland and his precious dreams transform themselves into a reality.
SASSOUN A mountainous region, comprising 25 to 30 Armenian and Kurdish villages, and a half hour's journey due south from the plane of Moush, Sas soun, has for long been regarded, as a cradle of Armenian patriotism.
Its natives are handsome and tall in stature, have never recognized fear.
Contemporary Armenian revolutionary history is enriched by their heroic devotion and sacrifice to the cause of national emancipation.
The Turkish government, realizing early the rebellious nature of the Armenians in Sassoun, attempted to drown the Sassountzis' initial attempts to free themselves in blood, by organizing the 1894 general massacres. But not even blood-shed could stop them as the Turks soon found out. There followed in 1904 the uprising of Sassoun and again the heroic defense by the Armenians in 1915, proving again and again that Armenian spirit cannot be crushed as long as there is one Armenian alive.
It has been said that cities or regions become famous after the exploits of some national hero. However, in regards to Sassoun, it may be safe to say that many an Armenian fighter has gained prestige after having fought in Sassoun.
SEBOUH (1872-1940) Sebouh, the famed Armenian emancipatory fighter, whose baptismal name was Arshag Nersessian was called "General Sebouh" by devoted Armenians. He was born in Papert in 1872.
He devoted his entire life to the service of his people and to the struggle to liberate his beloved fatherland. Still a very young man he joined the ranks of the ARF and became one of the soldiers of the famous volunteer chief, Kevork Chavoush. He next fought with the celebrated General Antranik and took part in the revolt of Sassoun in 1904. During the Armenotartar wars, General Sebouh, still a young man, was already a seasoned veteran. Upon the declaration of the Turkish Constitution in 1908, Sebouh laid down his arms until a few years later, the fatuity of this document was proven to all. With the advent of World War I, he joined the Armenian volunteer bands, and fought as regimental commander in the group headed by Antranik. He later fought in the battles that wrought Armenian Independence, and during the Republican years he held various military positions, fought in the Turco-Armenian wars of 1920, and played a decisive role in the suppression of the May 1920 Bolshevik uprising in Armenia. After the fall of Armenia he moved to the United States where he remained until his death on July 31, 1940.
During his last years, the veteran fighter never gave up hope that his beloved Armenia would still some day be free, and he preached this faith wherever he went.
SIAMANTO (1878-1915) Siamanto, baptismal name Adom Yardjanian, one of the foremost poets of contemporary Armenian literature, was born in Agin, in 1878, the very same year when the Armenian Question was to receive its first international recognition.
He was a well travelled man, and his spiritual experiences coupled later on with the soul shaking impact of ideals of national emancipation and liberty enabled his pen to cry of the millions of Armenians to be avenged.
Siamanto too was a victim of the 1915 deportations. With his death the Armenians lost the true singer of the most heroic period in contemporary Armenian history the Dashnaktzakan era, and era of unparalleled suffering and struggle which he expressed with deep emotion and imagination, and in doing so he created a literature unique and immortal.
SUINIK - - One of the oldest of Armenian provinces, Suinik was the dwelling of the noble Suni family, whose princes were the right hand men of many Armenian kings.
It is bordered on the north by the Lake of Sevan, on the east by Karabagh, and in the south and part of the west by the River Araxes.
It has been noted in history for its patriotic people. It is also in Suinik that the ancient canton of Vayotz Tzor was found. dating perhaps as far back as in the days of Haig.
TIGRANES (The Great) The definite history of Armenia may be said to begin with Tigranes II, called the Great (94-54 B.C.). His reign is cherished by every Armenian, because through his military prowess, when still a young man, the King of Kings, who was once a hostage in Persian hands, effected Armenia's widest expansion. He waged successful wars against the various barbarian tribes of the north. Against the Cappadocians, the Parthians, the Assyrians, and conquered Cilicia, Syria, and Pheonicia. He crossed swords with the Romans under Lucullus and Pompey. The boundaries of his kingdom extended from the river Kura to the Jordan, and from the Median mountains to the base of the Taurus range.
With Mithidrates, king of Pontus, his father-in-law and ally, for years he waged a bitter war with the Romans for the mastery of Asia Minor, but was eventually defeated when he failed to move with due caution.
With his defeat, the Artaxiad Dynasty started its decline.
VARANTIAN (1874-1934) The foremost theoretician of the Armenian Revolutionary Federation was Michael Varantian. He was a native of the province of Varant, in Karabagh and his baptismal name was Michael Hovanissian. It may be said without exaggeration that Varantian was actually the formulator of organizational orientation, the cultivator of ARF principles and theories, and their foremost propagator.
Beside his voluminous literary output, Varantian also served his people and organization fully as editor, lecturer, ARF representative to the Socalist International, embassador to Rome during the Republic, and historian.
Varantian died in France in 1934 after a heart attack.
A brilliant scholar with a wide scope, he was always a great believer in and held great respect and love for everything that was Armenian. In his lifetime he never once ceased his struggle and by his personal example he inspired everyone, especially the youth.
VARTAN Vartan Mamikonian who, through his mother was the grandson of the Patriarch Sahak, was one of the most patriotic nobleman of Armenia during the fateful days when Persia attempted to force Armenians in her dominions to embrace the Mazdean religion.
Vartan, who enjoyed the fullest esteem and trust of the Armenians, made every effort to have Hazgerd, King of Persia, rescind his decree against Christianity. But once convinced of the futility of his efforts, he gave the signal for a violent revolt in Armenia, and, though betrayed by one of his countrymen, Vasak Suni, he led his small force to face the Persian cohorts at Avarair.
Vartan the Great lost his life in this historic battle in 451 A.D. along with many of his men, but his courageous stand at Avarair saved the nation, for the Persians never expecting such stout resistance had to stop to make good their losses.
The memory of Vartan's supreme sacrifice and that of his fellow-soldiers has been so ardently cherished that to the present time the Armenian church celebrates the anniversary of the battle of Avarair and pays tribute to the heroes who fell there.
VARTANANTZ When the thirty vear war between Rome and Persia over the mastery of Armenia came to an end, Armenia found itself divided between Persia and the Eastern Roman Empire, also known as the Byzantine Empire.
The Armenians, of course, did not submit passively to this division of their fatherland and they carried on a stubborn guerilla warfare which crystalized itself in three successive rebellions in an effort to vindicate their religion and throw off the Persian yoke.

The first rebellion especially is significant in Armenian history, for it was during this time that the Battle of Avarayr was fought, settling once and for all that Armenians would not yield to the Persians in the matter of their religious liberty. In Armenian history this has been known as the Vartanantz War, after the heroic general, Vartan Mamikonian, who led the Armenian patriots.
The Battle of Avarayr took, place on July 2, 451 A.D. It is considered one of the decisive battles in Armenian history because though losers, Armenians proved their readiness to fight for their right to be religiously and nationally independent.
Led by St. Vartan, and inspired by Ghevont Yeretz, 60,000 Armenians faced an enemy hord of 300,000 Persians. St. Vartan and 1036 of his heroic followers were martyred before the day came to its end, but the Persian aim to subjugate Armenians and convert them completely from Christianity to Zoroastrianism, was thwarted.
VRAMIAN (1871-1915) One of the most colorful public and organizational figures in recent Armenian revolutionary history was Arshag Vramian, baptismal name Onnig Tertzagian.
He was a native of Constantinople, and he had received part of his higher education at the Kevorkian Jemaran in Etchmiadzin.
Vramian was a man of varied talents, and in spite of his extreme idealism, he was endowed with great organizational ability.
As a devoted servant of the Armenian revolution, Vramian, was at times editor of "Droshak" and the "Azadamart." He visited the United States as a field worker and became editor of the "Hairenik." He may well be considered the one who put the organization on sound footing in America. He spent considerable time in Van and assisted Aram in the organization of local self-defense teams. He was a representative from the district of Van to the Turkish Parliament from 1913-1914.
His end came under most tragic circumstances. Always a great believer in the basic good of men, Vramian, readily accepted the invitation of the governor of Van, the infamous Jevdet, to meet and reach an understanding regarding Armeno-Turkish relations. These talks, however, never materialized. Vramian and Ishkhan of Van, who had accompanied him, were immediately seized and put to death, thus officially opening the era of terror which was soon to envelop the Armenian people ZAVARIAN (1866-1913) Of the triumvirate that is credited with the founding of the ARF, Simon Zavarian was the heart, Christopher the leading spirit, while Rosdom was the untiring arm.
Zavarian, born in the village of Aikehad in Lori in 1866, was an unimpressive man, until someone looked well into his dark eyes, or observed his sudden impulsive gestures. Educated at the Petrovska College of Moscow, in addition to his agronomical studies, he had been steeped early in thoughts of revolution. And though he was peace loving and early in life Zavarian felt that the change in world politics could be effected by propaganda and evolution alone, he soon changed in view of the sporadic and furtive butchering of Armenians in Turkey.
