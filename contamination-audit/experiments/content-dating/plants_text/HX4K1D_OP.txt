COLONIAL APPRECIATION OF THE NECESSITY FOR UNION FROM VERY EARLY Colonial days a strong senti ment developed for union amongst the colonies-a union recognizing the sovereignty of each colony under a government of their people through representatives in a Congress or Convention of the confederated governments. The necessity for such a union was made the more manifest by the geographical situation of the colonies, their common commercial and industrial interests and necessity for defense against aggression of the French and the massacres of the Indians; the growing tyranny of 

England and petty quarrels among themselves. As time passed and the colonies grew and prospered the necessity for union became the greater and the more apparent.

Recognizing this necessity several of the Colonial Governors, especially those who had served as governors of several colonies successively, recommended to England that one governor be appointed over all of the colonies. Francis Nicholson, who had been successively appointed Lieutenant Governor of New 

York, Maryland and Virginia, as Sir Edmund

Andros, who Nicholson had succeeded as Governor of Virginia, had also been appointed Governor of all of New England, New York and Virginia, openly declared that the colonies should be united in a single confederation under one governor with a strong standing army.

As early as 1639 several towns in Connecticut entered into a, written compact under which, as its preamble recited, these towns sought to establish a "Decent government according to God to maintain 1

This compact recognized no allegience to any other government, not excepting England, and was, as it recited, "A combination and confederation together to maintain and preserve liberty and purity of the gospel," and to direct civil affairs by laws, rules, orders and decrees, as, "Shall be made, ordered and decreed." This compact remained the fundamental law of Connecticut until the

"Thus simply were the towns of this federal union, so obviously the germ of the union of States of the next century," observes Scribner's History of the

Several years later, in 1643, a similar confederation was made embracing the provinces of Massachusetts, New Plymouth, Connecticut and New

Haven. Under the written constitution of this confederation, described as the United Colonies of New England, all powers and rights given it were strictly defined and limited and each colony was guaranteed the right of self government.

In 1754 the British Government directed the colonies to form a confederation for mutual support so as to resist the encroachments of the French from 

Canada in the Ohio and Mississippi valley regions. 

On the 19th day of June of that year twenty-five delegates from seven northern colonies met at 

Albany, New York. Benjamin Franklin, a delegate from Pennsylvania, on his way to the Convention, drew up a plan for the union of the colonies under one government but found upon his arrival several delegates from other colonies had done likewise. 

The Convention adopted Franklin's plan for union, providing for a confederation of the colonies with

a President-General appointed by the Crown of England who was given a veto over the acts of the Council provided for, to be chosen by the legislative assemblies of the respective colonies. In this mode of choice of the Council members and the veto power given the President-General, can clearly be seen the origin of the Presidential veto and the original method for the election of Senators as was later provided by the Constitution.

The plan was, however, rejected by both England and the colonies. The first, thought too much power was given to the people and the second that too much was given to the King through the veto power given the President-General, appointed by him. In this objection is also, no doubt, found the origin of the Constitutional provision granting Congress the power to pass an act over the President's veto. 

Later, at the invitation of Massachusetts, the colonies sent delegates to a general Congress to secure their combined action upon the obnoxious 

Stamp Act passed by Parliament. This Congress met in the old City Hall in New York City on the first Tuesday in October, 1765, and drafted a Bill of Rights for the colonies and their people which was previous to the one drafted by the Congress on 

October 14th, 1774, to be followed by the Declaration of Independence on memorable July 4th, 1776. 

On the 9th of July, 1778, after their independence had been declared as the result of the Revolution, the now States adopted the Articles of Confederation under which all powers delegated to the Confederacy, thereby created, were lodged in a Congress of one House representing, not the people of the States but the State governments and so elected by their legislatures as representative of their governments.

CITIZENSHIP UNDER THE CONSTITUTION IN FEBRUARY, 1787, the Continental Congress created by the Articles of Confederation, passed a resolution calling a Convention of delegates from all the States to revise and enlarge the Articles of Confederation. The Convention met at Philadelphia and was presided over by General Washington as its President. On the 17th of September, 1787, after protracted study and debate and reconciliation of much conflict in opinion among its members, the Convention framed and presented to the States and the people the Constitution of the United States of America, for their adoption and the formation of a more perfect union between them, as is recited in the preamble.

The word people, however, as used in the preamble and with which we are all familiar, should not be mistaken for citizen. Nowhere in the Constitution is there any definite mention made of 

United States citizenship, or American citizenship, as we now term it.

While the provisions of the Constitution which provide that: "No person except a natural born citizen, or citizen of the United States at the time of the adoption of this Constitution, shall be eligible to the office of President" and, "No person shall be a 

Senator who shall not have been nine years a citizen of the United States," nor, "A Representative who shall not have been seven years a citizen of the United States," would seem to indirectly recognize United States citizenship, the Constitution, as stated, does not directly or definitely provide for such citizenship and these requirements of citizenship for

པ་ election to the office of President and as a Senator or Representative in Congress can equally as well mean citizenship in the States as united and this interpretation is supported by the provision, directly relating to citizenship, contained in Section 2 of Article IV of the Constitution, which provides, that: "The citizens of each State shall be entitled to all the privileges and immunities of citizens in the several States" (italics writer's). The only provision directly mentioning citizenship anywhere to be found in the Constitution. In thus securing to the citizens of each State the privileges and immunities of citizens of the several States, perhaps, the citizens of each State may so be considered citizens of the whole, or of the United States, but the Constitution does not so state but distinctly restricts the privilege to that, "of citizens of the several States," which applies to an indefinite number of individuals rather than a union or collection of individuals, and considering the jealousy with which the States regarded their separate State entity it cannot be supposed that the citizens of any one State ever intended by the Constitution to exchange their State citizenship for a citizenship in the Union which they and their States created by adopting the Constitution. 

That a citizenship of the Union of States was never contemplated by the framers of the Constitution is also further emphasized by many other provisions of the Constitution. The tenth amendment, adopted almost simultaneously with the adoption of the Constitution reserves all powers not delegated to the United States to the States respectively, or to the people. (Italics writer's.) Here again the word used is people and not citizens. In both the main body

of the Constitution and in the twelfth amendment it is provided: "In choosing the President, the votes shall be taken by States, the representation from each State having one vote, and in defining treason 

Section 3 of Article III uses the plural pronouns, 

"them" and "they" instead of the singular “it” in referring to the United States thus recognizing a plural union of States in place of a single federal or national government of the people of the composite whole.

As originally provided by the Constitution United States Senators were elected by the State legislatures and not by the people, either as citizens of the respective States or of the United States. Again, if vacancies occur during the recess of the legislatures of the States the State Executive is given the power to make appointments until the next meeting of the State legislature and not the President or the people, either as people or citizens of the States or of the United States. Likewise, if vacancies occur in the House of Representatives it is again the Executive of the State who is given the authority to issue writs of election to fill such vacancies, until the next regular election.

Though done in the name of the people, as its preamble recites, the adoption of the Constitution was required to be done by Conventions in the respective States and not by a vote of the people direct. 

The provision made for amendments also is not by a vote of the people but through proposals of twothirds of both Houses of Congress, or by Conventions of the States, to be called by Congress upon the application of two-thirds of the State legislatures. 

Then as either mode for proposing amendments is

chosen, by the adoption of such amendments by the legislatures of three-fourths of the States, or by Conventions of a like number of the States and not by the people.

As we have seen the Constitution is a studiously composed compilation of many previously made attempts for similar constitutions for a union of the colonies and is not an invention or creation of those men who composed the Convention which designed and framed it. 

It is quite apparent that the Constitution is a most carefully designed and coordinated whole and its objects and purposes can only be properly ascertained and understood when it is so considered. 

Each provision dovetails and is morticed into some other so that to change or destroy any one is oftentimes impossible without greatly injuring or destroying the whole.

DEMOCRACY OR REPUBLIC? 

EVERY GOVERNMENT naturally possesses three functions, Executive, Legislative and Judicial. In an absolute Monarchy, the Monarch possesses and exercises all three of these functions at will. He legislates by edict, judges of the meaning and purpose of his edicts and executes and carries them out according to his own arbitrary desire. In a pure democracy the people would attempt the same but lacking full accord in purpose, dissension among them must result and inevitably does end in revolution destructive of their democracy, ending in the monarchistic rule of dictators and military despots, as history has ever proven. 

James Madison, a member of the Constitutional Convention, writing in advocacy of the adoption of the Constitution, observes: "Democracies have ever been spectacles of turbulence and contention; have ever been found incompatible with personal security or the rights of property and have in general been as short in their lives as they have been violent in their deaths." 

Guarding against such abuse the framers designed and established, under the Constitution, a republican form of government rather than a democracy. This fact is definitely recognized and emphasized in the Constitutional provision contained in Section 4 of Article IV which provides: "The 

United States shall guarantee to every State in this Union a republican form of government." (Italics writer's.)

The Federal union of the independent States which compose it is divided into three departments,

executive, legislative and judicial. The powers of each are clearly defined and yet so coordinated as to give solidity to the acts of the government as a whole. The Constitution is the supreme law. All laws must conform to its provisions or are void and it is the grave duty of the judiciary, after fair hearing and impartial consideration, which the judiciary alone is equipped to give, to declare laws contravening the Constitution void, the final decision resting necessarily with the highest Court, the Supreme Court, created by the Constitution. All other Federal Courts are created by Congress by authority conferred upon it by the Constitution. 

As all laws are passed by a majority of a quorum of Congress with more consideration being given to their policy and expediency than to their constitutionality it does not seem at all improper or unreasonable to permit of their constitutionality being judged of by the Supreme Court by a majority decision of the whole Court as has always been the practice. 

Because of its great and important jurisdiction, the Supreme Court should always be composed of the ablest lawyers of proven integrity and patriotism with wide practical experience in practice at the bar and not of politicians or theorists, if the American Union and the Constitutional liberties of the people are to be preserved.

Against the inexpediency or impolitic nature of a law the Constitution guards first, by requiring its passage by two Houses of Congress, differently elected as originally provided by the Constitution, one, by the State legislatures, whose representative body it was intended to be, and the other by the

people, who it was intended to represent, and further by giving the Executive the power of veto of any law as he may judge to be wisest and best for the interests of all. To guard against any arbitrary or unworthy exercise of the veto by the Executive, the Constitution gives Congress the power to repass the vetoed law over the Executive veto by a twothirds vote of each house, which is quite frequently done. 

Both the Executive and the Judiciary are amenable to Congress through its Constitutionally conferred power of impeachment of any official for improper conduct in office. To guard against abuse and injustice both Houses of Congress must act. 

The impeachment must be brought by the House of Representatives and be heard before the Senate in a fair and regularly conducted trial of the accused official. A unanimous Act or Resolution of both Houses of Congress cannot remove a Federal official from office. Their removal is through impeachment proceedings only as provided by the Constitution. However, either House may remove one of its own members as the Constitution makes each House of Congress the judge of the qualifications of its members. This must of necessity be true.

EFFECT OF THE 17TH AMENDMENT UPON LEGISLATIVE CHECK AS ORIGINALLY designed by the Constitution the 

Congress it created consisted of two Houses differently elected so as to prove a check against either hasty or unwise legislation. It was known by the framers of the Constitution that two heads were better than one and a legislature composed of representatives of the States and of representatives of the people would prove to be better balanced in judgment upon all questions than one composed solely of representatives of either the States or the people. 

The first had been tried under the then existing Articles of Confederation without very great success. The Congress under the Constitution, therefore, was composed of a Senate, consisting of State representatives and a House of Representatives, consisting of representatives of the people. The wisdom and purpose of this dual arrangement in the composition of the Congress was explained by Mr. Madison-a member of the Constitutional Convention, later President of the United States and frequently referred to as the father of the Constitution. Writing in the Federalist he observes: "Another advantage accruing from this ingredient in the constitution of the Senate," speaking of the election of Senators by the State legislatures as representatives of the States rather than of the people, "is the additional impediment it must prove against improper acts of legislation. No law or resolution can be passed without the concurrence, first, of a majority of the people and then of a majority of the States."

Many years later, Mr. Justice Story, the most au- 11

thoritative of all writers upon the Constitution also explains this original design and purpose for two 

Houses of Congress, differently elected, as follows: "A Senate, duly constituted, would not only operate as a salutary check upon the Representatives, but occasionally upon the people themselves, against their own temporary delusions and errors. The cool, deliberate sense of the community ought, in all governments, ultimately prevail over the views of their rulers. But there are particular moments in public affairs, when the people, stimulated by some irregular passion, or some illicit advantage, or misled by the artful misrepresentations of interested men, may call for measures, which they themselves will afterwards be most ready to lament and condemn. 

In such critical moments, how salutary will be the interference of a body of respectable citizens, chosen without reference to the exciting cause, to check the misguided career of public opinion, and to suspend the blow, until reason, justice and truth can regain their authority over the public mind?"

Section 10 of Article I of the Constitution pro hibits any State entering into a treaty, alliance or confederation and by Section 2 of Article II, the 

President is given the power, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate the representative originally, of the States, let us remember-to make treaties and shall nominate by and with the advice also of the Senate, shall appoint Ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls as all other Federal officers, including the Justices of the Supreme Court, thus, when the States were represented in the Senate, giving them a voice in the making of all treaties and the appointment of all Federal officers in return

for their having relinquished this sovereign right and conferred it upon the Union. 

The Seventeenth Amendment to the Constitution, providing for the election of Senators by the people of the States, the same as the Representatives in the peoples House of Congress are elected, deprives the State governments of all representation in Congress and all voice in the making of treaties and the appointment of officials of the Federal government. 

It also removes that salutary check upon legislation to which both Mr. Madison and Justice Story referred as shown.

This Amendment, therefore, absolutely destroys the fundamental original design of the framers of the Constitution for two Houses in Congress to check each other and bring about wiser legislation through their independence of each other by the difference in the mode of electing each House.

Furthermore, Article V of the Constitution which is the Article providing the mode and manner for making amendments to the Constitution, provides: "That no State," notice not the people of no State, but no State, "without its consent, shall be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate." The singular pronoun "it" being used to signify State corporate entity and not the plural, their, as would have been used had the people of the State and not the State been contemplated.

Equal suffrage, necessarily implies some suffrage, as without some suffrage it is as idle to speak of equal suffrage as it would be to speak of equal wealth without some wealth. With no wealth at all persons would be equally poor, not equally wealthy, so by the Seventeenth Amendment the States being

deprived of all suffrage are necessarily deprived of equal suffrage. Hence, the Seventeenth Amendment to be legally adopted requires the consent of all of the States, otherwise those States which did not adopt it are deprived of their equal suffrage in the Senate without their consent against the express provision and command of the Constitution. The 

Seventeenth Amendment is the only Amendment to the Constitution which cannot be made as is provided for all others but requires the unanimous consent of all of the States for its adoption. The 

Senate, therefore, as at present elected by the people under the amendment adopted by three-fourths of the States, is but a de facto-in point of fact-body rather than a de jure-in point of right-body. 

Legislation passed by the Congress so composed, however, is valid since legally the acts of a de facto body are valid, especially where concurred in by a de jure one, such as the House of Representatives and a de jure President.

The proposal and adoption of the Seventeenth Amendment was brought about through propaganda that wealthy men were purchasing election to the Senate by directly or indirectly misusing their wealth to influence the vote of members of the State legislatures in their favor.

The charge was not substantiated except, perhaps, in one or two isolated instances, not more than a half dozen throughout the entire history of the Senate. Had it been fully substantiated it still remained true that 95 per cent of the Senators when elected by the State legislatures were fairly and properly elected and that the then Convention system proved much more satisfactory for securing

fairness in elections than the State primary system of today which has been the result of the adoption of the Seventeenth Amendment. 

Associate Justice Henry B. Brown, of the Supreme

Court of the United States-when the Seventeenth Amendment was before the States and the people for adoption-in closing a lecture to the law students of Georgetown University, warned the class against the adoption of the amendment, pronouncing it a most dangerous innovation upon the Constitutional checks and balances provided by the Constitution for safeguarding the liberties of the people.

APPORTIONMENT OF TAXES 

SECTION 2 OF ARTICLE I of the Constitution pro vides that: "Representatives and direct taxes shall be apportioned among the several States according to their respective numbers" and Section 

9 of Article I, that: "No capitation, or other direct tax shall be laid, unless in proportion to the census or enumeration hereinbefore directed to be taken." 

Again, in this the entity of the States is recognized and provided for which is the Constitutional purpose throughout and from the start the Preamble concluding: "Do ordain and establish this Constitution for the United States of America," and not for the united people of America. The Sixteenth Amendment disregards all of this purpose and policy directly repealing the provisions contained in Sections 

2 and 9 of Article I and providing in lieu of them that: "The Congress shall have power to lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment among the several States and without regard to any census or enuemeration."

Under the Sixteenth Amendment the people now are taxed as citizens of the entire country rather than as citizens of their respective States and disproportionately upon their incomes with the right to so tax their real estate and personal property also. 

This is as dangerous and arbitrary power as could possibly be given or possessed by any government whether a Monarchy, Republic or Democracy for the power to indiscriminately tax is the power to destroy as the Supreme Court declared years ago. 

Andrew Jackson also in his message to the people 16

upon leaving the Presidency, declared with wisdom, that: "Congress has no right under the Constitution to take money from the people unless it is required to execute some one of the specific powers entrusted to the government, and if they raise more than is necessary for such purpose, it is an abuse of the power of taxation, and unjust and oppressive." 

Because of freedom from excessive taxation and the constitutional guarantees of life, liberty, property rights and the individual's freedom to pursue his happiness unmolested and unhampered by burdensome laws the American people have been encouraged and inspired to that great effort which so quickly and effectively wrought a high civilization and prosperity out of the American wilderness and only through the maintenance of such freedom can they continue to advance and thrive.

No more constructively progressive step could be taken by the American people at the present time for the preservation of their Constitutional government and liberties than the repeal of the Seventeenth Amendment and the modification of the Sixteenth by adding to it the provision that all taxation of incomes shall be upon the same ratio or percentage and no excess profit or sur tax shall be imposed. "Lest we forget" a brief mention of some of the Constitutionally guaranteed liberties should be made here.

No Executive may dissolve the legislative assemblies of the people or prevent their holding their regular sessions and this applies to the President who can neither prevent Congress from assembling in regular session or dissolve it in either a regular or special session as the Royal Colonial Governors

often did of the legislative assemblies and councils of the colonies they governed. No citizen can be deprived of life, liberty or property without due process of law nor be denied the right of freedom of religious worship, speech and press. No legislative act can be passed attainting a citizen of crime or bring him to trial for an act made a crime after the act was committed. Every charge of crime must be upon an indictment of a grand jury and every trial therefor be by a jury of twelve citizens and conviction only after fair hearing and by unanimous vote. Neither a State or its people can be discriminated against in favor of another State or its people, excepting as now permitted in taxation under the Sixteenth Amendment and the commerce of the people and the States is protected against governmental interference for the profit of the government. These, and many other like guarantees are secured the American people solely by their Constitutional government. In every other government of the world even today there are no such securities given the people and they are oftentimes denied each and all of such privileges.

FREEDOM OF RELIGION, SPEECH AND PRESS BECAUSE OF ITS present increased importance through much modern misconception of the Constitutional guarantee of freedom in religion and speech this privilege will be discussed a little in detail at this point. 

It is guaranteed by the Third Amendment to the 

Constitution which provides: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press, or the right of the people peacefully to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.' 99 The colonies had been denied all of these liberties by their Colonial Royal Governors and Councils and thoroughly understood what they meant by the provision so what the words meant to the framers of the Constitution is necessarily the only true guide to the proper meaning of this Constitutional guarantee. It is of interest and importance, therefore, to accurately determine their meaning. Eighteen years before the American Revolution and twenty-nine before the adoption of the Constitution and contemporaneous with the lives of many of the founders, Sir William Blackstone defined the meaning of freedom of press as "Laying no previous restrictions upon publications and not in freedom from censure for criminal matter when published," and he added, "So true will it be found, that to censure the licentious is to maintain the liberty of the press." 

Mr. Justice Story, of the Supreme Court, who has before been quoted, and a most authoritative writer 19

upon the Constitution and other legal subjects, shortly after the adoption of the Constitution, wrote concerning freedom of speech: "It imports not more than that every one shall have the right to speak, write and print his opinions upon any subject whatsoever, without prior restraint, so always that he does not injure any other person in his rights, property or personal reputation; and so always that he does not disturb the public peace, or attempt to subvert the government. It is in fact designed to guard against those abuses of power, by which, in some foreign governments men are not permitted to speak upon political subjects, or to write or publish anything without the express license of the government for the purpose."

Such freedom then is the liberty to express one's views upon any subject in a proper manner and upon a proper occasion. By it persons are not permitted to transgress the rules of propriety or to speak vindictively about anything or person, or to attack the fundamental principles of morality and decency or to defy law and advocate the overthrow or the undermining of the government, especially a government which guarantees and protects such freedom when kept within reasonable bounds. 

In recent years the question has come before the Supreme Court for decision in several well considered cases. In the case of Toledo Newspaper Company vs. United States, 247 U. S. Repts., page 419, the Court said: "The safeguarding and fruitification of free and Constitutional institutions is the very basis and mainstay upon which freedom of the press rests, and that freedom, therefore, does not,

and cannot, be held to include the right virtually to destroy such institutions." 

In the case of United States ex rel. Turner vs.

Williams, the Supreme Court held that none of the 

Constitutional guarantees respecting freedom of worship, speech or press, or petition are infringed by an Act of Congress making provision for the deportation of alien anarchists, whether that act be construed to apply only to persons who advocate forcible overthrow of government and the assassination of public officials, or is extended to those whose opposition is merely expressed as a political idea. 

In the recent case of Gitlow vs. New York, 268

U. S. Repts., page 667; the Court said: "It is a fundamental principle, long established, that the freedom of speech and press which is secured by the Constitution does not confer an absolute right to speak or publish, without responsibility, whatever one may choose." And, "That a State may punish those utterances imimical to public welfare, tending to corrupt public morals, incite crime, or disturb the public peace is not open to question."

In this case a distinction was sought to be made at the bar between utterances which advocate the immediate overthrow of the government and those intended only to inculcate doctrines designed to create a public opinion for the overthrow of the government at some future time.

Justices Louis D. Brandeis and Oliver W. Holmes seemed to have been impressed with such a distinction, Mr. Justice Brandeis in a dissenting opinion in which Justice Holmes concerred, saying, "If in the long run the beliefs express the dominant forces of

the community, the only meaning of free speech is that they should be given their chance and have their way"—rather contrary to all previous conceptions upon the subject.

Mr. Justice Sanford, rendering the opinion of the majority of the Court, courageously replied: "If the

State were compelled to wait until the apprehended danger became certain, then its right to protect itself would come into being simultaneously with the overthrow of the government, when there would be neither prosecuting officers nor courts for enforcement of the law."

The laws of Congress passed in 1860, '69, '74, and '82, prohibiting and punishing polygamy practiced by the Mormons, as bigamy, furnish an example and illustration of the limitations necessarily placed upon freedom of religion. The enforcement of these laws brought much suffering and hardships upon the Mormons for their refusal to obey them. Many were arrested, tried and fined or imprisoned and in some cases exiled. Church property was seized and confiscated, the right of francise was denied the Mormons and Utah denied admission into the Union until its people abandoned their conceptions of their religious idea of marriage.

Whether the punishments prescribed by these laws and inflicted under them to enforce obedience was an infringement of the Constitutional guarantee of freedom in religion eventually was brought before the Supreme Court for decision and the court held that they were not and upheld the constitutionality of the laws.

All laws must be enacted and upheld in conformity with the higher law of man's nature and natural

understanding of right and wrong which nature implants in the conscience of all sensible men. Contrary beliefs and practices to this are not religious but irreligious and scandalous and their advocacy can not be justified as guaranteed by any indulged scandalous conception of supposed Constitutional guarantees.

A civilized and not a barbarous philosophy is the basis of American public opinion and jurisprudence under it and the Constitution in all of its provisions must be interpreted and upheld in conformity with this civilized thought and philosophy and the continued civilization and progress of the American people depends upon their ability to cooperatively maintain their American institutions and liberties under those institutions.

THE DANGER TO AMERICAN INSTITUTIONS THROUGHOUT THE entire Constitution and the first ten amendments the word people is chosen and used in place of citizen. The tenth amendment provides: "The powers not delegated to the United States etc. *** are reserved to the States respectively or to the people," not to the citizens. The entire policy of the Constitution was the establishment of a Federal government composed of States and their people rather than a national one composed of citizens of the whole. Under the Constitution the United States is only national when dealing with foreign powers, declaring war and making peace and in all international affairs. In these matters it possesses, under the Constitution, complete, and not limited, sovereignty and acts, on behalf of its people as a national government. In all of its internal affairs its powers and authority are limited except in a few matters such as establishing post offices and post roads, regulating commerce and navigation, etc. when again its sovereignty is national. 

The present danger to American institutions is in not thoroughly understanding the dual and limited nature of the Federal government and in distortion of the principles and policies of the Constitution to meet the supposed needs of modern trends and ideas. 

Distortion is an evidence of ignorance and weakness. The mind which can not comprehend, distorts. 

It is its only alternative. Every distortion is the forerunner of repudiation. A clever caricature of person or thing is prized for a time but when its novelty wears off it is discarded and soon forgotten. 

If American Constitutional provisions and limitations are distorted they likewise must, sooner or 24

later, be discarded and cast away-exchanged for something far less beneficial. 

It is against this danger that the author desires to caution and to warn-to clarify American thought against its own abuse in the present belief that all which was once good is now worthless. 

Every American understanding and appreciating 

American Constitutionally guaranteed individual liberty, the checks and balances in the government which safeguard that liberty, must be a staunch advocate and supporter of his government and the 

American Union under the Constitution.

District Court Judge Philips in rendering the decision of the District Court of the United States in the case of United States vs. Hannon, 45 Federal

Reporter, page 442, gives with accuracy the meaning and purpose of every organized civil society. 

He says: "The proposition is that a man can do no public wrong who believes that what he does is for the ultimate public good. The underlying vice of all this character of argument is that it leaves out of view the existence of the social compact, and the idea of government by law. If the end sought justifies the means, and there were no arbiter but the individual conscience of the actor to determine the fact whether the means are justifiable, homicide, pillage and incontinence might run riot *. Society is organized on the theory, born of the necessities of human well being, that each member yields up something of his natural privileges, predilections and indulgences for the good of the complete community; and he consents to all the motto implies, 'Salus populii suprema est lex;' and, as no government can exist without law, the law-making power within the limits of con-

stitutional authority, must be recognized as the body to prescribe what is right and prohibit what is wrong. It is the very incarnation of the spirit of anarchy for a citizen to proclaim that like the heathen he is a law unto himself."

Like a breath of fresh air upon a warm sultry day came that splendid defense of the Constitution in the typical American utterance of Chief Justice Hughes in rendering the opinion of the Supreme Court in the case of Schechter Corporation vs. 

United States, holding the National Recovery Act unconstitutional. "Extraordinary conditions," observed the Chief 

Justice, "may call for extraordinary remedies. But the argument necessarily stops short of an attempt to justify action which lies outside the sphere of constitutional authority. Extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power." "Kingdoms have edicts, cities their charters; 

Even the bold outlaw, in his forest walk, Keeps yet some touch of civil discipline;

For not since Adam wore his verdant apron, Hath man with man in social union dwelt, But laws were made to draw that union closer." says the poet, and the people of every government of the people, especially one like the Constitutional government of the United States-designed to secure the greatest possible liberty and freedom to the people-must respect and support its fundamental laws and institutions and not permit themselves to be misled into foolish changes through either dangerous and unnecessary amendments of the organic law or strained judicial interpretation and construc-

tion of that law, to meet the supposed requirements of changing popular demand of every moment. 

In a government of the people the people must see that public opinion bends and gives way to their Constitution in order to preserve it. The bending of the Constitution to meet the will of first one majority and another will only destroy the Constitution, stability, prosperity and good government. 

Besides often times the voice of a noisy minority is heard above that of a retiring and intelligent majority and is so mistaken for the latter.

Constitutional Convention ***. No, I mean some thing more even than that. I mean the Constitutional system of the United States as it works." "Our Constitutional System is not only what the judges say it is, but what the amending power can make it, and what usage will make it. Today 

I want to lay before you the suggestion that what we have to avoid is changing our whole system just by usage-by usurpation, by the necessity of twisting a document into distortion." 

Only through proper observance of its original purpose and objective can the Constitution, and our

liberty and progress under it, be preserved and maintained. It was the result of the labors of unselfish conscientious pioneers, strong in mind and body, who sought to establish and maintain the principles of true democracy in government and to safeguard these principles against attack from both within and from without.

The founders were not technically learned men nor were they skilled in the refinements of criptic thought though, in both mind and character, they were the best educated persons known to human history. Their education was truly, "An evolution from within and not a revolution from without," as genuine education has been accurately defined and described. In the Constitution they gave mankind the nearest approach towards perfection in government and afforded the people the least possible restraint consistent with law and order that could, or ever can, be afforded them.

Unless the American people are disposed to let themselves be robbed of their liberties by some form of international collectivism, brought about after the most devastating war ever known to the history of man, they must learn to understand and properly maintain the principles and purposes of their Constitution and not be misled or deceived by the artfulness of adroit propagandists. 

True humanity, like true charity, "begins at home" neither can be practiced in the American way under any form of government but that of the American Union as designed and established by the Constitution.
