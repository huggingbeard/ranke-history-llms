Faith, bias and temperament, then, have a way of interfering with training, precisely because one of the great purposes of training is to protect us against the illusions of faith, bias and temperament. For a critic to adopt Robertson's suggestion serves not only as a caution to the reader of the critic; it helps the critic himself, first of all. The critic begins with increasing knowledge of himself, which is the beginning of criticism.

In adopting, more or less modified because of space restrictions, the Robertson suggestion, I am not yielding to a passion for autobiography. Nor shall I, as may appear, be talking merely about myself. If the critic has a certain obligation toward the reader, the reader has no less an obligation toward the critic. If the critic explains himself, in Robertsonian manner, to the reader, the reader should, as it were, explain himself to the critic. He should become more consciously aware of his own faiths and biases and temperament and training. In fact, to read or to hear with discernment is to be oneself a critic. 

The reader, like the critic, should ultimately be able to say to himself, "This is good, but I do not like it. . . I like this, but it is not good."

And just as the scientist should be on his guard against the acceptance of a single, final, absolute Truth, so should the critic be on his guard against the acceptance of a single, final, absolute formula for Beauty.

In tracing my own background as a critic I seek as much to give the reader the opportunity to compare his own experience with mine as to suggest the forces behind the formulation of my flexible standards. Those standards are flexible as, let us say, the bamboo is flexible. They have deep roots, yet they bend rather than break; they bend, moreover, in conformity with the laws of their nature, not at the caprice of doctrinal winds. In the earthquakes to which Japan is subject it has been discovered that the flexibility of the bamboo provides better protection than the use, for housing, of more brittle material. This is not expediency on my part; it is rather the readiness of a fundamentally skeptical [but not always cynical] nature to make new adjustments in the light of new knowledge. 

Early it was forced upon my attention that most scientific

Isaac Goldberg men lack sadly the attributes of the artist, and that most literary and artistic men lack sadly the attributes of the scientist. My own mind-such as it is in its capacity as critic- 

I consider a fundamentally scientific instrument engaged chiefly in the arts. This in itself may represent a reaction against certain effeminizing influences in those arts, and may be one of my major self-deceptions. Let us follow the partial, and inevitably imperfect, schematic report of that mind upon itself. 

2. THOUGHT AS FRUSTRATION 

Long before I had ever read anything to suggest the notion, it had occurred to me, in such terms as a child will use for its own thoughts, that the first thinkers must have been the sick men of the race. This was simply because I had not noticed, in myself, anything like thought until I fell sick in my 11th or 12th year with a serious illness. Until then I was the normal child, fond of food and physical activity. I was built thin and could out-run most of my playmates. The games we played often carried us, during a single summer's night, from the depths of Boston's West End to the furthermost fastness of the North End, along the wharves of Atlantic avenue into the densely populated Italian district. Over this district still presides the church that carried the signal lanterns for Paul Revere.

On one of these wild chases I had caught a cold; it was neglected, and before I knew it I was being examined by a couple of dubious doctors. Now it was a simple cold; now it was pneumonia; now it was typhoid; again it was pleurisy. By the time the medicos had made up their minds it had become an undoubted case of empyema. An operation was called for. We were too poor to afford a hospital; besides, in this day, the sound of the word "hospital" in the slums was ominous. The surgeons practiced on the poor; they herded them; and a host of other such legends. I was operated upon at home, in the "front" room. Three things I still recall of that operation performed 

Over 35 years ago: the thrusting of a long needle between ray ribs, without the administration of ether; the cone-shaped towel by which the ether was administered, while I fought the three assembled physicians madly; waking out of the sleep and seeing the blurred eyes of my mother. The blur was in the eyes of us both, for different reasons.

Recovery was slow; for half a year, in fact, I went about with a tube inserted at the point where half a rib had been sawed away. The wound was not closed until reports from the bacteriological laboratory declared it safe-against tubercular infection to do so. The constant dressing of the wound, the spraying that left me for a moment breathless, the sudden awareness that one had a body, the need to be careful, the long period in bed before I could visit the doctor to have these ser-

Dictatorship Over the Intellect vices performed-these experiences effected a radical alteration in my being. I believe-in fact, I know-that even contemporary parents, for all the contributions of modern psychology, have a very imperfect notion of how adult the thoughts of a youngster can be.

Thought began with me as a result of frustrated muscular activity. Day after day I lay, then sat, in my bed, wondering about everything. I had watched my parents crying over my illness. I could tell when they had been concealing their own fears, from me and from each other. I very quickly learned to scan the doctors' faces for hidden verdicts. Instinctively, when they used words that I could not understand, I began to be uneasy. Convalescence was like a second childhood before my first was over, both as to the renewed timorousness and to the pleasures of being served without any personal effort. 

My interest in medicine dates from this experience; it is an interest that began as fear, graduating then to speculation and wonder. It is an interest that is still tinged with hypochondriac suggestion.

For several years after I was allowed to return to school I became short of breath with the slightest exertion. From champion runner I had been demoted to the rank of slowest walker. Childhood is not very chivalrous. If a quarrel arose among us, my opponent would be advised, by his gang to "soak him on the right side." They knew that this was my very vulnerable spot, and that even a glancing blow would lay me out.

In my confused way I was led to bitter reflections upon man's fairness to man. I boiled with the desire to fight back; I boiled even more with the foreknowledge that I musn't—that I could not, because the effort would wind me, and I could so easily be knocked out by a blow on that deep cut. 

From that day to this I have been an expert in the avoidance of unnecessary physical labor. This is not a euphemism for saying that I grew lazy. Far from it. I am, at the things that claim my interest, a tremendous toiler. No worse punishment could be visited upon me than enforced idleness. Certainly, however, I cannot pretend to like physical exercise. It is for others, but not for me. At the time when youngsters begin to feel their mettle-to experiment with cigars and drinks-to pick up girls on the streets-I was still too busy trying to keep a body from under-exerting itself and a mind from running away with its owner. That Latin saw about the sound body in the sound mind makes a music, to my ears, like Utopia. Regardless of the uses to which my mind has been put, it overbalances, in my mechanism, the body. It is much too late to change that imbalance.

For the rest, much of what has been said about the dignity and the virtue of labor strikes me as making a virtue out of necessity. Hard toil is more often degrading than it is dignified. The purpose of the proletarian revolt is to mitigate

Isaac Goldberg it, not to glorify it in a halo of romance. High purpose may confer dignity upon toil, but it is part of that high purpose to make such physical toil unnecessary.

The range of the. mind, compared with the range of the body, is illimitable. I have always had a vivid imagination, both for fears and for pleasures. But-and this may strike some of my readers as an affectation-I cannot remember the time when I really harbored the usual illusions. Perhaps as a protection against the deceits of hope I have never allowed myself to expect unreasonable things. I should, if labels are necessary, set myself down on the whole as having been a realistic child, with an intuitive feeling that fantasy is a part of reality. 

Thus I was early-and unjustly-scorned for lacking enthusiasm. I still am. What was meant was that I could not give myself up to an orgy of unreasonable expectations. In a cold, paradoxical way I happen to be very enthusiastic. I do, how- ´ever, dislike public display of emotion. 

My senses from as far back as I can remember, have been very acute. Perhaps my vast irritability as a child was partly sourced in this peculiarity. Certainly I was cursed-so I was later told-with a violent temper. I had become, quite naturally, a spoiled youngster. This was another thing that a prolonged convalescence had done for me. For a while I wore my experience as a vainglorious soldier wears his badge of glory. 

I became a little actor, strutting his role. Yet all the time I knew I was doing this, and the pleasure of the strutting was quickly diminished by the self-knowledge. It is said that the sense of humor is little other than a sense of proportion. Another way of saying the same thing, perhaps, is to root a sense of humor in self-knowledge.

In a child, this tendency toward introspection and selfknowledge, coupled with a dislike of public display of emotion, suggests the emergence of a critical nature. The Socratic injunction to "Know Thyself" is not, when strictly carried out, an unmixed blessing. In one way or another we pay a price for self-knowledge. We pay in spontaneity; we pay in self-esteem; we pay in the capacity for unreflective enjoyment. To know one's self-really to try to know one's self-is a discipline that can become pitiless. It may become a species of spiritual self-devouring. Diogenes, before setting forth with his lantern, should have bethought himself to look into a mirror, and then into his own eyes. If the sense of humor is rooted in selfknowledge, so is the sense of tragedy. Both are, among other things, studies in proportion-man's measurement of himself against the possibilities of life and the inevitability of death. 

If I did not wish to appear too paradoxical I would say that the sense of humor and the sense of tragedy are two aspects of a single realization-ultimate futility, ultimate meaninglessness. 

To recognize the ultimate meaninglessness of life is not therefore to be a pessimist. Such a recognition is not a goal to

Dictatorship Over the Intellect achieve; it is a stage through which to pass on the way to endowing life with the best meaning that it can be made to have for us. It is as if man, in his need for pleasure, must find it even in the philosophical contemplation of his pain. What else, indeed, is at the bottom of our enjoyment of great comedy and great tragedy? Memento mori-Remember That Thou Shalt

Die yields to amor fati, the acceptance of one's fate. Before comedy we say to ourselves, "what an amiable fool I am!" Before tragedy, "How noble am I in my struggle against odds that in the end must leave me defeated!" And, strangely enough, in this self-understanding lies a deep measure of solace and even comfort.

I spoke of the acuteness of my senses. To this day my sense of smell, aided by a very generous nose, detects odors in food, let us say, that make me appear sometimes as a crummy dyspeptic. I am forever smelling gas-leaks where none apparently exists. In the atmosphere, I am sensitive to differences of odor or pressure or temperature that more fortunate human beings fail to register. . . . I dislike, with the inevitable exceptions, being touched, shaking hands, rubbing against crowds.

My eyes, which have done an enormous amount of reading in many languages, as yet do not require the aid of glasses for books; I have just begun to use them for distance. They are extremely sensitive, however, to intensities and directions and changes of light. I can be mildly nauseated, for example, by a sudden change-as by the passing of thick clouds across the sun-from light to darkness. I have a very vivid color-memory. 

Keenest of my senses, however, is that of hearing. I began to read very soon, and, from the first, I have always heard as well as seen what I read. 

3. INTERNATIONALE 

This acuteness of hearing argued for suspectibility to and fondness for music. It may be the self-solace of one who, by circumstance, was shunted from music to letters, but I consider myself slightly more the musician-in the larger sense, and certainly not the narow one of a performer upon an instrument ---than the literary man. This, strangely enough, to me has a direct bearing upon my later development towards the ideal of intellectual independence.

Music, as has often been said without understanding the deeper implications of the remark, is the "purest," the least "representative," the most "immediate" of the arts. One can see color in painting; one can see-though one should also hear, if possible-words in books; that syntax of sound, that plastic organization of tone which we call music, demands a more concentrated type of attention. It lends itself less easily to irrelevancies. Outside of imitation, such as the drums of a parade, the call of a bird, the whispering of leaves, and such

Isaac Goldberg really non-musical procedures, it is difficult to use musicwithout the aid of words-for transmitting emotions that have no essential relationship to the art. The composer is, perhaps, potentially the most free of creative artists; he is less ringed about by the necessities of a particular epoch, language or attitude. When I speak of freedom, it should be remembered, it is with a distinct feeling of its relativity under all conditions. 

This sense of hearing has a direct bearing, for me, upon other phases of an outlook that seemed inevitably to head in the direction of internationalism. It was early associated with my interest in languages.

That interest, to be sure, was awakened almost at the moment that I became conscious of the use of words. This is a very common experience among Jewish children, for, in their households, they hear constantly the employment of two or three tongues. [I must add that this was much more true when I was a child than it is in this country today.] There is the idiom of the nation; in my case, as a Jew of American birth [in Boston], English; there is the folk-vernacular, Yiddish-a speech set down in Hebrew characters, but fundamentally in structure a German dialect; there is the language of prayer, Hebrew. 

Such a heritage, already, is an internationale in miniature. 

One of the sad beauties of having been born a Jew is that, wherever one may travel one discovers a strip of fatherland. 

Russia? One's parents came from there; one's grandparents were persecuted there; one's unknown relatives have found, under the U. S. S. R., a new linguistic freedom, a new economic hope, on what is to many a freshly consecrated soil. Germany?

The home-tongue of one's father and mother derives from it; other relatives have known persecution under Bismarck, under Hitler. Spain? It is the same story, repeated with melancholy variations. If patriotism is anything but glorified jingoism, the 

Jew is born, as it were, a patriot of the world. From such a point of view, whatever the practical necessities of an unfortunate era, Zionism is a step backward toward nationalism. 

But I wander from the point. To a sensitive child such tri-lingual surroundings are a valuable experience. Difference in languages to him is, from the first, not the invention of a high-school principal with demoniac schemes for torturing the young. He absorbs, from life itself and not through books, through experience and not through rule or rote, a feeling for grammatical and lexical distinctiveness, for idiomatic locution, for the very spirit of language. He notices similarities in roots, he ponders upon the varying effectiveness of methods. He wonders why prayers in Hebrew, as recited by men in the synagogue, should sound so differently when recited, in Yiddish, by the women at home. Thereafter Hebrew will be for him a masculine tongue, and Yiddish, a feminine. Later, indeed, he will learn that such, historically, was the case—that women, not being required to know the tongue called Holy, read their prayers

in the humble vernacular, which a man was too proud to use for sacred purposes, since such use would stamp him as being too ignorant to manage the supposedly ancestral tongue. 

Later still he will realize that the humbleness of the vernacular derives largely from the low economic status of those who use it. Yiddish has remained distinctly a "proletarian" language, while Hebrew will never lose its overtones of religious majesty and aristocracy. Quite aside from its marvelous richness, the English language today exercises a rulership that is in direct ratio to the economic importance of the peoples who use it.

Most Jewish children, then, grow up more or less language-minded.

In my own case, this internationality was very shortly supplemented by another influence. As a youngster I lived in a neighborhood-let us call it by its proper name, slum-that was almost in equal parts Irish and Jewish, with a scattered percentage of Poles and Italians, and, in our grammar-school district, a small colony of Negroes. Children are in many respects so much more sensible than their parents-so much more sensible, alas, than they themselves will be after they have been "educated" by priest and rabbi and teacher, and have grown to man's estate. In those days we knew nothing of boundary lines between nation and nation; of creeds that severed mankind instead of uniting it. These things we had to be told; had to have drummed into us. We could curse in five languages-we slept at each other's homes-we experimented with each other's national dishes.

In later years, some of us began to hear about the superiority of one tongue to another-an unscientific attitude, at best, which is shared by more than one teacher of languages; and about the inferiority of one race to another-scientists are not at all agreed upon the very concept of race. Some of us were saved by our childhood memories from capitulating to these facile prejudices. Most of us, I am sorry to say, were not. We had become "responsible" members of our respective communities—a process that too frequently connotes confirmation in the tribal totems and tabus. 

To the internationality of my inheritance, then, was added an internationality of environment. Words, to me-words and the structures of words-are, and always have been, among the miracles of the universe. As fleeting as the breath that shapes them and warms them with meaning, they yet remain as perdurable as the emotions that first sought for them a healing utterance. But then, to all but those unimaginative souls who seeks wonders only in the past and only in so-called holy writings, is not everything a miracle?

One's mind is often convinced long before one's body has given consent. Phrased differently, the conflict has come down in Pascal's famous dictum, "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know." And to flout the reasons of the heartto attempt to ride, like a Juggernaut, over the claims of the body, is a species of inverted sentimentalism; one might call it, indeed, with a slightly derogative twist, mentalism. 

It may appear contradictory that in a book dedicated to the freedom of the mind I should defend the claims of the body, or the heart, or whatever you choose to call the irrational urge within us. That irrational urge may nevertheless be some unintegrated portion of rationality. Body and mind, after all, are not separate entities; they are forces of which our beliefs and our acts are the resultants. That so many of us should have become radicals because, forsooth, we want a more equitable share of the world's goods-because we like physical comfort-because we loathe the bodily and mental disease bred by slum-life-because we hate hard toil and look forward to more leisure and more pleasure-because, in a word, we wish to have an easier and a happier time of it, by no means lowers the status of the idealism that has grown upon that soil. 

I am suspicious of all idealism that has no well-defined material basis. Ethics no more exists in a void or a vacuum than does esthetics. Structures do not grow from the roof downward. Neither, in an enthusiasm for building that results in a fixation, should they remain all foundation. Foundations are for building upon; they are not the structure itself. 

Temperament, then, is a valid foundation for liberalism or radicalism. Long before I was a radical mentally, I was a radical temperamentally. When, very soon, I became impatient with religion, it was not a mental action at all; it was a reaction. So, too, was my emergent attitude toward sex. So, too, as the last of the series, would be my attitude toward the problems of politics, economics, and international idealism. 

All these steps involved a new freedom-the shaking off of superstitions-the rejection of old responsibilities and the acceptance of new. At first, however, they were as physical in nature as the blinking of my eyelids when a mote lodged under them as the dilatation of my pupils in the dark-as the

shrinking of my hand from a blaze. Despite indoctrination, I reacted to these complex stimuli mostly as a body, not as a mind. Later, like most minds, mine was able to discover plenty of reasons for what my body liked, and plenty of reasons against what it did not like. This is not always a meaner use of mind. Not so mean, for example, as compelling itself to discover reasons for liking what the body manifestly does not like, or for concealing from itself unpleasant truths. 

Never truly religious, I definitely rejected religion for the reason that no orthodox Jew was permitted to write on the Sabbath. I liked to write, even in early childhood. I could not understand why, on a Sabbath, I shouldn't. Not then, and not now. So simple a crisis as this lay at the conscious bottom of what became my equally simple, unargumentative Atheism. 

Having dared to ask the first impious question, I found that the rest was easier. Sometimes, when I consider the endless streams of ink, the cosmic eruptions of breath and oratory, that have been devoted to arguing the existence of God, I am stunned by the sheer wasteful stupidity of it all. I have become convinced that service to God, so-called, is a means of evading service to man. Of that, however, elsewhere.

Liberation from the superstitions of sex, with which theology is so poisonously populated, was not so easy a matter. 

Religion, even for the most devout, has an atmosphere of unreality. Sex, even for the most devout, is real-magically, alluringly, inescapably real. 

4. ATHEISM SOCIALISM 

As far back as I can remember, I had found it difficult to reconcile the world of religion with the world of every-day actuality. Or, to put it another way: to accept a state of things in which one lived a double life. I cannot yet, after more than a quarter of a century, understand how people-without laughing or crying—can have a Sunday personality and a week-day personality. Arguments for Freethought may have done much to consolidate my position as an Atheist, just as studies in physiology and psychology would do much to strengthen my conceptions of sexual liberty and the need of controlling that liberty. The reason, however, in these matters lags far behind experience. One rebels against such silly prohibitions as that against writing, or any other simple activity, on the Sabbath; one is made dubious by coming upon the editor of a Jewish weekly eating forbidden ham; one hears, even as a kid, about a real-estate speculator who is a high figure in the synagogue, yet has driven a man to suicide by his mercenary tactics; one feels, rather than reasons, about the perfunctory manner in which prayers are rushed off as rapidly as on any Tibetan prayer-wheel; religiousness, in fact, has begun to die as soon

Isaac Goldberg 

13 as it becomes a recognized routine. It is something for seven days a week, or else it is nothing. 

For me, and precisely because I took, in serious manner, the ethical accompaniments of this routine, religion became nothing. If I did not wish, again, to appear too paradoxical, I might say that I became an Atheist because I was too religious-in a scientific sense-to be a worshiper. However, what I am trying to impress is this: long before we are reasoned out of a belief, or into it, we have been emotionalized out of it, or into it. And just as I am inclined to suspect any idealism that does not rest upon a material foundation, so am I inclined to suspect a reason that is too decisively divorced from emotion. Of course I know that such divisions as intellect and emotion are more or less arbitrary; nevertheless, it is as important to feel right about a thing as to think right about it. 

More important, perhaps. Once the emotions have become fixed, they are stubborn customers to reason with.

A gamin brought up in the slums, if his eyes and ears are normal, learns as much out of school as in. It may be years before he realizes to the full what he has learned, but in later life he will not have to theorize about conditions, or accept descriptions from the outside.

Long before we kids were initiated into sex, we had a practical, unformulated but vivid experience of it that later made us laugh at our so much older would-be instructors. In back alleys, in cellars, in coal-bins, on door-steps sheltered by the dark, we had come upon scenes that needed no translation into the evasive Latinity and Greek of professional sexologists. 

We knew very well what went on in Bertha's brothel, and why she herself sat behind closed shutters all night long, making a short sibilant sound to the passers-by. We knew the difference between "fifty-cent houses" and "two-dollar" houses, because on Sunday mornings our newspaper rounte shifted us to the Back Bay. There we not only delivered the heavy Sunday editions but also ran errands for people too lazy to get out of bed, and too indifferent to disguise what they were doing. 

Long before our high-school years we had been introduced to the spectacle of gonorrheic and syphilitic infection, and to that curious pride of the street-corner according to which one could not lay claim to manhood's estate unless one had contracted that first case of gonorrhea. The germ, indeed, was flaunted as a badge of distinction. What is it, amid such surroundings as these, that keeps one child uncontaminated and leads the other astray? What is it that makes a few of the youngsters rebel, instinctively, against the gang-ethics? For unconventionality has conventions of its own, and sometimes it takes a deal of courage to rebel into what passes for conventionality. For myself, I reacted distinctly against such companionship as this. Nor had I failed to notice that some of the gang-leaders were regular attendants at church or syna-

gogue. This, of itself, is not advanced as an argument against ecclesiastical institutions; societies are not to blame for the character of their associates. It is simply that inconsistencies were forcing themselves upon my attention. 

I cannot see that my acquaintance with these anti-social creatures did me any harm. At this stage, too, I devoured every dime and nickel novel that came my way. I remember how ardently my teachers inveighed against these illiterate, corruptive dissipations. All they did to me was to teach me a number of "big" words that came to the assistance of my vocabulary, and that I afterward incorporated into my school work; in this way I got better marks in English. When 

I recall what a terrible thing it was to be caught with one of these periodicals in one's hands; how some of the children were thrashed by their parents for reading the Pluck And Luck series, or the Old Cap Collier, or Nick Carter, I think of the up-to-date gangster movie and laugh wryly. Could our most vivid imaginations have conjured up the dash and fire of contemporary celluloid?

What I am trying to get at is this: it is difficult to establish a direct relationship between educative or emotional cause and effect. Columbus sets sail for India and discovers 

America. Saul sets forth to find his father's asses and comes upon a kingdom. The minister, in the fervor of training his son to God, finds that he has fathered an infidel. The Communist's son may exhibit an atavistic passion for the religion of his ancestors.

A group of us were initiated into Socialism by one of the worst of the anti-social elements in the neighborhood. To look at him, to hear the sound of his voice, one would have set him down as a corruptor of youth. I am afraid that some of our parents never ceased from regarding him that way. 

My first sight of him was all but nauseating. He had been playing in a crap game. There had been a disagreement. He was accused of cheating, then of lying. Now, you may accuse a man of stealing your wife, of defrauding through the mails, of stealing from widows and orphans. At most, he will sue you for defamation of character. But intimate that he has cheated in a crap game or lied, and nothing but pistols for two will wipe out the insult.

At once a free-for-all broke out. Ben the Socialist received one of the worst drubbings I have ever witnessed. When the gang was through with him he was bleeding profusely from his long nose; lying in the gutter, half-blinded by punches, groaning and cursing. I got him a towel and water. It was in this way that I was first introduced to the concept of socialization.

Ben had traveled all over the world in cattle-boats. He had picked up a deal of miscellaneous lore. And somewhere in his wanderings-he was still a very young man-he had

15 come into contact with the economic doctrines of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels.

To our confused and not even half-understanding ears it sounded like a new evangel. It was a window opening upon a new world. It fed our resentments against poverty, our inchoate dissatisfactions with the world, our rising spirit of rebellion. It crystallized the meaning of our experiences. By this time we had gone more than half-way through high school. 

Almost to the end of my high school days I was, in my youthful way, an anti-woman-suffragist. Why? Because one of my teachers had once said to me, "There's only one thing a woman can do that a man can't do: have babies. That's her business in the world." The argument had seemed unanswerable. 

That was not important, then. We had begun to ask questions. 

The later high-school years, for me, were a queer balancing of my teachers' opinions against the unorthodoxies of Ben. 

It was not long before I had read myself far beyond Ben's tutelage in Socialist doctrine. We have lived to become heartily ashamed of each other; he, of the radicalism for which he used to reproach me; I, of the man who had so smugly settled into "bourgeois" rectitude and, not content with that, had begun to preach against the non-conformity of youth. 

5. BREAKDOWN BUILD-UP 

Between the time when I was graduated from college, or, rather, between the time when I received my post-graduate degrees and found a place for myself in the literary and musical world, I underwent that valuable educational experience called a nervous breakdown.

I have read and listened to very much about nervous breakdowns. Let me assure you, at once, that I do not know all about them. Let me assure you, also, that in my unprofessional opinion the doctors do not know all about them. And, at the utter risk of being blasted for a backslider, may I suggest that this common phenomenon has a "spiritual" quality as well as a physical.

One hears it reiterated that overwork never led to a breakdown of this sort. This, I cannot believe. Whether overwork is a cause, or merely a concomitant, I cannot say. Certainly it makes wonderful soil for the condition to flourish in. I had worn myself out doing at least seven years' work in five. College is expensive, even to the proletarian sons who make their way through with scholarships. The sooner out, the better, so that returns upon the investment may begin to come in. This is a sad way to look upon learning, but one lives in this world and in no other. 

My mind, however, had been shaken no less than my body. 

At college I was receiving the fruits of bourgeois culture, and let me say at once that this is a real, a valuable, a permanent

culture at which low-grade Communists sneer in vain. Lowgrade Communists. . . . For Lenin knew better, and wrote better. Every civilization produces its contributions to the universal fund of knowledge. Learning, like life itself, is a continuum, and though we reject much in the light of new knowledge-witness how much, already, the Communists in the U. S. S. R. have rejected of their own tactics-there is much that we retain in the light of old knowledge. . . . Outside of college I was being drenched in another culture, that of "scientific" Socialism. It was a valuable discipline. It balanced the tacit capitalistic bias of my college training. 

I say tacit because, in college, I never had the feeling of being coerced in my views. My Socialism was publicly known at Harvard from the first day that I entered as a freshman. 

It was a matter of newspaper record. Nevertheless I regularly was granted scholarships every year, won prizes in addition, was graduated with highest honors. Had I desired to go into teaching, I might have-I surely would have found plenty of difficulty. Many years later, however, I was to teach at 

Harvard itself.

I feel no indignation to this day when a college refuses to employ a radical. I feel somewhat melancholy for human nature, that is all. I knew what I was in for; I could not expect those hostile to my economic beliefs to employ me if they feared that I would be a "dangerous" influence. Educational institutions-as institutions-reflect the dominant economic interests. Not so much as the churches do, and often with a paradoxical passion for free speech and the free mind that does them credit.

My breakdown, as I see it now, was greatly aggravated by the paradox in my own life. By the conflict between old ideas and new. By an internal tug-of-war, in which expediency fought against some demon of honesty-with-self, yielding at last fully to that demon or deity. I could not understand then, at 20, as I understand now, how differences in opinion, even on minor points, can rouse the primal beast in man. In this respect radicals are quite as bad as conservatives. 

My breakdown was, in part, just such a conflict, in terms of a civil war. In other language, it was a disintegration, preparatory to a new integration. The physical experience of my childhood ailment had found, at last, a counterpart in the world of ideas. I believe that such breakdowns leave as permanent a scar upon one's mental outlook as any wound inherited from a major operation. I am no more the same in ideas, since that time, than I was the same in body after that little adventure with shifting diagnoses and empyema. 

That breakdown was the first phase of a build-up. It must have been other things, too, for the physical and mental symptoms were distressing in the utmost degree, even though they did not confine me to bed or keep me indoors. But a build-up,

Isaac Goldberg 17 a re-building-up-this, too, most assuredly it was. A religious person would call such an experience a "conversion," and speak of the new principle of integration as god. 

This breakdown-build-up was a final self-confirmation of my radicalism. And, lest there be any misunderstanding, let me add that radicalism refers as much to one's attitude toward problems as to one's notions as to the most efficacious ways of introducing fundamental change. One may be, inside the dictionary and out, a radical without favoring violence. I abhor violence, both for its physical dangers and for the regressive, savage nature of the reaction that it breeds. 

But, as I have said, violence is usually thrust upon the violated, and the crowning irony is that the violated are made to appear as the aggressors.

In after-years, when equally smug doctrinaires would look at me and assure me that no college man-meaning mecould feel sympathy with the proletariat, I would look back quizzically. These facile generalizations! Many of these men held good positions, and were far better off than I. I doubt that they had ever felt the pangs of hunger, as some of us others had known them in childhood; I doubt that they had ever peddled newspapers through New England's blizzards or in its merciless, humid heat; I doubt, in fact, that they knew what proletarian meant, particularly its sad etymology. Their twisted logic ran somewhat like this: Rich men's sons go to college, therefore every son who goes to college has the class sympathies of a rich man. I am a worker; I did not go to college; therefore anyone who goes to college cannot sympathize with the problems of the workers. 

I remained a radical despite such radicals as these, and surely not because of them.

I am afraid that, even as an official editor, I was not a model radical. There is little of the doctrinaire in me. To say it in sacred language, I believe that the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. To translate into secular terms, I believe that Socialism and Communism, or any other heterodoxy that looks toward freedom and equitability, were made for man and not man for them. Something in me resists absolutism in any form. It was long, indeed, before I reconciled myself to the inevitability of natural law. If this sounds foolish, I must set it down in the interests of a more complete account.

I am suspicious of all unitary, totalitarian, absolutist action or thought. Particularly thought. Often we must act as if we alone were in possession of the ultimate truth. Otherwise, many things would never get done. To think, however, to believe, in all passionate sincerity that our solution is the only one possible, especially when dealing with complex social problems, is a little too much like deputizing for a God in which we have ceased, presumably, to believe. It may be said

that such intensity of belief makes for intensity and heightened effectiveness of action. It may make also for lowered effectiveness through fanatical impatience.

And so we return to Mr. Robertson and his faith, bias, temperament and training. 

6. COLLECTIVISM 

In the theological sense I have no faith, and have lacked one for so long that it requires on my part a special effort to conceive of an orthodox, church-going, devout communicant. 

I do not question the sincerity of such persons, though I do deplore their intellectual limitations. If they really mean what they say when they use the word tolerance or toleration, I suggest that they do not question the sincerity of such unbelievers as myself. Those religions or religious groups who have risen to the noble implications of tolerance seem to be agreed that it applies to their reciprocal relations, but ceases to be an obligation when they severally confront an Agnostic or an Atheist. In that case they are hardly tolerant at all; they are smug. Tolerance, however, surely does not mean the acceptance of that which one tolerates. It is therefore not contradictory to use, against that which we may tolerate, all the weapons of reason and enlightened emotions, if we happen to find it in the way of what we conceive to be progress. 

I am not a "militant" Atheist. By this I mean that the crusading spirit does not burn very brightly in my soul. There are times, none the less, when, under attack from this church or that, the Atheist may do excellent work for both the gayety and the enlightenment of the nations. For there can be little doubt that, whatever religion may seem to this individual or that-whatever it may have stood for in the history of what we are pleased to call civilization-it has become a vested secular, economic interest.

It is not an accident that free-thinking has gone hand in hand with liberal and radical economics. Church and State, in the beginning, were one. Even today, when supposedly in countries like the United States they are separated, the influence of the Church appears in laws and customs, in the antagonism (happily lessening under the pressure of economic necessity) to birth control, in censorship-drives and other antiliberal manifestations.

I believe that the Church as an institution has outlived its day, that slowly it is decaying through socio-economic process. And, to be perfectly frank, I prefer to have any "militant" fighting against it done by those whom Nature has made into fighters. The implications of every social and individual advance are against the Church. In such revolutions as that in 

Russia, which abolished the Church as part of an economic program, I recognize-quite apart from details-a logical act.

Isaac Goldberg 19 If the Church could abolish the U. S. S. R., it would. I recognize, too, that the seemingly laissez-faire attitude on the part of men like myself, as regards the Church, has its dangers for freedom; it is a relaxation of eternal vigilance. One cannot, however, be alert on all fronts at the same time; here, as in all other human pursuits, there is a division of labor. 

At this point it is usual for some one to ask, "What? Have you no sense of awe and mystery in the face of Nature?" Of course I have. And that is one of the later reasons why any 

Church failed to answer my intellectual or emotional needs. 

That sense of awe, of mystery, of enthralment, of ecstasy, of impenetrable enigma I find fully nourished by science and art. 

And I find it insulted and frustrated at every step by theology. 

It is one of the humorous assumptions of the Church that it answers this need of man for a statement of his relationship to the universe. The Church, in practice, obstructs man's questioning; it obscures his answers. It "solves" an unsolvable mystery with a trick-box of miracles and fairytales. It imposes a book of answers that caricatures man's vital sense of curiosity. It does not, in a word, nourish this need of man; it violates that need; it anesthetizes it; it is an opiate, not-like science and art-an elixir. 

I am utterly out of sympathy with the contemporary tendency among some scientists to manage a reconciliation between science and religion. Unless the meanings of the words are twisted out of their respective orbits, such a reconciliation can be effected only if one side covertly or overtly surrenders its foundations. Religion, as instituted and developed by the 

Church, is a catalogue of superstitious, of puerile interpretations, of anti-science and fanaticism. Insofar as any person, however otherwise intelligent, subscribes to the dogmas of any Church, he ceases in this respect to be intelligent. Flirtation of science and religion at this late period in the world's history can lead only to bastard progeny.

My bias, then, is away from unquestioning adherence. 

For the esoteric divagations of theology I have an amused, even an amazed, pity. As for the distinction between religion and theology, it has its importance; in practical church life, however, it exists only in the dictionary of some persons' minds. 

Take theology away from religion and you strike the Church in its most vital spot.

By demanding an adherence that asks no questions, any institution issues a challenge to man's freedom. Freedom is not a birthright; it is an achievement and a constant reachievement. We are all of us, even the most independent, forever under the temptation of sinking into the restful anonymity of the crowd. The crowd, indeed, is an integral part of us. Without it, without the social configuration, we possess little, if any, meaning. Emerging from it, the individual challenges not only his background but the deeply-rooted crowd-

instincts in himself. Individuation, like freedom, is an achievement and a constant re-achievement. For it is a freedom within a freedom. This we shall have occasion to recall. 

The Church, like all other absolutist institutions, must weaken through the very fiat of its own over-insistence. He who, like Ibsen's Brand, demands all or nothing, in the end comes away with nothing.

My bias against unquestioning adherence has led me into suspicion of all rigid systems, regardless of the ism under which they happen to be subsumed. Mental allegiance has its dangers outside of theologies. A small spot in our minds should be left secure against conquest by even the most convincing, the most armor-proof, logic. Once we have committed ourselves to a belief or a rigid system we naturally develop a sight specially acute for those data which support our ism and a correlative blindness to such data as would tend to undermine it.

Fanaticism, alas, is so human a characteristic that it dwells outside the cloister as well as within and may corrupt the scientist and the philosopher as well as the theologian. 

Every ism breeds its fanatics. Nevertheless, if I had to choose among fanatics, I would favor the scientific or the philosophic type, for science and philosophy contain within themselves the antidote for these aberrations. 

In science and art, then, I find everything that religion pretends to satisfy, and through them I am rid of theological impedimenta. No limits are set to the all-powerful curiosity of man; no limits are set to the expression of that curiosity and wonder. One works forward to ever-renewed experience, not backward to static assumptions. I know that this freedom of art and science is sometimes more theoretical than real; indeed, this monograph was conceived as a protest against the intrusion, into science and art, of theological regimentation, whether that theological spirit was born in the Church or out of it. It is, as the sub-heading states, an affirmation of freedom in the arts especially, since they happen to be my province. 

Yet I must insist that at the bottom of theology is mental slavery; at the bottom of science and the arts is mental liberation, emotional expansion.

In economics I find it difficult to place a label upon the resultant of my beliefs. This is not evasion; it is an attempt to be clear. My direction, I trust, is unmistakable. I have referred to it before as leftward and forward. It is doubtless 

Marxian in origin and in general tendency, without accepting Karl Marx as a king who can do, or prophesy, no wrong, and without acknowledging the infallibility of a Lenin or a Stalin. 

I repudiated religion before I was out of grammar school because it was, so to speak, a Capitalism of the spirit. Capitalism I repudiated, before I was out of high school, because it was a veritable religion of private profit. There was, as I

Isaac Goldberg 

21 once wrote in reviewing the novel, Elmer Gantry, by Sinclair 

Lewis, that Great God, Business, and that Great Business, God.

So I became an Atheist in economics, too. 

Socialist and Communist are terms that suggest themselves as a description. Without being now definitely one or the other, I am a good deal of both. Every vote I cast since I became of voting age was for the Socialist ticket. On the matter of violence I find myself in a paradoxical state. Personally I abhor it; I am a pacifist; I do not consider that I have the right to advocate violence for others as long as I regard it as a reaction toward barbarity and shun it myself. Yet 

I realize only too well that there are historic moments when the violence of the propertied classes [who control the armies and navies, and who use their economic power to keep the expropriated class in subjection] breeds the answer of violence. 

When the worm turns he is beyond thinking of ethical niceties; he has been forced to fight for his very existence. 

Arms, then, which are the ultima ratio regorum-the last argument of kings-may become by historic necessity the ultimate resort of peoples. Birth means travail and blood; even the child, born of love, tears realistically through its mother's entrails, quite unmindful of the romantic poetry that brought its parents together. Revolutions that begin with the printer's ink of manifestoes have a habit of eventuating in the thickness of blood. In governments, as in Newtonian physics, actions breed equal and opposite reactions. Violence responds to violence. 

Circumstance laughs at definitions, and may make fighters of pacifists. Willy nilly. In the long run, nevertheless, the patience of educative methods may achieve as much as the impatience of direct action. Revolutions have a way of retracing their steps; for their actions, too, breed reactions. The general aims of the Russian revolution, if not its methods and certain later developments, enlist my sympathy because it struck a body blow at the institution of Capitalism. It is part, and a vital part, of humanity's advance away from class exploitation, and the evils bred by the private ownership of social necessities-away from rampant anti-social individualism. 

Notice that I distinguish between social and anti-social individualism. This distinction is at the very center of the chapters that follow. 

If names must be used I am content to call myself, in economics, a Collectivist, with both a temperamental and an intellectual resistance to doctrinary enthusiasms. Since no man can be all things, I should-if, again, labels had to be chosen describe myself as an intellectual-a man of thought rather than of action, who protests against such facile divisions as "thought" and "action." As if action may have any individual or social significance without thought behind it! 

As if thought, in a very profound sense, is not a form of ac-

tion! As if the true individual does not rise from, rather than sink into, the collectivity! As if the collectivity were not a pooling of the sum of individual resources, so that out of its pooled power a new, more powerful and intregrated individual may arise! As if the outcome of mankind's struggle toward self-elucidation were a set of robots tagged with the primary descriptions of their functions: "intellectual," "proletarian," "type A," "type Z". . . .

Without the concept of the integrated individual-the man or woman made whole by a personal organization of experiences in a given society-the concept of the collectivity becomes a verbal mysticism. Without the concept of a collectivity in which, for which, and out of which such an integrated individual arises, the concept of the individual becomes a verbal anarchism. Individual and society are not necessarily antitheses, opposites, enemies; they are correlations, harmonies, friends.

The problem of the free intellect rests largely upon the basis of this harmonic conception. And it should be remembered that harmony is not identity; it is, not unison but consonance; not a self-effacing surrender to a common note, but an energizing mergence, so to say, with a common chord. 

Resisting all dictatorship over the intellect—all compulsory programs for the scientist or the artist-one but proclaims, as in the proper place we shall see, the higher functions of the collectivity itself. Isms, to vary the hackneyed theme, were made for man, not man for isms. 

I cannot pretend, in these few pages, to erect a philosophical system. Any "system" that my spirit would favor could not be too rigid, for there is-in my eyes-something soul-less in a rigid consistency. Life transcends logic. It is possible to be too rational. One of the great conquests of the mind is to realize the limitations of pure mentality. All that truly lives has its saving measure of inconsistency. Undeviating consistency is an attribute of the inanimate, and in human beings may easily bring about a sort of living death. 

In a world of relativity freedom itself is relative. All the more, then, should it be on its guard against encroachment, and seek to understand its fuller functions.

II. The Lower Learning 1. NO DEMOTIC ROAD TO LEARNING, EITHER 

"The ignorance of the so-called educated classes," said 

Huxley, "is colossal." I have heard the sentence quoted with the insinuation that its corollary should read, "The knowledge of the so-called illiterate classes is stupendous." The wisest of us, in all conscience, dwell in a state of suspensive ignorance, and the zero line does not draw a neat division between the educated above and the ignorant below. The lower learning is found in the high places; the higher learning is found in the humblest walks. The invention of printing has laid a demotic road to the former. Once even kings were compelled to study; now he who motors may read. The lower learning is thus clearly a product of our contemporary illusion of democracy.

It is so easy, at this point, to go astray that I had better make certain attitudes very clear. 

The crowd, so-called, manages to get along with as low standards as possible. This is a statement of fact, not an expression of snobbery. I am not saying anything against the potentialities of the crowd. I am not using that term, as I shall have to repeat now and then, to indicate an oppressed proletariat. Even in this advanced stage of the world's technical progress, it is the exceptional person who seeks to improve his mind. For the rest, an intellectual law of gravity seems to exert a powerful pull earthward.

The average man is average because in most matters he follows the line of least resistance. Learning, when not directly associated with the needs of making a living, is regarded as a luxury—as a more or less direct attack upon one's scant time for pleasure. It jangles the line of least resistance. Most children naturally dislike school. Most grown-ups naturally dislike learning. The mind, on the whole, is a general nuisance. 

There is another reason for the low standards of the crowd. It does not serve the purposes of any dominant class to have the common people know too much. That is, really know. There is little objection to reading, writing and arithmetic; that makes better and cheaper office help. There is no objection to religion; that makes more subservient employes and more docile spirits. The commoner is victimized at the very start by man's natural indifference to thought, by his low position in society, by his need to make first of all a living. That it is difficult to educate man to his own advantage 23

is shown by the hard time all minority parties have had in so literate a country as the United States. 

It is shown even in the one country of all the world that today is dedicated to the elevation of the proletariat. The dilemma suggests the basic dilemma of the religions. If all men were but kind to one another, say apologists for Christianity, we should not need all these economic systems. Christianity teaches them that kindness. Therefore. . . . Therefore what? If all men were but kind to each other, we should have 

Christianity; it would not need to be taught. In the same way, if the proletariat were but alive to its own interests, it could save itself, and with itself, the world. The proletariat, far from being that romantic people-hero which Communist doctrine makes of it, is a recalcitrant entity. It must be-and is being helped against its will.

This does not say that it must therefore not be helped, or that it does not respond to such assistance. It merely says what I started out to say: that something even in learned people resists the impulsions of the mind-that, beneath the relative stupidity fostered by the dominant class there is a biological and a psychological inertia with which we have to deal. 

We were able to witness a similar inertia in the early days of the U. S. S. R.-an experiment that demands, and demands imperatively, the education of a vast peasantry of illiterates. 

Aspects of this inertia we may still witness in the expression of certain anti-bourgeois prejudices. All educational systems have a certain element of indoctrination, particularly in the teaching of history and economics. Early Soviet education in these subjects made no attempt to disguise the propagandistic element; it went so far, however, that there was the threat of a complete severance between the past and the present; children knew nothing of what had gone before, and were taught to repeat key-words without the historical or geographical data to give them any true meaning. "Bourgeois" became a verbal exoneration from any intellectual duty to the past. The present-day attitude of many 

Communists toward "bourgeois" education-meaning any non- Communist consideration of data-sometimes verges upon the ridiculous. Their own leaders-Marx, Trotsky, Lenin-were much wiser; they did not violate the principles of evolution and seek to create, between one culture and another, an unbridgeable gulf. It may be possible for a predominantly agrarian organization like the Soviet Union to speed up civilization so as to arrive as soon as possible at an industrial status; it will not be quite so possible to ignore the great achievements of bourgeois culture and arrive, at a leap, into something optimistically called a proletarian culture. 

The analogy between economic revolution and cultural revolution is an analogy only, not an identity. We shall have occasion to discuss this in greater detail. For the moment, I

Isaac Goldberg 25 am concerned with what I call the Lower Learning as it exists in the United States. We shall find, in the U. S. S. R., that a lower learning exists there, too, and that, in the fervor of the new vision, it has corrupted even the real minds of the Soviet Republics. To understand the lower learning of the one nation will mean to understand better the lower learning in the other. 

As one illusion leads to another, we may trace the origin of our lower learning to the illusion of culture that is conferred upon the populace by widespread instruction in the rudiments of reading. How does it work out? A literate mob must have something to read. Having the external tools of thought, it must get the feel of thinking itself. As soon as the more imperative problems of living have been met in one fashion or another, the next step in the imitation of the class immediately above is the acquisition of an ornamental education. Just what, matters little. It may be a course in the cosmography of 

Dante, or a history of English literature in 10 lectures, or an inspirational address on the social significance of the modern drama (this last, especially, for some reason which I have yet to fathom, is sure-fire with the ladies); but if it is culture, it is the thing. Far be it from me to make mock of the aspiring, or of those who would guide this eager horde per aspera ad astra. Only I have felt a dismal suspicion, of late, that the guides are too often as unreliable as their customers are insincere; that about the whole performance there is an unpleasant glitter of sham and incompetence; that the taint of uplift has invaded the field; that, to use a homely Russian proverb, for the nth time democracy-or, to be more precise, arithmocracy-has inevitably spat into its own porridge. 

My first serious doubts were aroused when, fresh from college, I sought a position as a high-school teacher in my native city. My adviser, who has since risen to one of the most important positions that can be held in the city's educational life, eyed me with a certain pity, I thought. "We don't want fellows who know anything," he said to me, with a directness that I have always admired. "Do you know whom we want for our high-school teachers? Good mule-drivers! Good muledrivers!" I quote the man without rancor; he paid me the compliment of telling me the truth. It was too early for me then to realize that a college or a high-school is not primarily an educational institution. I still dwelt in the illusion that the literate was also the literary. I was still, in a word, a victim of the lower learning.

An age that is built upon and around commerce-that makes of the high-school a municipal training-school for cheap clerical help-that appraises Time as an element to be outwitted rather than as a dimension to be enjoyed, naturally makes a fetish of speed. So, too, our culture-chaser wants his learning not only condensed, but quick. No time for frills, for hesitant speculation, for adumbration. Give it to him in neat

packages, in laws laid squarely down, in clear-cut outlines. 

Nor is this enough. He must be flattered in his conceit. What he is getting is the real substance; the rest is highbrow. He receives the essence; the rest is vaporizing. 

I state the case, of course, in crude terms. In its simplest form it represents a newly acquired leisure groping outward for a social grace. What chiefly concerns the type I have in mind is, consciously or unconsciously, the acquisition of a veneer. And though this is not necessarily the type held in mind by the assiduous compilers of the anthologies, outlines, prize books, primers and what not that load our book counters, it most certainly is the type that constitutes the representative purchaser of these short-cuts across the field of learning. Who else reads them? Some reviewers, surely. Some half-competent would-be writers of poetry or short-stories, who haven't the sense to read the masters themselves and let the teachers go hang. A few well-meaning ladies and gentlemen who soon discover the error of their way. The rest is cultural incapacity or insincerity.

We have thus produced a literature that takes its place logically, not with books and magazines of a valid sort, but with spurious medication by pellets and nostrums. We have produced, at last, an education that works while you sleep, with no time lost from your regular occupation. 

2. THE ANTHOLOGICAL MIND 

Ten years ago, the aim of the anthology and its fellow forms of abbreviated information coincided with the latest fashion in feminine attire: to get as much as possible into as little space. If display upon the library-table is one of the chief purposes in view-and it is interesting to observe how even the intellectual magazines are not above the library-table insinuation in their advertising-the ideal of multum in parvo is reversed, and we get as little contents as possible in as imposing an outward appearance as may be. An anthology, to begin with this typical product of our literary age, is from the standpoint of the compiler ideally a personal document, representing an epoch, a style, or a form filtered through the selective mind of a personality. At best, from the standpoint of the reader, it is a means, suggesting paths of exploration and fields of pleasure. What is it in practice? Largely a hit-or-miss compilation, so inclusive as to render itself nugatory, and used by the reader, not as a means, but as an end. There was a time when readers did their own selecting; so doing, they were selfencouraged to exercise choice, to form standards, to know a favorite author from snout to tail, the good, the bad and the indifferent. Today the pressure of living is too great for thoroughness; our lives, like our readings, tend to become anthological, with an outsider exercising the choice. The joys of

Isaac Goldberg 27 wading through the bad and the good, as well as the best, are denied us. Yearly we are served the "best" by vicarious process.

How much, indeed, can the annuals devoted to the "best" plays and movies and stories of the year do to cultivate an esthetic attitude toward the drama, the story and the motion picture? Their chief appeal is to amateur writers of plays and scenarios, as low a class as is possible to discover in the range of the pseudo-creative mentality. Whatever the theory behind their production, which is largely commercial, the result is to confer an illusory value upon material already defiled by the crowd. What, in pure fact, was the net profit of the famous 47 Workshop, presided over by that late excellent and most affable gentleman, Prof. G. P. Baker, whom Harvard lost to the greater glory of Yale? Dramatic platitude has stalked abroad with collegiate indorsement; the rest, by exception, is O'Neill.

Our cultural life thus tends to become a life of shreds and patches of snippets. Of snippets, literally, are such organs as the Literary Digest and its numerous latter-day imitators composed.

Reading, which was once a pleasure, has thus become a study. Poems and stories are marked and graded, like themes at school and college. About the whole pleasure of letters, indeed, has been thrown an atmosphere of class-room instruction that makes it disgusting. Naturally, you cannot keep every good story or poem out of an anthology, but you can let a ruck cf bad ones in and have them circulate-as many of them dowith the stamp of academic approval. The general effect is to create, not a public for the better things, but a horde of half-baked intellectuals who regularly go through enough tabloid courses to provide gossip for the dinner-table. Born of a speedy age, these things tend to be done in haste and to be repented of not at all.

If you doubt this view, as one advanced by a mere literary and musical gentleman, read between the lines-and not so deeply between-of the advertisements. The advertising department knows to whom it addresses itself, and why. Sometimes these mentors. are becomingly modest, for whereas Mr. 

Brisbane, in his palmy days, used to assure us that the equivalent of a college education might be acquired from about seven minutes reading a day on the cars, contemporary cultural advisers have raised the figure to 15. Our shelves are not measured by the books they contain, but by the number of feet taken up by the books; an age of speed must save not only time but space.

The effects of such varied factors as these may be discovered in the places where they least have a right to be found. I do not refer, in the instances which I shall presently point out, to such minor matters as erroneous dates, grammat、

ical slips, cases of heterophemy familiar to all such as handle the pen or typewriter, and like lapses from external correctness. With every respect for these lesser but none the less desirable excellencies, criticism is surely something more than sublimated proof-reading and lessons read to authors by misplaced schooldames. But one is not unreasonable, upon opening the pages of a widely and superlatively advertised Outline of Literature, to expect from its editors and contributors at least a dependable guidance. One asks for no unduly excessive esthetic penetration, or abstruse speculation. Surely, however, there should be an approximation to facts, and no heaping of specialized lack of taste and misinformation upon popular ignorance.

The utterly inexcusable, botched and bungled sections of this Outline are those devoted, in the third volume, to modern foreign letters. The crimes are of commission and omission, as well as of proportion. Let proportion pass. Let pass even the fact that not a line was found for the culture of South America, although it has produced one of the greatest of modern Spanish poets and one of the chief prose-writers, as well as a flourishing Portuguese literature in Brazil. Ascribe such an objection to the oxyopic and microscopic delvings of a specialist. But how about the entire absence of old Portugal? Was there not a page in these more than 1,100 for Camoens, at least, and his Lusiads for the Camoens over whom many an Englishman, before Sir Richard Burton and after, has labored patiently in an attempt to translate his ringing octaves and his poignant lyrics? Might not the name, if nothing else, of Eca de Queiroz have been mentioned?

But now turn to Italy and to Spain. Worse still, and more of it. If two names stand out in modern Italian fiction as being of the first historical importance, they are surely those of Alessandro Manzoni and Giovanni Verga. But so far as the reader of the Outline is concerned, these men never existed- I Promessi Sposi, I Malavoglia and Mastro-don Gesualdo were never written. If one name stands out in 19th Century Italian poetry, it is that of Giacomo Leopardi. Yet Leopardi, too, is among the missing. Imagine an account that limits itself to 

Carducci, D'Annunzio and Papini, discussing them in two-and-ahalf pages and devoting half of this scant allotment to an excerpt from Papini's maudlin book on Christ! The whole section is below even the lax standards of a suburban Sunday supplement.

Companioning this account of modern Italian letters is that of latter-day Spain. Echegaray and Galdos are passed over with mere naming, and then, as the lone representative of Spanish literature today, there is Vicente Blasco Ibanez! That even this man's best books are left without mention is by this time to be expected, but that half of the precious space should be devoted to an extract from Blood and Sand is beyond pardon. Just as

Isaac Goldberg 29 Manzoni, Verga and Leopardi escape the chronicler in the Italian section, so here the one glory of modern Spanish letters is overlooked in its entirety. The improvised historian has never heard of the "generation of '98," which opened the windows of the stagnant, classic-ridden cathedral that Spain had become and let the winds of a new Europe blow through the dank aisles and cob-webbed corridors. With the extract from Blood and 

Sand sprawling over the page, there is not a line for Pio Baroja, Benavente, Valle-Inclan, the Machado brothers, the 

Quintero brothers, Perez de Ayala-every mother's son of them culturally and intellectually far more important than Blasco. 

Here is a work that should, at every point, have been presided over by literary specialists. Consider the position of the aspiring sub-intellectual in quest of guidance, pursuing "culture" on the hot foot. He will not even know that certain central figures have lived and written for him—that their works have long been in English, awaiting that eternal rediscovery which is the only true life of literature. Thus does a “plain story simply told" become an important story patently garbled. 

It was in the marshy field of psycho-analysis, in the early days, that we found the lower learning at its amusing worst. 

Art may be a function of the genetic impulse, but the man in the street surely responds more readily to the sexual source than to the artistic tributary. It is but mouthing the obvious to say that the immense vogue of books on the subject has no more to do with a public desire for learning than has the popularity of the leg-show. The book and the revue are both aphrodisiacs. 

So, for that matter, are the poem and the novel and even the symphony, but here the effect is largely unconscious and theoretical, while in the case of the psycho-analytic work the connection is readily demonstrable. There are persons otherwise sane who believe that about every mental ill to which the flesh is heir may be cured by a sexual debauch. I have heard the cure recommended for such diverse ailments as disappointment in politics, inefficiency in studies and nervous pains in the chest. 

The worst of it is that at the bottom of such phantasies is a glimmer of truth. But the lower learning deals by preference with the phantasies.

The flimsy, careless, unconscionable writing in most of the psycho-analytic manuals for the many has merely encouraged soft emotions where hard thought was especially needed. 

And the opuscules on auto-suggestion a la Coue have completed the work of deterioration. In them, the lion of science and the lamb of religion lie down together, and the lamb proceeds, unnaturally but surely, to devour the lion. 

To extend these merely suggestive annotations to the radio, to the movies and the like would be merely to multiply the numerator and denominator of the fraction by the same number. 

In all popularization there is an element of loss; to make something understandable to the people is to remove something of

what is to be understood. The difference between the lower and the higher learning-which is an intellectual, and not a class, distinction-is the difference between adulteration and purity, between affectation and sincerity, between surface appearance and fundamental solidity. In the one, knowledge is a thing for show; in the other, a pursuit self-contained and self-justified. 

That the higher learning is itself adulterated by extraneous elements is merely an argument for its own self-cleansing. 

3. COUNT BRAINS, NOT HEADS 

Today, the apparatus for the lower learning is immense; it includes the news service of the cinema, most of the columnists with their grist of meaningless information; the radio demagogues; the "fan" magazines; a stupendous and stupefying omnium gatherum, a hodge-podge, of facts and fiction. 

Not so much to know, as to "be in the know," becomes the desideratum, not only among common-place minds but among persons of a certain native intelligence. 

I am not opposed, please bear in mind, to the spreading of education, even in pellets. Seeds fall, and have a habit of sprouting; if not here, then there. I am opposed, however, to the wilful democratization of learning in such a way as to cast aspersion upon the learning itself. The fact that some high-brows are pedants does not confer upon the low-brow a superiority inherent in his low-brow status. The treatment of the learned class in Russia, immediately following upon the revolution, was largely compounded of pure vindictiveness. There is much of that vindictiveness against the learned classes even in the United States. How much of this resentment the class has earned, I am not prepared to say. A distinction should be made between the learning and the class as a class. 

For, however much one may please to misinterpret and contort Marx's theory of the materialist dialectic, one cannot establish an easy-going parallel between an economic structure and the essence of learning. There are things which are as true as human ingenuity can discover, and that remain true through any change of economic organization. So, too, there are fundamental human structures-biological and psychological set-ups that remain stable despite the different patterns and interrelationships assumed by the body politic and the body economic. 

Man is the constant; social structure is the variable. If this were not so, such a concept as dialectical materialism would lose most of its value. 

Let it sound counter-revolutionary, Fascist, reactionary, conservative, or what you will, man must in every instance beware of surrendering to the illusive magic of his own numbers -to the settlement of problems by the counting, not of brains, but of heads.

I do not argue, as some sentimental sociologists do, for

Isaac Goldberg 31 the recall of the invention of printing. The cure for print is more print. Spread it thin first, if it must be so. But rub it in deep. For a little learning is a dangerous thing not only to the little-learner. . . . The lower learning has exerted, in many instances, an evil effect upon the intellectual; it has afflicted him, indeed, with an inferiority complex that takes the form of a false bonhomie -a caricature of intellectual democracy. 

In all persons of superior intelligence there is nevertheless a strong pull toward lower standards. Intelligence is a discipline that must be forever consolidating its conquests. The crowd element in all of us represents a powerful inertia. It resists that advance which is typified in the emergent individual. It is all the easier for the intellectual to slide back toward the crowd from which he has emerged since he is, as are we all, mostly crowd even at his most highly educated. 

The intellectual life resounds with lip-service to culture. 

What, then, is the amount of mere lip-service from the crowd? 

Under the signature of George A. Dorsey, for example, I discover a new definition of the "esthete," who is described as "a light-headed and empty-headed modern social parasite, who toils not nor spins, nor breeds;, who is as anemic as a jelly fish, and whose motto is 'Art for Art's sake'." Now, I know the esthete type in its valid as well as in its exaggerated exemplifications, but I must protest that this would-be funny definition is itself densely crammed with exaggeration, with what I have called false bonhomie between scholar and man-in-thestreet, with disguised condescension toward that man-in-thestreet-with the intellectual's fear of not belonging-with a subtle repudiation of that which distinguishes man-as-mob from man-as-individual-with a facile mis-reading of a famous phrase with moralistic judgments. 

It is high time that we really knew what we were talking about when we used such words as "highbrow," "intellectual" and "esthete." It is high time that we decided, in the interest of the commoner himself, that he must be brought, together with his more learned companions, and in no spirit of condescension, up to the highest levels we are acquainted with rather than be allowed to accommodate himself, and pull others down to, the lowest practical levels.

Here, for example, is a great work of art, which has withstood the impact of several revolutions, and which will withstand the impact of revolutions to come, because it is true to something deeper in man than the forms which his social organization may assume. What makes it great is the evidence of a rare soul, not a common one, however many the common traits that genius must share with the least of us. It is for the leaders not to emphasize the commonness of the genius and pull him down to the crowd-level, but rather to underscore his rarity. 

Art and Science do not flatter mobs. They create individ-

uals. The creation of individuals is the supreme function of any great economy, of any great collectivity. 

4. MORE HIGHBROWS, ESTHETES AND INTELLECTUALS 

One word was too often profaned for Shelley to profane it; what shall we say of its profanation today? But I do not speak here of love. Words are much like coins that wear thin in circulation. Too much usage, it would seem, sometimes debases the coinage. Words, like empires, have their rise and fall; they come in like fashions and like fashions go out. Even as human beings, they flourish and die; but sometimes we may hope for a resurrection. I have particularly in mind three words that of late have suffered a decline. These words are "highbrow," "intellectual," and "esthete." The problem they present is not one of definition, although definition could help to clarify our ideas as to their real meaning; what I am concerned with for the moment is rather their rehabilitation.

Today, to call a man a high-brow is to offer him a not too delicate insult. He does not have to know, nor does the one who employs it, that it is almost-not quite a translation of the Latin derivative, supercilious; he feels, as it is meant for him to feel, that he has been branded as a person without a practical relation to life, as one who is self-removed from the necessary commonplaces of living. So far, perhaps, little harm has been done, for there are those of us who can constantly suffer such reminders.

There is a danger here, however, more subtle than the insult itself. A close observer of the so-called educated classes may discover it at work even in those who pass as our intellectual leaders. The vogue for the humanization of all knowledge has not been without its analogous dangers. And it may well be that between these two perils there is a close interrelation. In his eagerness to simplify the world of the intellect for the man in the street the man of intellect has, while raising the general level of knowledge, tended to lower his own lofty standard. I know persons of brains who, in their terror of being called highbrows, deliberately cultivate qualities and expressions that at bottom they despise. Here, in the story of one little word, we may discern a valuable commentary on the misunderstanding of democracy. 

One stigma of the high-brow is supposed to be his use of "big" words. I recall an automobile mechanic who objected to the vocabulary of his physician. "Who can understand all those long terms?" he complained, upon receiving certain instructions in the glib cant of the medical profession. He was not entirely wrong; doctors talk like that through force of training and habit and can, at need, translate their lingo into the vernacular. The very next day, however, when the physician whom I have in mind, having at last decided to learn how to

Isaac Goldberg 33 drive a car, came to the garage for instruction, the selfsame mechanic treated him with a barrage of automotive technicalities that were as terrifying as anything in Gray's Anatomy. 

Mr. Babbitt, who is so ready with his condemnation of the long words that are used by artists, writers, critics, and other members of the intellectual fraternity, would be surprised to discover how many equally long words he himself uses in his daily task of selling bonds, automobiles and bathtubs. Highbrow in this particular sense is a relative term. The man who sells me an electrical refrigerator, and who with warm enthusiasm begins to expatiate upon the technique of its operation is, when confronted by my utter simplicity in these matters, just as much a high-brow as I would be if I were suddenly to begin lecturing him upon "significant form" (provided I knew what that was) or the function of distortion in modernist painting. 

We were all taught at school to eschew the long Latin words for the short Anglo-Saxon ones. Now certainly, I am making no argument for the sesquipedalian talk that flowed from Rabelais' scholar. But it is a mistake to imagine that all great and deep thought may be put into words of one syllable, or that the scholar who is skilled in mathematical or linguistic abstractions is therefore spinning fine and irrelevant fantasies in a world that has nothing to do with ours. It is not always possible to put big ideas into small words; that such condensation is expected is merely another evidence of what we might call the infra-democratic, tabloid age. It is an undignified concession to haste, waste and the lower forms of popular education. 

Those Latin words that so terrify some of our low-brows are the common speech of the Latin-race low brow. The division between big words and little is, it seems to me, a false one.

Not the length of the word is important, nor its Nordic or Mediterranean origin, so much as its aliveness. The terminology of the automobile mechanic-his magnetos, carburetors and the rest-never strikes him as high-brow, despite its narrow technicalism, because it concerns things and processes with which he is in close, daily contact. That is why medical cant flows so easily from the lips of a doctor. Let us stop measuring words and pay more attention to the life that is in them. 

I am concerned about the fate of "high-brow" because the decline of the term and the disparagement that it has come to imply have affected the scholar himself. To rehabilitate the term somehow or other involves a rehabilitation of the type for which it stands.

Just as the scholar-a certain kind of scholar, and by no means a corporal's guard-is made unpleasantly self-conscious by the accusation of being a high-brow, so does he dwell in fear of being called an "intellectual." Some years ago it really became difficult to discover a bonafide intellectual in all of New York City. The word had become a taunt and all the intellectuals were engaged in hurling it at each other. There were

those, to be sure, who had so caricatured the preoccupations of the intellectual life as to lend a certain justice to the odium into which the word had fallen. For there are always such in any pursuit of life. Yet I must confess that there was something, to me, degrading about the manner in which our intelligentsia seemed to flee to cover at the mere mention of the adjective that had grown into a noun.

It all bears eloquent testimony to humanity's fear of ridicule. The forces of evil have sensed this, and have discovered that they have no need of reasoned opposition; their first argument and their last is the laughter of ridicule. Now, it is the mark of a superior intelligence that he can hear this laughter undismayed and it is no compliment to our intellectuals that they have allowed the term under discussion to pass unchallenged in its disparaging connotation. 

Specialized knowledge and the increasingly involved terminology that it implies are hardly anything to be ashamed of. 

Too many devourers of Outlines of this, that and the other, have been flattered into the belief that the Outline is the cream of the subject, and that all the rest is "hot air" for the highbrows. Saccharin has passed for sugar and too many aspiring low-brows have starved sweetly. 

There is an old saying in the humble Yiddish tongue about the man who, having acquired money, proceeds to seek honor. 

It is a transition that is not at all limited to race or time. Even those who are loudest in their proclamations as to the supreme virtue of a cash existence suddenly reveal, once they have laid a secure financial foundation, aspirations to be known as collectors of something besides coins. Dimly they achieve the understanding that man cannot live by bread or money alone, though without bread surely we die. These are the very persons who, in their original state of unenlightenment, poked fun at the artist and esthete for their preoccupation with unworldly values.

What does this mean? It seems very simple to explain. The so-called practical type has a sneaking admiration for or envy of the so-called impractical, intellectual, type. Too often, of course, the wealthy man in quest of honor would achieve his new status by sheer power of purchase. He is following a fad; but somebody in his set started it, and at the bottom of the fad lay this hazy tribute to brains. Some years ago I used to be entertained quite regularly by the scarcely credible, yet easily anthenticated, stories that were told to me by a friend who then owned one of the largest book establishments in Boston. 

He enjoyed-it was enjoyment-the patronage of many of the nouveaux riches who had determined to become nouveaux intellectuels as well. It was no uncommon occurrence for him to receive an order such as "Send me up 20 feet of books right away; I've just put in a library." Or, "What have you

Isaac Goldberg 35 got in some nice morocco sets that will go with the Spanish red sofas in my new living room? 

I should blush to name the ancient and honorable sets of which he was thus enabled to dispose. Of course, as I have said, much of this, on the part of the purchasers, was unthinking imitation. Books, at the time, happened to be the right thing to have on display in one's living room. Yet beneath this sorry display lay deference to a certain system of values.

At the same time that these phenomenal book sales were taking place, art, in the same social stratum, suddenly became a matter of importance-or, rather, of prestige. Unoccupied females who hadn't read a classic in their own tongue swerved without notice from their courses on the Cosmogony of Dante and grew strangely wistful about plastics, about volumes and line and pattern and design and form. It is only one generation from wollen underwear to silk step-ins, if any. It was one of the newly-rich mothers of this generation who was heard inviting an artist to her home, after she had duly attended his exhibition: "You must come over to visit with us very soon," she urged the painter. "We have some very nice things on our walls. All painted by hand."

To use words-and ideas and attitudes-that one does not understand is common, and on the whole, a venial sin. The fondness for long words, employed in the cause of producing a good impression, is based upon an unacknowledged respect for those who can use them properly. And it does happen, with comforting frequency, that those who assume a virtue if they have it not end up by having the virtue, too. So that there is hope even for the lady whose new home reflects only the taste of the interior decorator who happens to be the vogue in her set. 

The worst sufferer among these terms is undoubtedly the word "esthete," together with its derivatives. Its ancient origins were high and honorable. It indicated, as it still should indicate, one who is discriminatingly sensitive to the impressions of surrounding life and who, because of this uncommon sensitivity, is more or less skilled in describing his sensations. 

What, in our own day, has it sunk to? As recently as 1880 it received a body blow at the hands of Gilbert and Sullivan in that delight of all Savoyards, the operetta Patience. Even in his own day Gilbert was accused of having launched a fell assault upon the cult of beauty, and thus having come with aid and comfort to the rescue of the Philistine in his eternal war upon the finer qualities of the race. I do not think that this was so. Anyone who is acquainted with the non-musical plays written by Gilbert in verse will not need to be told that Gilbert himself had much of the esthete in his makeup, however much he may have tried to conceal it behind a mask of bluntness and even cruelty. The queen of the fairies in Iolanthe was

singing for Gilbert, as well as for herself, when she confessed that 

This heart of mine 

Is soft as thine, Although I dare not say so. 

But Gilbert's aim is beside the point. The Postlethwaites, the Cimabue Browns, the collegiate Oscar Wilde that he satirized so effectively deserved his castigation. It was they, not 

Gilbert, who were caricaturing the cult of beauty. Unfortunately, however, having first intended to satirize the clergy, Gilbert found it more expedient to train his guns upon less obvious game. The result was that, for multitudes who could not be expected to exercise niceties of discrimination, he powerfully reinforced the current prejudice of the man in the street against the man, so to speak, in the studio. "The high esthetic line" was again, and this time insidiously, presented to the public as an effeminate pursuit. The esthete himself, although Bunthorne turned out to be a fraud, became an epicene character, as dubious in gender as in deed. 

Only seven or eight years ago we had in this country a striking example of how a similar attitude, and perhaps one similarly justified, operated to produce like results. I refer to the well-remembered essay by Ernest Boyd entitled Aesthete: 

Model 1924. That trenchant piece did a lot of good and, I fear, some harm. Very frequently in private I heard Mr. Boyd accused of having done in this monograph what Gilbert, on a larger scale, had done in Patience.

Just as the word "professor" has been made to cover public services so widely divergent as boot-blacking, vaudeville pianism and scholastic pursuits upon university faculties, so has the term "esthetic" been applied to such widely differing activities as the philosophy of Benedetto Croce and interior decorating. The jokes at the expense of the interior decorators' supposed effeminacy have, of course, become part of contemporary American folk lore.

The word has been denatured in yet another respect-one that again reveals the sneaking admiration of the man and woman in the street for the very artist type that they affect to abhor. People who make no pretense of being esthetic, who have not heard the word or would be offended to have it applied to them, nevertheless are plainly complimented when they are told, or tell you, how sensitive they are. Usually, theirs is a sensitiveness to things that hardly matter. But they, in their own way, desire to be artists, too. 

The degeneration of the word has somehow carried with it a degeneration in the quality for which it originally stood. 

Just as many a high-brow has come to fear being called a highbrow, just as many an intellectual has come to fear being called an intellectual, so has many a spirit of esthetic temperament come to fear being called an esthete. Our cultural capitals

Isaac Goldberg 37 are full of just such citizens as these. Out of this fear is born a sort of protective coloration-I should call it discoloration by which the intellectual, the high-brow and the esthete seek in more or less forced actions to reaffirm their solidarity with our common mortality. Thus is the compliment of the "low-brow" returned.

I have in mind one of the finest esthetic spirits in this country-a gentleman who has distinguished himself with his contributions to the criticism of our emergent native arts. I had long respected his work; I still do. And I had been long eager to meet him. Well, we met, this gentleman and I. And almost the first thing he told me, with an unmistakable purpose in his emphasis, was that at last he was making some real money, and that his interests in life were by no means exclusively cultural. Now, that might have been the right "line" to take with somebody else; not with me. The fellow was seemingly afraid lest I should take him for what he was—a finefibered, sensitive soul. What was there about this to make him feel uneasy or apologetic? Why did he, of all people, need to underscore his financial soundness, lest otherwise his pursuits should appear to be idle and unjustified? I am inclined to think that he had conceived a fear of the very word "esthete" that he had helped, in its best sense, to acclimatize among us. 

The impression that the esthetic approach to life concerns itself only with delicate refinement is erroneous. 

One may bring discriminating sensitivity to a prize fight as well as to a painting or a symphony. At the root of estheticism lies, not hyper-sensitivity, not effeminacy, but that sterling quality known as disinterestedness, which is by no means a synonym for lack of interest. 

Esthetics, disinterestedness, after all, is an attitude, not a vocabulary. Consider what happened at Philadelphia during the baseball world's series of 1931. "Pepper" Martin had suddenly risen from obscurity into the position of the leading feature in the series. He so captivated the imaginations of the baseball fans that, when he came to Philadelphia, even the

Philadelphians rooted long and loud for this rival from St. 

Louis. His appearance at bat was the signal for thunderous cheers; here was playing that rose above the categories of conflict. For the moment it was the game itself, not the victory, that was important. The attitude is imbedded in the popular saying, "May the best man win." In much the same way, wherever Babe Ruth used to appear on the circuit of the American League the fans forgot momentarily their private passions and sent up prayers for the Bambino to swat out a homer. 

It is high time that we ceased fearing names. We need more high-brows, more intellectuals, more esthetes, and more men and women who understand that art for art's sake does not mean the repudiation of humanity, any more than does science for the sake of science. In the long run, the "practical"

man has proved, for the world, less practical, from even a pragmatic standpoint, than the man in the studio and the man in the laboratory. Successively the world has entrusted its destinies to the theologian, the soldier, the man of law, the man of commerce-and these guides have led us-whither? 

We need, even for the practical life, more of the qualities that the scientist and the artist can give us more disinterestedness, more imagination-more high-brows, if you will, more intellectuals, more esthetes!

III. Science and Art 1. FALSE DIVISIONS 

Genius and mediocrity are not, speaking from the aspect of the human species as a whole rather than from the standpoint of any two individuals, divided by an unbridgeable gulf. 

The difference is in degree, not kind. If it were truly a difference in kind, we should dwell in a world that lacked all connection with the world of genius. It would be impossible for us to appreciate genius-to have any suspicion of what it was, since the circles of its existence and of our own, respectively, would not even overlap, thus depriving each of the small sector of common ground that makes reciprocal understanding possible.

In the same way, every person is both a scientist and an artist. The differences in these cases, too, are differences of degree, not kind. We may go further and say that the differences between science and art themselves are differences of emphasis, and not, as so many people easily suppose, differences of material. Our vocabulary-for language is a highly perfected, yet still a highly imperfect, technique for the communication of thoughts and emotions-is full of misleading terms. The existence of such separate words as "mind" and "body" leads us by almost imperceptible sorcery to imagine that the things for which they stand are as separable as the words. In much the same manner we forget that such divisions as "physical" and "psychic," "body" and "soul," "mental" and "material," "real" and "ideal," "sensual" and "cerebral," and sc on down the long, long list, refer to neatly divisible entities rather than to largely linguistic conveniences. 

We are scientists-every one of us-when we become curious about the nature of life and attempt to organize all that we can learn about it into a body of useful knowledge. We are artists-every one of us-when we shift the emphasis from the accumulation and control of knowledge to the emotional thrill of experience to the expression of experience for the purpose of heightening the thrill for ourselves or [what really amounts to the same thing] communicating it to others. 

So, at this point, we find it useful to remember that such terms as "emotional" and "intellectual," "scientific" and "artistic," and their more or less familiar twin-companions, likewise represent not so much opposing conceptions as correlated ones. They are, so to speak, children born of the same 39

parents, differing perhaps in outward appearance but tracing their existence to the same origin.

We think of music, for example, as an art, and of the composer as an artist. Yet the fundamentals of music are sourced in science, and though the composer does not have to know physics, music has a physico-mathematical foundation. Harmony, to the student of musical composition, may be an art; to the student of sound it is a science. The same is true of all pursuits in which man engages. It is only the petty scientist who looks down upon art with a superior air, simply because its results are not, like those of science, measurable. It is only a petty artist who looks down upon science because its concern is with the more material and more measurable aspect of things rather than with the expression of our emotional attitude toward them.

Our daily conversation is full, likewise, of conceptions that represent false unities. We are asked to use the word "religion" as if it alone included the notion of ethics. "What," runs a familiar question, "is to become of standards if we let religion die?" What; one might ask, has become of them, in any case, with religion alive? One would imagine that among non-believers there were no standards of ethical procedure. 

Religion, as a matter of fact, includes a number of conceptions that are not implicit in systems of ethics. And Atheism, certainly, is by no means opposed on principle to ethical standards. The unity between Atheism and libertinism is just as false as that between religion and the exclusive possession of lofty standards.

Specialization may be carried to the point of humorlessness, and what began as an attempt to achieve a broader and deeper feeling for the universe may end as à narrower and shallower feeling. I was present once at an impromptu debate between a composer and a physician, and could not help remembering, as they argued, the too easy saying that a specialist is a man who knows everything about one thing and nothing about everything else. [There is a deal of truth in the observation, and even more of the commoner's envy of his superior.]

The composer was complaining to the equally well-known physician that public interest in music, especially his modernist type of music, was apathetic. "Now, take you physicians," he blurted. "What do you know about music? What do you care about music? Isn't it a shame, for example, that a man of your prominence and intelligence should be so wilfully ignorant of a great and noble art? How can you really be said to live anything like a full life when you deliberately ignore the artistic side of your nature? How can we expect anything of the common man when you, the patrician of the intellect, remain so indifferent?"

The physician, for a moment, was nonplussed. Like so

Isaac Goldberg 41 many of his busy colleagues, he had always meant to catch up with the arts. Sometimes, to be sure, he was scared off by the type of devotee that he encountered. All at once, however, a wave of indignation came over him. "See here, Mr. Musico," he permitted himself to retort. "I like your narrow-mindedness. What do you know about science? What do you care about science? Isn't it a shame that an artist of your prominence should be so wilfully ignorant of this vast field of investigation? How can we expect anything of the common man when you, a patrician of the emotions, remain so indifferent?" 

The composer was taken aback. So completely, in fact, that he had no time to invent a rationalization and had to have recourse to the plain truth. "Frankly," he replied, genuinely contrite, "you have me there. . . . I'm as bad as the people against whom I've been complaining. You know, I'm such a busy man. My art is my life. I am ignorant of science." "Oh, I'm ignorant enough of art," confessed the physician. "No, don't let me off as easily as that," continued the composer. "I mean it. It never occurred to me. I never felt, until this very moment, how narrow my interests had become. 

I don't even know the science of the sounds I use in my work. 

I hadn't even realized that others could become engrossed in their specialties even as I had become. I feel like a fool." 

Similar arguments do not always end in such honest statements Specialists-and, in a way we are all specialists, too, however inexpert-are too often like the child who laughs upon hearing an unfamiliar foreign language spoken. It is not one's own, therefore it is ridiculous.

The world of ideas, like the world in which we live, is round. It is well, very well, to cultivate our garden; but we should know that the world is full of other gardens-that, indeed, to expand the Voltairean figure, all the world is a garden. It is necessary, in order to classify and control the elements of our knowledge, to analyze; it is even more necessary to make a synthesis-an organic, an integrated unityof those elements. The world, at best, is a jig-saw puzzle that will never be solved. In our enthusiasm over recognizing new parts let us not forget that they have their position in the complete picture that is, always remembering that it is not for us to bring that picture to ultimate completion. 

I am fond of quoting a line from George Lewis' now neglected Life of Goethe. "The whole man thinks," he wrote, in order to emphasize just this roundness-perhaps I should say sphericity-of thought. But the whole man feels, too. And he is not really a whole man unless his thinking and his feeling have become integrated, organic—that is, vital aspects of an underlying unity.

It is the part of the lesser scientist to make a rigid distinction between the real and the ideal, between reality and imagination. Life-the pattern that we impress upon mean-

inglessness so that it may be turned to our purposes-is a conquest of the imagination. Without the imagination—the capacity for making images--there could not be what we call reality. 

Reality, for us, has no meaning apart from the image that we form of the world. An existence it may have; not a meaning. 

We may say, then, that Reality is just as imaginary as the imagination; or that the Imagination is just as real as reality. 

This is not hazy metaphysics; it is truth to our experience. 

We can know the world only as it appears to us to our senses and what we can do with them. All Life, in Vaihinger's famcus phrase, is an as if. . . . A simile, or a metaphor, or a symbol. A relativity, with ourselves naturally as the point of reference.

It is at this point that we may consider with profit the underlying worth of such terms as "objectivity" and "subjectivity," together with the almost countless isms that populate the books on criticism. At the risk of seeming to over-emphasize the principle, I must indicate that these are not so reciprocally exclusive as one has been taught to believe. 

The world of ideas is round. Romanticism does not exclude what we call classicism, unless we limit the words rigidly to their historical dates. And even then I should not care to defend the thesis that the Classical age had no "romantic" elements, or that the Romantic period had no "classical" elements. Thinking too much in terms of isms blinds us, more or less subtly, to likenesses that would undermine the feeling of opposition between one ism and another. It is the familiar case, from a different angle, of analysis that fails to make the proper synthesis. 

To say "I am a romantic," or "I am a classicist" is more a boast of self-limitation than a definition of artistic beliefs. 

Toward some situations we react like romantics; toward others, like classicists. Life is an experience, not a stencil. Toward the same situation, indeed, at times we feel a so-called romantic response, and at others, a so-called classical. Who, especially in the arts, is to say us nay? Rigid adherence to an ism is but another form of absolutism, which is as a door shut against the living stream of experience. It is a committal of our future to a set of responses that may not be adequate to the unpredictable stimuli. It may express a strong conviction, but inevitably one that cannot be complete. 

2. ILLOGICALITY AND VITALITY 

No matter how logical may be the structure of our position, we had better keep a tiny crevice open for exit. For the illogicality that may save us from surrender. Croce has said somewhere in his study of Dante that it is this illogical element which represents the true vitality. Perhaps it is not even illogical. Perhaps it is part of a more inclusive logic. We do

Isaac Goldberg 43 not live by Truth, but by the accumulation-and therefore a constant re-arrangement-of "truths." "Objective" and "subjective," to return to two terms that have acquired new life in contemporary politico-economic controversy, are not opposites. They are correlates-phases of attitude, which would really lack meaning to us were they sharply marked off from one another.

Man is at once the object and the subject of his own thinking, just as he is at once the indistinguishable member of a social group and an individual distinct from his group. He is objective when he considers the world outside; he is subjective when he considers the world inside of him. Both worlds are part of the larger world in which he has his being-aspects of an organic unity, not forces inevitably disunited. It is impossible for man to live solely as a subjective creature, unless he retires into the privacy of mental illness. It is impossible for man to live solely as an objective creature, unless he surrenders his identity, and becomes a robot. 

In the political terminology of today we may make an equation between "subjective" and "individual"; also between "objective" and "social." But to make an equation, as is constantly being done, between "subjective" and "unreal," and between "objective" and "real," is to fall into a facile errorto make the too easy assumption that only those things are real that we can touch.

Contemporary criticism of the arts and of life in general, as practiced by the Communists now in power in the U. S. S. 

R., as well as by their adherents in the United States, tends toward harmful confusion of such terms as "individualism" and "subjectivism." Because individualism in economics has bred [and been bred by] the major abuses of Capitalism, they make an equation between individualism in economics and individualism in the arts and even in the sciences. Individualism and subjectivism become anathema, in the mistaken notion that for a collectivist state only collectivism on the part of the artist-as well as on the part of the producer and distributorare pertinent.

The equation between economic individualism and individualism in the arts and science is a false one. Individualism in economics tends, through control of the sources of life, to suppression of living. Individualism in the arts and sciences [it is virtually another, a different, word] tends through freedom of expression toward release of living. Words are more than their etymologies; words, too, are like the notes of a score, which remain dead on paper and must be played upon the instruments of our minds.

The implications of these terms, so far as they refer to criticism of the arts under Soviet rule, I shall take up later in these pages. It is enough, for the moment, to point out

how easily we fall into the habit of arguing with words, rather than with the meanings underneath those words. 

Science organizes our knowledge of life. Art organizes our feelings about life. 

In the light of this fundamental conception we may appreciate the folly of trying to keep Science and Art definitely separate. We may appreciate, also, the less obvious folly be hind our facile scorn of Art for Art's sake. 

The scorn is rooted in a misunderstanding both on the part of some artists and the part of the general public. Few people, for example, would discover any objection to a phrase such as "science for the sake of science." They would not interpret it as a withdrawal from the interests of mankind. They would not interpret it, certainly, as a symptom of hostility toward that public. They would not, if you pursued the discussion, deny that the scientist, in his laboratory, engaged seemingly in the study of irrelevant or remote details, was performing a useful function by preparing those details for the magic touch of synthesis. At this touch, the mass of detail becomes a meaningful pattern, enlisted in the service of that very humanity from which, to a superficial observer, the scientist had apparently withdrawn.

There is another meaning to science for the sake of science. The scientist, recall, represents mankind's passion for knowing his universe and co-ordinating that knowledge. The co-ordination of that knowledge, as distinguished from the unsynthesized accumulation of it, is what we call wisdom.

Knowledge comes but wisdom lingers, says the proverb. In other words, it is far easier to heap up data than to integrate them into a significant unity. The pedant, for example, is one who, in his passion for knowledge, falls short of wisdom. He stops at analysis, and fails to make the needful integration. 

Learning, for him, becomes an end, rather than the means to fuller and happier living.

The important syntheses of science are not daily occurrences. They do not happen in response to the impatience of the crowd. About the investigations of the scientist there is something austere, half impersonal. He does his best-knowing the frailties of the human mind-not to allow his results to be colored by his wishes. This is not because the scientist is impersonal, lacking in warm desires, or human bias, but because as scientist-he must obtain as complete a report of the universe as is possible to human limitations. He recognizes those limitations; he seeks to allow for them; but he recognizes, too, that some of them may be transcended. His passion is accuracy. He reports not what he hopes for but what he finds. A minor inaccuracy in a detail can mean a major inaccuracy in the larger synthesis. If new data seemingly point to the error of a long-held, deeply-cherished theory, the personal

Isaac Goldberg 45 emotion of the scientist has nothing to do with the case. 

Science for the sake of science, then, means little else than loyalty in the service of science. It is one of the highest conceptions that man has evolved. It shares, indeed, the dignity of Greek tragedy, which is likewise among the loftiest of man's conceptions. Such loyalty connotes a determination to conquer the universe by conquering, first of all, one's pre-conception of it; by readiness to alter one's conception in the light of new knowledge.

It is simpler, of course, to appreciate this dedication in applied science rather than in its purer forms. A tractor that does not work is improved, not by prayer or propaganda, but by a fundamental knowledge of mechanics. No false or biased reports upon the laws of gravity or of thermodynamics will make the tractor operate. The case is no different in the laboratory. 

The closer one starts to such truth as we can discover, the more likely one is to lead to other truths.

Not this only. The very attitude of science toward the problems of the universe serves as a corrective to its own errors. And here we come upon one of its advantages over the dogmas of theology. One reads, altogether too often in these supposedly enlightened days, and sometimes from the pens of philosophers and scientists, that science has its dogmas just as has theology.

In a sense this is true; in a deeper sense it is false, and dangerously so.

In what sense is it true? Simply that, on its way to ever fuller syntheses, science adopts certain theories. Nobody knows better than the scientist himself that all adoption of theories is provisional. A hypothesis is good only as long as it serves better than any other hypothesis as an explanation of all available data. It is scrapped the moment a more inclusive explanation is discovered. It is never regarded as an absolute. 

It is not in the nature of scientific investigation to trust in absolutes.

Consider, on the other hand, the nature of theological dogma. Such dogma, in the first place, is not open to question; it is open only to acceptance. It is not a provisional statement; it is an absolute command. Distinctly, it precludes examination by the intelligence. It dwells upon inaccessible heights, secure forever against being scrapped. It is not in the nature of theological dogma to court investigation.

Dogma, in theology, is self-perpetuating absolutism. 

Scientific theory dwells in a different climate of the human personality, points in a different direction, and preserves an emotional and intellectual honesty that does not pause at selfdestruction. 

Let us hear no more, especially from the philosophers, about the dogmas of science being likened to the dogmas of theology.

3. IVORY TOWERS 

From science for the sake of science to art for art's sake is not a long step. Beneath each lies the self-same loyalty and honesty. I know that there are historical reasons for having associated this famous slogan with an unsocial, or even an anti-social, attitude. Just as the scientist, however, in his isolation, may prove far more serviceable to the human race than the eloquent demagogue who stirs up the crowd with superficial appeal, so may the artist achieve like results in a like isolation. The ivory tower is a symbol, not a tangible reality. 

Surely we should be on guard against confusing such terms as sociable and social. The hermit is hardly a sociable creature. Yet, in his hermitage, he may evolve theories that will one day shake society to its foundations. 

The name of such a hermit may be Karl Marx. The name of such an ivory tower may be the British Museum. Was ever man more "high-brow" than Marx? His Capital still requires constant exegesis-explanation and re-explanation. Was ever man more "intellectual" than Marx? Later we shall see that his spirit of dedication possessed even a "disinterested" quality that is related at once to the dedication of the scientist and to the much (and confusedly) despised estheticism of the artist.

The artist reports upon the emotions of mankind. Just as the scientist seeks to push back further and further the boundaries of the knowable, so does the artist seek to find fuller expression for an ever wider and deeper range of expressiveness. To science there are no Bluebeards' private chambers. 

There are no untouchable holy arks. There are no names that must not be pronounced. The artist shares a like curiosity.

He is, again like the scientist, an experimenter, speculating not only upon what is or was, but upon what might be. He, too, has a strong, undoctrinary loyalty to his vision. 

The artist, however, deals with less tangible elements than those of the scientist. His conception of that truth to which he is so loyal is therefore different from that of the scientist. Yet the quality of his truthfulness is the same. Ḥe cannot, without damage to his integrity, falsify his reports upon the human being. He, too, must be on guard against preconceptions. This is difficult. For, by definition, art affords to the artist a wider application of his own personality to the problems of his profession. Art, more than science, may embody the prejudices of the artist. Art, more than science, may embody the prejudices of the epoch. All this, without any conscious intervention on the part of the artist. 

This cannot alter the fact that the artist represents the eagerness of humanity for the fullest possible satisfactions of experience. For this reason theology, or any other system intent upon standardizing the beliefs and actions of mankind,

Isaac Goldberg 47 bears down upon science and art with equally heavy hand. The natural trend of both science and art, without the specification of program or purpose, is in the direction of freedom. It must be. For, just as science contains within itself the corrective to its errors, so art-broadly considered-contains within itself the self-restorative principle of its balance. Just as, from the accumulating data of science, we are enabled to construct more inclusive syntheses, so from the accumulated experiences reported by art we are enabled to construct a more inclusive vision of the emotional world. A particular example, or school, of art may strike us as unbalanced; the totality of art, like the totality of science, works toward a fuller truth. 

Art for art's sake, then, means truth to personal vision. 

From this standpoint, the multitudinous schools of art, the swarm of isms, need by no means be more than superficially hostile to one another; beneath their verbal and programmatic differences they are related by the truth of their respective personal visions. They are not creatures from different planets; they are quarrelsome first cousins.

In affirming the right to free thought and the right to free imaginative experience I affirm the right-indeed, the duty-of science and art to untrammeled cultivation of their respective fields. I keep in mind, too, the thought that freedom may consist of a limitation entered into voluntarily, as a result of a fuller understanding of the conditions. Freedom without a limitation should be accepted, not submitted to. 

Without full freedom in science and art, it is not likely that we can attain to a fuller understanding of life's conditions. Submission to theological dogma is not freedom or fuller understanding of life; it is slavery and, at the extreme, mental suicide.

Science and art are possessed of autonomy-self-determination. In the interests of better service to humanity that autonomy should not be denied or restricted. Science for the sake of science, and art for art's sake, assume the basic importance of this autonomy-this self-government and selfresponsibility. Observing these respective rights, humanity does but confer upon itself (for scientist and artist are but phases of its own personality) the right to inviolate living.

In a sense that is not at all mystical, suppression in the end returns like a boomerang upon ourselves, and freedom that we confer even upon those with whom we cannot see eye to eye redounds likewise to our own benefit.

IV. Problems of Criticism and Censorship 1. WE ARE ALL CRITICS 

We have agreed that there is no such thing as absolute freedom. The phrase is really a contradiction in terms. Freedom connotes the exercise of choice. There must be alternatives between which a choice is to be made. The artist is restricted by the nature of his material. That, at bottom, is what material means. One cannot chisel stone the way one carves wood. One cannot use paint the way one uses ink. A story cannot reproduce reality or imagination in the way that music can; nor can music tell a story in the way that is open to words. This does not say that the arts are not constantly attempting to poach upon one another's preserves. The sense of limitation, however, is very soon reached when the artist tries to transcend the physical potentialities of his special medium.

Freedom, in a very fine sense, is at the opposite pole from lack of responsibility. Freedom, indeed, demands increased responsibility. Freedom is not a sinecure; it is a high privilege. Freedom is not a burden that every man can, without training, endure. It is one of the most arduous of disciplines. 

It is not conferred by the law; the law can only assure it and protect it. It is not, for that matter, truly abrogated by law; the law can, however, obstruct it and subject it to persecution. 

The work of art has at least two fundamental aspects, which are known by many names that are but variations of these aspects. We speak of form and content; or of matter and manner; or of substance and style. The artist not only has something to say, but he says it in a manner that heightens the effectiveness of his saying. The phrase, "something to say," is misleading. The artist does not deal in "messages," though often his product will have an intensely ethical as well as esthetic effect. One might, if one did not wish to appear too paradoxical, say that ethics is the esthetics of living, and that esthetics is the ethics of art.

Ethics, the morality of action, seeks nevertheless a heightened beauty and effectiveness of living. Whatever would add beauty to living is, by definition, esthetic.

Esthetics, the morality of art, imples on the part of the artist a virtually selfless dedication to the loftiest principles that his efforts and his studies can evolve. Such a dedication, 48

Isaac Goldberg 

49 by definition, is a moral [not a moralistic or a moralizing] quality. 

Ethics and esthetics, then, are not hostile disciplines; they are correlates. Carried to extremes, they become selfcaricatures. Ethics, harnessed to theological dogma, easily becomes a dead and a deadening ritualism. Esthetics, divorced from the realities of life and art, easily suggests every vice of prolonged in-breeding.

Such terms as "form" and "content," as we have seen, in their separateness as words suggest a false separateness as realities. The material of the artist is his content, his substance; but so is his form, his style. Form is not, in art, a mold into which material is poured and shaped. Form is part and parcel of the artist's material; material is part and parcel of the artist's form. Of the two he makes a living unity. 

When we say of a writer that he has an engaging style, we mean that he looks upon his world in such a way as to recreate it for us in his own image. That re-creation is far more than mere correctness of grammar applied to some chosen subject-matter. It is a world seen in only the way this particular artist can see it; the style that we admire is an organic part of the way in which he re-sees that world. The vision is the style; the style is the vision. Style is not removable or applicative, like a mask. It is not to be put on and taken off like a garment. We might say that the vision is the body, and that the style is the soul. Can you, outside of theology, part body and soul without killing the individual? Is not, in this only valid sense, soul an aspect of, a heightened function of, body, and not a separable entity? The wave is a form of the water. The water is the substance of the wave. Form and substance are one and the same.

Style and substance, form and content, in the arts are functions of the self-same material. Given the same subject for a tale or a poem, 10 artists will produce 10 different versions of the material. Not because their vocabularies are different; the difference in their vocabularies, and in their treatment of the material, will derive ultimately from the major and minor differences in their respective personalities. 

Whatever seeks to plane away these personal distinctions may make of these men better soldiers, better toilers, better [at least, more amenable] citizens; it will make them worse artists. It will make the world more efficient in a practical way, perhaps; it will give to action a more solid impact. This, however, it will achieve at the cost of robotization, regimentation, standardization. Man, sacrificed to a philosophy of the factory, himself becomes a factory-product. 

Making worse artists of men is not the remote catastrophe that it sounds. We are all artists. Artistry is a phase of the less restricted life. If art were the remote, impractical affair that the man-in-the-street too easily takes it for, it

would not be so quickly subject to the whims and the purposes of dictators.

In the same way that we are all artists, we are all critics. 

Criticism is inherent in all artistry, just as artistry is inherent in all criticism. Nor are we to form of criticism that immature conception which sees in it a sort of glorified faultfinding coupled with a schoolmarmish delight in assigning rank. Criticism does not judge or pontificate; it discriminates. 

It cannot ever become, in the laboratory sense, scientific, because it does not deal with measurable quantities. The scientific attitude, however, can very well be brought to bear upon criticism of the arts; in the past few years we have become, as a result of scientific investigations, much better informed as to the true nature of values in the arts. Between art and science there is a constant and vital interplay. Is this so strange? Would it not be, on the other hand, strange if it were not so? Can there be, between one aspect and other of the same activity, anything but vital interplay? Yes; when death supervenes.

Criticism is no more abstracted from the daily preoccupations of living than are ethics and esthetics. It is, indeed, a refinement upon them both. Its crudest, almost unconscious, form is, "I like this," or, "I don't like that." The moment one adds a "because" one has entered, however inexpertly, upon the path of formal criticism. It is experience, etymologically as well as vitally, that makes the expert.

There is another reason why dictators are, especially in the early phases of their rule, so harsh with the arts and all that pertains to them. Even the most abstract researches of the scientist seem to possess a far greater practical usefulness than do the arts. The arts, and the spirit of criticism that attends them, unfailingly, point away from utilitarianism. Dictators are interested chiefly in action-for-purpose. Their sole use for the arts, at first, is as part of a program for action. 

That which does not lend itself to this purpose is suppressed or destroyed. That which does lend itself to the dominant purpose is harnessed to the purpose even as the belt is harnessed in the factory, or the soldier to the ranks. 

Action demands physical power. The arts demand mental and emotional power. Action, from the social standpoint, is centripetal; it seeks the center of its goal. Art, from the social standpoint, is centrifugal, because it is individual. Even when it is enlisted—as there is no reason why it should not be—in a collective purpose, it remains individual. Thought shares with art this centrifugal, individualistic tendency. Thought is deferred, interrupted, even experimental, action. Emotion is deferred, interrupted, even frustrated, action. Thought and art are the blue-prints; action is the structure, much less alterable or revocable than the thoughts and emotions out of which it was built.

51 Under dictatorship, then, art preserves itself only at the cost of becoming utilitarian-at the cost, in other words, of less or more subtly submitting to denaturization. It surrenders its autonomy, and with that surrender goes the surrender of criticism, whether in the political or the artistic field. 

Criticism and the arts are dispossessed and banished to a small province where they await the opportunistic changes of policy by which they shall flourish or decline.

Life is full of dictatorships. When the arts are not fighting for their very existence, they are striving against the indifference of mankind. The same is true of the less spectacular forms of thought. Science is an exception more apparent than real. Because, when applied, it serves economic masters, it is, and very naturally, of pertinent interest to the commoner as well as to the exploiting master. That selfsame commoner, however, ceases to be common when he develops an interest in the less obvious researches of science. 

Let us defer for later chapters extended consideration of the dictatorship over the arts. Let us first look into that lesser dictatorship that goes by the name of censorship. 

2. CAPITALISM AND RELIGION 

No one lives without censorship. Part of our training for life in a complicated social structure consists in accepting restraints, as a necessary phase of dwelling in society. We very quickly learn that life, individual or social, imposes certain limitations. Wise living makes the attempt to discover which of those restraints are subject to the devices of human ingenuity, and which are inherent in the nature of things. 

There is no law against my running 10 miles every morning before breakfast; no law, that is, except the limitations of my organism. By training, perhaps, I could increase my prowess on the race-track. In the nature of things, however, there must be a limit. And if I am wise, I accept that limit, and exercise my wits within the area of my capabilities.

Freedom to achieve the impossible is not freedom at all; it is a jest, or a mockery. The limits of the mind, again in the nature of things, are far more elastic than the limits of the body. Thought easily outruns action. Because it is not action, however, little compulsion is put upon it until society faces a crisis, minor or major.

We are never so free, in any society, as we appear to be. 

During the late-and unfinished-war, men were arrested in these United States for reading, in public, the glorious symphonic, basic opening lines of the Declaration of Independence. 

To these immortal lines, which had so long seemed so schoolishly innocuous, events had suddenly given dynamitic potentiality. The rulers of the nation-oh yes, a democracy has rulers, and they are not the People-deemed it a dangerous time for

these lines really to mean what they said. Later cataclysms in Europe proved that their fears were not altogether without foundation.

Censorship is a necessity during war. Naturally, once given the excuse for exercising it, no administration is going to consider too nicely just how far it should go. The war measure serves as excuse for stamping out, at the same time, all dissident groups. 

Peace has its censorships, too. They may be, in origin, theological, pathological, economic. And it is strange how all three origins may combine, somehow, to favor the status of things as they are, against the progress toward things as they should be. When we are forbidden, in our plays, in our books, in our daily intercourse, to use certain words, to express certain ideas pertaining to sex, we are confronted with the power of the Church. Tolerance, as we have seen, somehow does not include the unbeliever, or the Freethinker. Theology, being in part a pathological condition, confers upon its censoriousness a pathological tinge. It fears sex; therefore we must not enjoy it. It abhors the body; therefore we must not cultivate it. 

It worships a Utopia in the sky; therefore we must not look to our heaven upon earth.

This attitude is well-understood by contemporary psychology and psychiatry, however much those part-sciences-in the persons of certain opportunistic individuals-may truckle to their "divine" origin. The theological mind, one of the great corruptive germs of modern civilization, may be likened to the drunkard who considers himself sober and everybody else intoxicated; it is sickness posing as the physician who brings health. Its attitude toward life is, almost by definition, negative; for the theologian's heaven itself is a denial of, a negation of, life. Life is merely a dark corridor to this eternal nonsense that is called eternal life. The theologian is, in Nietzschean language, the great Nay-Sayer.

Though, in this country, Church and State are in theory separated, in practice the one still infects the other. Our views upon, and our clinical treatment of, the problems of sex, .are poisoned-I know of no better word for the influence-by ecclesiastical superstition. In this respect, as in more than one other, Religion and Science are definitely at odds. Any progress must be bought at the price of ecclesiastical retreat. 

Happily, the Church, behind all its pompous proclamations, is retreating. It has begun-after its subjects have shown the way of tacitly ignoring their reverend shepherds-to countenance birth control. Someday, perhaps, it will even reach a state of enlightenment upon abortion, and learn from the sexual humanity of Soviet Russia.

Theologico-pathological censorship works into the hands of economic censorship. Whatever keeps the people meek postpones retributive or corrective action against the dominant

Isaac Goldberg 53 class. That dominant, exploiting class is, for all its external show, interested first of all in profits; only secondarily, if at all, in religion. The later history of Capitalism, indeed, is a soul-less caricature of the noblest aspirations of religion. Few are the church-leaders today, of any denomination, who would defend the Capitalist system outright.

Between Capitalism and Religion, in fact, there yawns a chasm almost as wide as between Science and Religion. For a different reason, however. Religion, it will be recalled, is an amalgam of superstition and ethical aspiration. He who combats religion combats, chiefly, the vast accretion of superstition that has all but submerged the ethical element. Now, Science is rapidly ridding religion of its superstition. This is a body-blow. On the other hand, Capitalism has made it increasingly impossible, in this world, to live up to the ethical standards enjoined by religion. This, so to speak, is a soulblow. Capitalism, moreover, whose breath is as corruptive as that of theology, if not even more corruptive, has begun to make it impossible for Science to live up to its lofty standards. 

Whenever profits are threatened it stifles invention, or buys it up; it directs the path of science into commercially profitable channels, and tends to deflect it from the direction of economic change.

Capitalism, like religion, is compelled to make its concessions to a growing enlightenment on the part of the commoner. 

This it does, together with religion, as a measure of expediency. 

Organized censorship, then, is an expression of social compulsion exercised by those in power. It is offensive because it comes from outside and is arbitrarily imposed. 

To live is to exercise self-censorship; to check one impulse in favor of another. To this no sensible person objects, because his sanction derives from himself. The price of expression is a certain amount of repression. It is good for us. 

It toughens our spiritual fiber. Do we have to explain to ourselves, at this stage, that one may be an Agnostic or an Atheist, and yet be spiritual? That one, indeed, cannot be "spiritual" unless one is free from the merely external, imposed, conventional, lifeless "spirituality" of theology? True spirituality-you may find a better name, if you object to the religious, mystical overtones of the word-is engendered in, and in turn engenders, selfhood. That which is individual in us objects to regimentation, even in the most glorious of causes. 

If, in such a cause, it accepts a necessary curtailment of activity, it does so with full knowledge of the cost, discounting the promise of a visioned future.

One may curtail the activities of one's body. The mind is not so easily controlled. Thinking may be directed, but not by the one who is doing the thinking-subjected. It is largely an involuntary function, almost as vital as the circulation of the blood; to interfere threatens stagnation and mental 

---

death. If, for example, Capitalism were absolutely consistent it would destroy the printing-press. The next logical step is to control it.

In the spectacle of the moving-picture industry we encounter a pretty demonstration of censorship working from the inside as well as from the outside, and in either case against the interests of popular enlightenment. 

3. THE "MOVIE" MIND When the League of Decency issued its pronouncement upon clean pictures, there was a great flurry in Hollywood. 

The Church, at last, had spoken. We know, from long and sad experience, what cleanliness means to the Church. It means, in so far as is possible in these days, sex-lessness, body-lessness, mind-lessness. Is it not strange that censorship of any kind does not work in the direction of wider freedom for the masses? Is it altogether an accident?

The moving picture industry, however, had exercised its own censorship long before the ecclesiastical powers and potentates had stepped in. As an industry, it was interested primarily in private profits. As an industry, it sought the widest distribution for its products. This meant that it must appeal to the average American mind. Or, would it be too incorrect to say, the average Universal Lack-of-mind?

That lack-of-mind was discovered to be symbolized by the 12-,13-,14-year old populace of the nation. The movies as an institution, as distinct from the real brains to be found among the directors, writers, actors and cameramen, are aimed at the age, interests and understanding of puberty and early adolescence. They are self-censored toward this fixation-a case of voluntary arrested development.

So that, when to this fundamental self-injury was added the insult of theological interference, some of us protested against the League of Decency on principle. On principle, mark you; for we had our own notions as to what was already the matter with the movies. They were about to be moved down from the puberty-level to the level of childhood. It is not at all an accident that, coincidental with the attack of the League, we were presented with a series of films in which children—even tots-figured in the leading roles. That, from this circumstance, a number of good pictures evolved, attests to the variety and fullness of life rather than to the wisdom of this now double censorship. Even so do wise and cultured men grow up despite our public school system, and despite some of the mentors that we let loose upon our defenseless youth.

What is so patent in the "intellectual" history of the movies represents the conditions in other types of popular amusement, in most public-school and college education, and in al-

Isaac Goldberg 55 most all the other phases of the emotional and intellectual life of the nation. Nor is this because Capitalism, like some huge ogre, presses down upon the various institutions and commands them to pervert our amusement and our education. 

Nor do Capitalism and Religion necessarily meet behind the scenes and plot the destruction of the common people. 

There is little doubt that such scheming individuals exist in the Church and the State. But the nature of Capitalist corruption is neither so personal nor so simple as all that. 

Whether for good or for evil, the influences of society are so enmeshed that the very impersonality of their power and of their impact asks from the scientific investigator a like impersonality of approach. Blaming individuals-pointing out their very definite connection with corruption-demanding their punishment is very pertinent in its way. We must not underestimate the importance of the salient individual, for evil or for good. This should not blind us, however, to the terrific compulsion of the social structure itself. 

The weakness of religious injunctions on behalf of the good life; the weakness of conventional social ethics; the weakness of reformism, lie alike in a predominantly individual approach to problems that are rooted in the social soil. One may have every intention in the world of being a model citizen, until one comes up against the choice between annihilation and conformity to the regnant social practice. Not the reigning ethics, as it exists on paper; but the reigning practice. To be social-minded is to realize that social evils demand social treatment.

In our better art, as in our more discerning history and biography, this attitude is mirrored in the abolition of sharply defined heroes and villains. To be sure, the world still has its villains and its heroes, but they are understood in relation to the life out of which they were born. Our great men and women no longer are paragons of virtue or horrible examples of vice; they are human admixtures of failure and triumph.

They do not stand apart from the rest of humanity, like a special genius; they are, instead, our very selves, raised to higher powers of good or evil. "There, but for the grace of God" [which one may translate, there but for the caprice of heredity and environment] "go I."

This thought should give us a little humility, or, at least, make us pause a while before we distribute, with selfrighteousness, praise or blame. Living in a practical world, we find it necessary to control individual expansion for the sake of social security; we find it, therefore, necessary to reward and punish, as if the individual alone were responsible for his deeds. As we become more enlightened, we recognize the reciprocal influence of man and his surroundings, of man as unit and mankind as group.

Any revolutionary party, through the very passion of its revolt, tends to personalize its hostility and the object of that hostility. This, of course, without reference to the justice of its cause, with which I am not at the moment concerned. 

All opposition, conservative or radical, tends to partake of this nature. In individuals it leads to what is known as a persecution mania-the illusion that one is being set upon by enemies. That entire nations may be afflicted by persecution mania is to be seen in the history of Germany since the accession of Hitler. In the same way-psychologically speaking-it is too easy to rouse the United States, especially with a vicious press ready to help, into the fantastic fears of a red invasion. 

In the U. S. S. R., for all the historical, scientific, impersonal rigors of the Marxian dialectic, popular notions of Capitalism rest upon individualistic conceptions. The ability to face a problem in abstract terms is as foreign to most people as is the ability to deal with the higher abstractions of mathematics.

To the average American, the democracy is summed up in George Washington, Abraham Lincoln, and a few other national figures. In Soviet Russia of today, the Socialist-Communist revolution is summed up in the figures, first, of Stalin, then of Lenin, and, only in the end, of Karl Marx. One is reminded of certain Christian regions of Italy, in which the chief object of worship is the local saint, then Mary, and only in the third place, Christ.

I wonder whether Soviet Russia is not the first country in history to introduce, among the most humble of its people, under the terrible odds that it must invariably encounter, the corrective virtues of abstract economic doctrine. Much of its success, thus far, must be illusory. The historic past has a way of maintaining its hold in ways peculiar and potent. 

Images of Lenin and Stalin replace the old ikons; in effect, however, it is ikons that they continue to be. One does not root out the element of worship by rooting out the object of that worship. Worship creates its objects, and re-creates, until the emotional sources have been reached and purified. 

4. OUR SUPERFICIAL CIVILIZATION 

Now, to return after this longish detour to the problems of censorship, there is usually an element in it of the presecution mania.

The abstract principles involved in it become personalized, just as, in the first days of history, mankind personalized the mysterious forces of nature. In religion, for that matter, which is a vestigial carry-over from these earliest days, the personalization persists, often in a most inglorious, not to say unscientific, manner. The fear of persecution breeds retaliatory persecution. We burn or hang our witches, after projecting upon

Isaac Goldberg 57 them our own psychological terrors. We go heresy-hunting. 

We call for the reaffirmation of loyalty to God and nation. We demand of our teachers pledges of allegiance. We go on the scent for smut. We demand the deportation of alien radicalsi. e., we cry out for the removal of all who, by holding views other than our own, threaten our supremacy, then our security. 

We are beset by enemies; let us crush them. 

All the hard-won conquests of civilization, which consist precisely in the understanding and the control and the eradication of such mental states as these, go by the board at the moment when serious change appears. Might again becomes right. In the long run, under the surface, has it ever really ceased to be?

Social life is thus a constant challenge to, and a constant threat to, the restraining, deliberative influences of the brain, which came upon the scene so relatively late in the evolution of the individual. Civilization is an unending tug of war between the brain and the primitivity of the body. Thinking delays action. Rulers, for this reason, ask of masses not thought, but ratification. The brain itself, to most of mankind, is almost an intrusive inconvenience, so that side by side with our lip-service to intelligence goes a half-concealed suspicion of it—a resentment that can, under special conditions, become a hatred. 

Brains, intelligence, independent thinking, today are hated in Germany, the country in which for so long they were preeminent. The Church has always frowned upon the independent use of the mind; it has, as a matter of natural policy, resisted mentality. The measure of its power, indeed, has always been the measure of that resistance. Even in Soviet Russia, which is avowedly dedicated to the education of the masses, education has taken largely the form of indoctrination. 

That one prefers the doctrines of the U. S. S. R. to those of 

Hitler, Mussolini or any particular church, does not alter the essential observation. However glorious the cause in which the hopes of man are enlisted, he finds himself soon or late stifling the independence of thought, and treating the opposition to silence, exile or slaughter. As crises rise, the level of our civilization falls. The culture of centuries disappears over-night, and the one remaining use of the brain is to rationalize to provide reasons for, to justify, to extol, to make seem inevitable-the regression to savagery. 

At the basis of that savagery is man's hunger for food and man's hunger for sex-the passion for self-preservation and that second hunger, the lust for the flesh. The madness for power may well be rooted in both these appetences, especially the first. Power is assurance of survival. Whatever is at the basis of man's primitive self is at the basis of his idealism. 

This fact has been used by persons who call themselves cynics to justify their attitude toward life. I have no desire to

moralize with, or preach to, cynics; I should like merely to point out that the foundation is not the whole structure—that the source is not the river-that the alphabet is not the book --that origins, however deeply involved with results, are not in themselves the results.

BOOK-FLOODED U. S. S. R. 

The burning of the books in Berlin, on May 10, 1933, was an appropriate symbol. It was an auto-da-fe of intellectual freedom. It was more; as it derived its form from a day when men and women, not their works, were burned in the flesh, so it pointed forward to a day of actual, not symbolic, execution -to the return of dueling, to the return of the headsman, to a general reversion in the direction of brutality and ruthlessness. The history of the Third Reich, since that day, has borne out amply the promise of this revolting conflagration. 

It is true, and it is well to repeat, that man may burn his fellow-man, and the works of his fellow-man, but that ideas are proof against flame and flood. It is a cause for melancholy, none the less, to watch how unerringly all dictatorship gravitates, whatever the reasons alleged, toward the suppression of the intellect.

When German students lighted the pyre of print-German students, recall that sent up in flames the Bible and the writings of such disparate minds as Karl Marx, Helen Keller, Sigmund Freud, Heinrich Heine, Arnold Schoenberg, Upton 

Sinclair, Franz Werfel, and a company distinguished chiefly for their liberality, their humaneness, their aspiration toward a more equitable world, they signed the death-warrant of intellectual and academic freedom.

They might, with equal appropriateness, have dumped in the works of Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, the great cultural banner of Germany. For the holocaust of books and the spirit that moved the pyromaniacs was a stout repudiation of all that Goethe had lived for in the realm of the free, the international, mind.

Goethe stood for the broadening of national into international outlook. He did not ask for the surrender of nationality; he did uphold, however, the pooling of intellectual resources into a vast universal culture. This was almost a logical necessity, in view of the multiple interests that engaged him throughout his long life. Goethe's all-inclusive mind naturally conceived of a Welt-Literatur-a world-literature. The mind embraces internationality long before human institutions, requiring the agreement of many minds, indorse it. Goethe, whatever the limitations of his outlook revealed by later social enlightenment, belongs in the line of the world's "universal" 59

men; he is one of those great individuals who stands, not so much apart from, as a significant product of, the collectivity. 

Goethe represented, and championed, the interpenetration of cultures. He did not regard Germany as a Chosen People of 

Kultur. He opened windows upon Europe rather than closed doors. He did not discover, in any single country, an absolute culture to which all other nations must pay the homage of imitation. He reaffirmed the democracy of the human spirit. 

Goethe has been repudiated in Germany not alone by inference, but by direct statement. The Bavarian minister without portfolio, Esser, on July 1, 1933, stated the Nazi case succinctly Goethe was "guilty" of being "internationally minded" and a "stranger to his people."

Is it surprising, then, that the recent history of the German university has been a caricature of learning? Dictatorship means the return of might as right; the exalting of action over thought; the suppression, the degradation of the intellect. 

For the intellect is that part of us which asks questions, which weighs answers, which calls for deliberation. The intellect does not fit readily into uniforms.

The University was once the pride of Germany. The freedom of the academic mind, of the university professor, was its glorious boast. The University, here as elsewhere, symbolized one of mankind's greatest conquests: the untrammeled range of the inquiring spirit. It stood for dedication to truth, for that virtue which even today is perhaps the last quality of the intelligence to find appreciation and understanding: disinterestedness, which, far from being lack of interest, or aloofness, is a sort of ubiquitous alertness-an attitude above partisanship, special pleading, or facile rationalization. I shall return to this. Indeed, I am trying never to leave it. 

Upon this highest achievement of the human spirit dictatorship frowns. I do not believe that the Russian bureaucracy, for all that may be urged against it, has in this respect sinned as brutally as have Hitler, Mussolini, and their satraps. 

In the very conception of the classless society, indeed, lies the glorification of a certain disinterestedness. I am not defending the treatment that has been meted out to the intellectual in Russia, or the attitude toward the intellectual that American Communists have taken over from Moscow's vacillating bureaucrats. I am not condoning the treatment of the recalcitrant peasants. I believe, as will later appear, that the Soviet conception of the arts and their relation to social problems is, in important respects, fallacious. Nevertheless, the official 

Germany and Italy of today are of a far lower caliber than official Soviet Russia. It is a tribute to human inconsistency, and perhaps a warning to the tacticians of the U. S. S. R., that on the surface the regimentation of Germany and the regimentation of the Soviet republics should look so much alike.

The German university, representing the flower of the German intellect, has become an arm of politics. The lower learning has conquered the higher. The tribal suspicion of the superior man, the dictatorial mistrust of the free-functioning intelligence, has clamped down its power upon the academic life.

To science and to art are dictated the discoveries that they shall make.

Art and science exist now, in Germany and Italy, for the ratification of the official prejudice. Not even mathematics is left unregimented. It is for this reason that one discerns, through the darkness of conditions in the U. S. S. R., a ray of light that does not penetrate the Nazi gloom.

The Nazi and Fascist dictatorships face backward. The 

Soviet dictatorship faces forward. For the Nazi and the Fascist the State is absolute. For the Communist the State is a trustee, until that day-however distant-when it shall have legislated itself out of existence, and ushered in the classless society a society that has been purged of exploitation for private profit. The Nazis rationalize exploitation; the Communists rationalize expropriation. Yet, amid the turmoil, the poverty, the regimentation of Soviet Russia, every step taken by the government points to the sincerity of its avowed motives. Through the blood of its errors it makes progress toward the final emancipation. Emancipation, to the Nazi, is neither a goal nor a path.

That path, in the U. S. S. R., is strewn with victims of bureaucracy. Yet, with all the criticism that is to be leveled

It is not an accident of dictatorial psychology that in Germany books are burned, and that the production of literature has diminished, while in the U. S. S. R. the book, as such, is glorified, and book-production, as well as the arts in general, flourish.

This is to be seen in the respective treatments of religion. 

Germany's repudiation of Christianity is not a repudiation of the superstitious, degrading element in all theologies. It discards one theological structure for another, because that other better rationalizes its renewed cult of the warrior. Christianity--in theory, at least-exalted peace. The U. S. S. R., in repudiating all religion, acts in accordance with a deeply-rooted conviction that religion has long been the opiate of the people, debasing their minds with savage demonologies, instilling hatred of heretics, obscurng the realities of this life with illusory pictures of the hereafter, creating an humble psychology in the working classes and serving for all these reasons as one of the great mainstays of the entrenched exploiters. 

It is not to the point, for the moment, that this view of religion may be incomplete and unjust. The new religion of

I doubt that any religion of the recognized type has had a nobler purpose.

Whatever errors the Russians have committed they have sought to correct. Certainly they have not been heistant about changing tactics from year to year. Psychologically, perhaps, they are still the savage tribesmen that they were. Scratch a Russian and find a Tartar, runs the saying. Why not "Scratch a German and find a Hun"? Why not "Scratch any 'civilized' man and find a barbarian”?

Ludendorff's abjuration of Christianity is neither so terrible nor so important as it sounds. Who-in war-is a Christian, or anything else under the sun but a man whose chief

purpose is to kill? What difference is there between the death meted out by a bullet shot from a Pagan gun, a Christian gun, a Mohammedan gun, or a Buddhist gun? In war, do not the official priests, of whatever belief, bless the cannon and the man who touches it off, and the cause in which he is enlisted? 

War is a religion in which all other creeds are merged in the single creed of murder. Inter arma silent musae, or, as we should say, when the cannon roar the muses are speechless. When the cannon roar, the religions, for all their lofty professions, are likewise speechless, unless they find-as usual voice for the side of their respective masters. 

Ludendorff, thus openly proclaiming the attitude of all warriors, on whatever side, is simply more honest than his fellow militarists. Besides, it is the privilege of a nation to adopt whatever religion it desires. Do we not live, for the moment, at peace with a world of the East in which Christianity is not the official religion? 

Have not these Eastern nations, from practical experience with the professions and the practices of the Christian nations, a right to question the paradox that is Christianity? A nation, a people, has a right to its own gods. 

The U. S. S. R., out of the logic of history and economics, proclaims an atheist state. It does not, as has been so widely roared, deny the observance of the Greek Orthodox ritual to its people, unless they are enrolled members of the Communist party. Are we not on good terms with the U. S. S. R., and is the creed or non-creed of any nation a reason for severing amicable relations? Or is toleration nothing more, really, than a beautiful sound?

I am trying to separate deeds from names; realities from labels. The regression of the German nation to heathenism would be, on the whole, a calamity to the world. It would, if consistent, bring into the open the brutality that is at the bottom of all war-machines, regardless of the creeds under which they are enlisted. It would compel competitors to become as primitive, as brutal, as ruthless as the new paganism with which they will be contending.

They should remember, however, that had the Versailles treaty been dictated by a truly "Christian" spirit, they might not now be contemplating a Europe ready to flare up into a vast holocaust. The entire situation, on the part of Germany and of her enemies, stinks with hypocrisy. For all the undoubted sincerity of the individual worshipers concerned, religion, as exploited by the various powers, is part of that cosmic stench.

For, at bottom, the contending powers are actuated by a religion that arises above the minor creeds as an emperor rises over the petty kings of his empire. To regard the conflicts from the standpoint of the lesser contentions is to be lost in the true perspective.

That religion is Capitalism-the secular religion whose true and living and mystic god is Private Profit. Its altars

are international markets. Its priests are magnates. Its sacrificial victims are the people. 

I do not wish to state the case in terms too personal. I do not believe, as I have already written, that Capitalism is an ogre bent maliciously upon the subjection of the human race. 

Or that capitalists, as a class, are monsters in the service of that ogre. If economic determinism holds for the proletariat it must hold as well for the bourgeoisie and for the capitalist class. Within the circle of our individual responsibilities we are alike the victims or the beneficiaries of a pattern that is woven out of human weakness and human strength and human fallibility. This is not to say that there are no villains of history and no heroes. How can we say this in a universe that has thrown up, from the sea of humanity, such tidal waves as Lenin, Hitler, Mussolini? History is not at the caprice of the individual, however; he may shunt it off the track, oppose its direction [which need not always mean progress]; in the end, none the less, he appears as the wave, not the oceanthe human symbol, not the human destiny. 

That destiny, if there be any vital meaning to a word so drenched with theological implications, derives from man's position in the socio-economic plan. He is, in the simplest terms, a "have-not" or a "have" who lives by exploiting the "have-nots." For all the constant shifting of membership that goes on among the various classes-the haves, the have-nots and those wavering uncertainly in the middle the class-types themselves remain well-defined. The threats and the solaces of religion are, in the face of the solaces and threats of social and economic position, unreal. Economic conflict soon or late puts all the other conflicts into their proper, subordinate places in the scheme of things. Only man's intellect and only the intellect of the exceptional man-can rise above the intricate network of implications contained in his manner of making a living-in, consequently, his economic status. 

When Pagan fights Christian, when Shintoist fights Atheist, when even brother fights brother as in the Civil War that divided our North from our South, and still divides them with reverberations more or less delicate, it is a fight at bottom for the means of making a living. Nothing more noble, nothing less essential, than that. Making a living, or insuring control over the materials out of which a living is made. . . . 

Control over materials whose possession will insure, in turn, control over the subordinate classes. Control over the middle and the lower class from whose toil derive the private profits of the dominant economic system.

3. UTOPIA THEOLOGY AS DICTATORSHIP AND VICE VERSA 

I have heard members of more than one cult glibly dismiss all talk of collectivist economy with the word, "Utopia"; just as, by common agreement, we dismiss the religions of older civilizations (the Greek and Roman, for example) as mythologies. VI. Propaganda 1. ART AND CLASS 

Propaganda is another of those words that we owe to the church. Today, however, it refers to the spreading of any doctrine. More, it has acquired certain overtones that are not heard in the dictionary definition.

Propaganda is a weapon for spreading indoctrination. Its etymology emphasizes the spreading; propagation, for example, is the spreading, the multiplication, of the species. Its use, however, emphasizes that which is spread-the doctrine. During the War, it revealed its true character as that of a prejudiced, lop-sided, juggled account; its intention was not to enlighten but rather to obscure-to present, in exaggeration, everything favorable to its cause and to minimize or to forget anything unfavorable. Propaganda seeks, not the truth, but adherents. It is only secondarily an instrument of enlightenment; it is an instrument of conversion. It is not Justice; it is the District Attorney. Moreover, it is not interested in the calm qualities of thought; it seeks converts to action. 

There is a subtler aspect of propaganda, which it is worth while to investigate. It will make it easier to understand the 

Communist criticism of the arts, as practiced under Capitalism and under Communism, respectively. 

Art is not an activity in a vacuum. Even the most aloof forms that it may take-and aloofness in art, despite anything you may hear to the contrary, does not necessarily mean aloofness from the interests of flesh-and-blood humanity-have a social connotation. And by that same token, it should be remembered, they have an individual connotation. 

As the product of an individual, art sinks roots in biology and psychology. As the product of a society-the society of which that individual is a component-art derives sustenance, but not all its sustenance, from the socio-economic structure of the epoch. The inter-relation between these factors is as complex as that between the human mind and the human body, and quite as unshatterable. Yet, as we anatomize the body, so we anatomize the arts.

Historically considered, for example, the arts show a movement parallel with that of the social forms. It is not an accident that the lower classes did not appear in the arts until they had begun to acquire significance and power in society. 

Even the language of the every-day world was absent from the drama until a little more than a hundred years ago, when Vic- 73

tor Hugo could boast that he had made a king ask, in just so many words, what time it was.

The artist, usually without realizing it, takes the social substratum for granted. Unless, of course, he is led to revolt against certain features in art or in society. Revolutions in art have had a habit of running generally parallel-though not always precisely in time-with marked changes in the social structure. Should this be so surprising? Changes in society are caused by, and in turn cause, changes in the individual. 

There is a point in this complicated relationship at which the balance is impaired and a new equilibrium is called for. 

The most important thing in life is keeping alive. The most important element in the social structure is the economic organization-the way in which one makes his living, and, as a consequence, the economic class in which one finds oneself. 

I believe it is the virtue of great art that, while it exemplifies this complicated relationship, it also rises above it. 

This is what we mean, indeed, by universality. Concerning the more purely esthetic significance of this universality I shall speak later; for the moment I call attention to the socioeconomic implications of the arts. 

Art, then, inevitably displays the characteristics of a class instrument. Or, let us put it this way: art lends itself to exploitation by economic classes. This it does in both a negative and a positive way. By assumption, by simply absorbing the contemporary milieu, it may take for granted not only the existence of things as they are but the rightness of things as they are. This, of course, is a negative attitude. It may, on the other hand, so present its story, its picture, as to justify or condemn that state of things.

This is not the whole of art, by any means. Essentially, it is not even a major function or purpose of art. It is, nevertheless, one of the effects, one of the influences, of art. It is the type of effect and influence that persons with a major interest in economics would naturally seize upon and exaggerate. 

In the same way, during the early hey-day of psychoanalysis, persons with a predominantly psychological interest seized upon the psychological aspects of the arts, exaggerating the general importance of these aspects as well as their particular significance. This may not be scientific; it is quite natural, however. 

Now, when we are first confronted with an example of a self-consciously new effort in the arts, especially one that involves the recognition of a new trend in social ideas, we ourselves become self-conscious. Whether the artistry of the piece-let us assume that it is a play-is impeccable or inexpert, we are very likely to cry out in protest, "Propaganda!"

The words, in this case, may just as conceivably be the token of a class-reaction as of a dramatic critique. There may very well be, in countless plays that we accept without protest,

Isaac Goldberg 75 a strong element of "propaganda"; but it happens to be propaganda that accepts the status quo, and therefore, though it may vitiate the play quite as much as any propaganda enlisted in a different cause, we pass it over. 

Even the most stupid of us resents the notion that something is "being put over"-that we are being taught something in a devious manner-that, for all the sugar-coating, it is a pill that we have swallowed. This sense of smuggling we feel much more keenly when unfamiliar ideas are in question. Almost everybody seems to possess the rudiments of an artistic feeling that rebels against the more obvious forms of propaganda. 

Almost everybody, that is, seems to recognize the aim of propaganda, when it does not happen to be enlisted on one's own particular side. It is altogether human that propaganda should, at best, tend to gloss over any elements that would react unfavorably upon a prospective convert. It is equally human that propaganda should, at best unconsciously and at worst very consciously, "load the dice" in favor of its plea. 

This occurs not only with politicians, but with men who, by profession and intention, observe a rigid austerity toward their labors. Even a scientist, with however slight a bias toward this interpretation or that, will unconsciously veer toward his favored interpretation. Of course, being a scientist, he will have set up a complicated process by which he is protected against such facile surrender. "The truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth," which in court trials is practically a comedy, and in real life an impossible-because humanly unattainable-ideal, is the goal of perfection toward which science points its gaze.

In this respect, so far as concerns the different realm of the arts, the artist is actuated by an ideal more picturesque to the unimaginative, but no less austere at bottom, than that of the scientist. If he is as lofty as his ideals, he regards the world with a certain objectivity, despite the heightened subjectivism of the artist as compared with the scientist. Whatever the truth may be-and wise men have always said that there are truths, but no Truth-his approach to it is as untrammeled as human fairness can make it. 

The artist, however, from the very nature of his pursuit and of his temperament, is more exposed to the temptation of returning a biased report upon his experiences. He is more likely to gloss over those experiences; to "load the dice" so that they will roll to the right combination of numbers. This, as I say assuming that we are dealing with honest men and women-is largely an unconscious procedure. One of the many problems of criticism is to disengage the extraneous elements from the fundamental-to reveal the artist in the process of deceiving himself to extricate, in other words, the propaganda from the essence of his work.

Naturally, when a man sets out deliberately to color his report we are dealing with a different, and a simpler, problem. 

In discussion of questions that acquire terrific heat from their contemporaneousness and the results that hinge upon their settlement this way or that, men abandon all pretense of fairness; they even deride its virtues. They reveal their bias frankly; they no longer seek purely to convince; they would, if possible, herd or dragoon their opponents, or their more deliberative companions, into uniformity. 

2. PROVING TOO MUCH 

I do not want to be misunderstood as condemning, without qualification, all propaganda as such. Discussion of principles was never more important than it is today. And, as one would presume, that discussion is at its most acrid and least scientific in the field of political and economic debate. The passions of self-interest are far more tempestuous than those of artistry, because they reach down to the very roots of life and concern the bare maintenance of that life. 

Propaganda does not, moreover, unless carried away by the fervors of religious or economic fanaticism, represent itself as a perfect solution. We proceed, not from imperfection to perfection, but from the less efficient to the more efficient. A cause, to win adherents by the regular process of discussion and conviction, does not have to prove that it is perfection; it need prove only that it is better than what it seeks to replace.

Proving too much is an ancient temptation, and one to which human nature is especially addicted. It is a failing that characterizes all propaganda. It also acts as a boomerang, implanting suspicion and aversion instead of confidence and conviction. The skilful propagandist, especially one who is innerly convinced of the side that he has espoused, should be a careful psychologist. Admission of weakness may be one of the greatest of his strengths.

Propaganda deals entirely with substance, with matter, with content. It flouts the particular artistry of manner, style, form. Propaganda has a "message" to deliver, openly or in disguise. This is why, unless it is well done, it fails of its complete purpose. Humanity, so much in need of learning, so clamorous for instruction, yet resists being taught. The sermon, even to many church-goers, is a symbol of boredom, not only because usually it is a bore, but because it is a moralistic lecture because, once you know the denomination in which the minister is enrolled, you know the tone, the color, the content and the direction of his sermon. The element of indoctrination has entered; this in itself is a static bore. The element of surprise has been eliminated, which adds to the boredom.

77 Unless, of course, one is oneself so impassioned by the doctrine that simply to hear it restated is to be reconfirmed in its virtues.

This is the explanation of the wild applause that used to greet the hero of melodrama when he appeared, in the nick of time, to unbind the heroine from the railroad tracks, thus saving her from the sure destruction that was rushing upon her in the shape of a locomotive at 60 miles per hour. This explains, no less, the outburst of applause that greets a political play, attended by the intelligentsia, when the strikers are saved from capitulating to the bosses by the heroic sacrifice or strategy of the socialistic or communistic hero. What this applause means is "Hurrah! You're on my-therefore the right-side!"

It is, in other words, indorsement of propaganda. The rightness of that propaganda does not enter into the discussion at this point. Such simple statement and elementary indorsement do not happen to represent the highest form of human emotion or human thought. To over-simplify is, for these who really wish to know, to falsify. Once again there is a victory. for the lower learning; and that adjective applies, we must remember, not to the class out of which the learner comes but to the attitude that he brings to his experiences. 

In the chapters that follow I shall discuss more specifically the question whether, and to what extent, propaganda belongs in the arts . . . whether art is propaganda, or should be whether man's mind can live by propaganda alone. 

Here I am concerned with whether propaganda may be art, whether artistry belongs in propaganda. ...

There is no activity to which artistry does not apply, if only as the preferable difference between doing a thing badly and doing it as well as the conditions allow. 

I remember days, back in 1914-1918, when I would spend long hours arguing for more stylistic care in presenting, by means of pamphlets or of newspaper articles, Socialist propaganda. In those days it was as socially hazardous to be a Socialist and on occasion hazardous politically as well, as it is in these days to be a Communist. The comrades, with their prejudice against anything that smacked of bourgeois attainments, abhorred grand opera, full-dress suits [into which I have never been able to insert myself, out of a similar prejudice!] and literary style. In vain I argued that style was not merely an external application-that it was a form of high efficiency, and that it would make propaganda more palatable to those who with justice were regarded as prospective converts. They could not conceive that style meant something other than long or unfamiliar words, pretentious statement, "offish" lingo. Style was a class-i. e., an upper-class-frill.

The wise propagandist adapts his language to the capabilities of his ideal audience. Many a workingman of pre-war

days was alienated from collectivist thought by the startling, foreign, forbidding vocabulary of its sponsors. "Proletariat" is still a word that proletarians do not understand. "Dictatorship of the proletariat," however beautifully it may be explained in the economics of Marx, adds confusion to puzzlement. I am not commenting upon the validity of the idea; I am discussing the legitimate problems of the propagandist. 

The phrase, "dictatorship of the proletariat," pulls in two contrary directions; on the one hand it suggests, in "proletariat," the liberation of the downtrodden, while in "dictatorship" it suggests a clamping-down upon liberties. "Bourgeoisie," besides being difficult for the untrained reader to pronounce, is foreign in more senses than one. 

I have read so-called proletarian fiction that was as mannered, as difficult to get through, as any "bourgeois," affected style. Many of the propagandistic tracts of Communism read as if they were addressed, not to toilers, but to college students, and to the brighter boys of the class, at that. I am not seeking to glorify a too simplified language, and most certainly I am not upholding that over-simplification [about which there is often something dishonest] against which I was talking a few chapters back. I am insisting that propaganda requires a special adaptation of language and approach to the needs of the person who is to be convinced. It is a procedure that deals with elementary data. American propagandists have much to learn from Soviet writers such as Ilyin, who manage to simplify while reducing the sacrifice of essentials to a minimum. 

To ask of propaganda a severely balanced account of pros and cons is to request it, on the noblest grounds, to commit suicide. Such an ideal is, in mathematical language, a limit for it to approach. Propaganda, really, should convince nobody; it should put one in a frame of mind that makes further investigation seem worth while. It should invert the famous 

Shakespearean line about winning us with honest trifles and deceiving us in deepest consequence. No sensible propagandist desires to convince everybody. "There are persons," wrote a famous essayist, "who have no right to agree with me."

Propaganda, at bottom, is a utility-form rather than an art. It drives onward toward specific conclusions, whereas the better type of art deals with the emotions and the thoughts as adventures in experience. Art tends to deepen and to broaden one's acquaintance with the world and with people. Propaganda tends to concentrate the attention upon a small sector, to the exclusion of the rest of the circle. This is not to deny the proper place of propaganda in the service of a cause; it is to insist, however, that the arts are not that proper place. 

Propaganda might be described as the shock-troops of a cause. Not everybody can write it, or read it, or care for it. 

However deep the intelligence that may have been brought to bear upon the cause that propaganda proclaims, propaganda

79 as such presents a subtle assault upon the intellect by coloring its case. This is true, as I have said, especially in political and economic conflict. We resist it, or we yield to it, in accordance with our personal bias. To it we are asked to bring a free mind; to it, in too many cases, a free mind has not been brought in the first place. 

Am I demanding a counsel of perfection? Am I asking too much of fallible human beings? I am. By asking the impossible we stand a chance of receiving a greater proportion of the possible.

I am not presenting the scientist and the artist as creatures altogether apart from the propagandist. Self-interest (which is by no means always a form of hypocrisy) blinds us all to facts that make us uncomfortable, and lends us a sharp vision for the things we wish to see. It even makes us see those things when they do not happen to be present, and not see them when they patently are.

It is interesting to watch modern thinkers in their progress toward the de-emotionalization not only of our thought, but of our vocabulary. Watson with his Behaviorism; Kohler with his Gestalt philosophy; Pareto with his attempt to suggest a reduction of sociological problems to quasi-mathematical formulae . . . and all of them in a search for words that shall be emptied as much as possible of emotional references. 

It is interesting to see how, without any philosophical or intellectual aims, the various dictatorships, too, make of man a more or less denatured symbol in an algebraic equation. 

Propaganda, particularly when it rolls outside its proper orbit, shows these same tendencies. For all its appeal to the emotions rather than to the head; for all its emphasis upon content rather than upon form; for all its personalization of abstract forces such as religion, morality, bourgeoisie or capitalism, it flattens the dimensions of both the abstract forces and the human being who is under their spell. 

When an artist, impassioned by the vision of a new truth, seeks to proclaim that vision in a novel or a play, it is not so surprising that he gives life to his ideas at the expense of the life in his characters. For, deep in his sources of creativeness, it is the ideas rather than the life of his characters that prompt his work. In propaganda novels it is a common observation that the secondary characters are more alive than the protagonists. This should not be surprising. The protagonists stand for the author's "message." They are less people than animated principles. . . . To propaganda we look for ideas; to the arts, for human experiences. This is not a description of two worlds that never can meet; it is a description of the same world from two different points of view.

3. PROPAGANDA AS IDEOLOGY 

Emotions are older than abstract ideas. Great works of art do not stand or fall by their content-by their ideological framework. Were art merely a repository of ideas, to be judged by the scientific, historic or economic validity of those ideas, how would we be able to explain the persistence of Homer, the 

Greek tragedians, Dante, Shakespeare, Goethe?

We shall have an opportunity to go further into the implications of this question when we come to the consideration of the Materialist Dialectic as applied-and misapplied-by those who would make of Communism a measuring-rod of the arts. Let us be content, for the moment, to say that propaganda, as distinguished from the arts, does stand or fall by its ideational content-by what it propounds as doctrine. 

An exact equation between propaganda and art is therefore impossible. Both are realities, yet they are essentially incommensurable. Does this mean-with specific reference to the Materialist Dialectic-that there is no essential relationship between the arts and the economic configuration? By no means. Just what the nature of that relationship may be we defer, again, to the chapters that follow. 

We may set down this much, however: just as it would be wrong to evaluate the worth of propaganda by any artistry of utterance that had been brought to bear upon it, so would it be wrong to evaluate the worth of artistry by the truth or untruth of the ideas that underlie it. Propaganda is predominantly a vehicle of ideas. By the demonstrability of those ideas, regardless of the manner in which they have been presented, expertly or inexpertly, the propaganda is properly judged. A beautiful girl may very sweetly and smilingly assure me that two and two are seven, and use all her charms to convince me.

All that I can reply is that two and two are four, and that, whatever it may have to do with the case, she is a beautiful girl. Art does not deal fundamentally with ideas. It may, so to speak, aver that two and two are seven, and yet, by means of this false mathematics or despite it, reach something that has emotional validity. The reasons that we give for what we do are never as certain, as demonstrable, as the specific thing that we do. Love, for example, is real, and certainly it is earnest, but reason is neither its source nor its goal. The arts are quite in the position of love.

As a matter of fact, propaganda finds itself in much the same boat. However cogent the reasoning behind it, we cannot be too sure that it states the whole case. Propaganda, willynilly, may partake of the nature of art, just as does science. 

And, by one of those basic human paradoxes, the effect of art, even if not the intention, may be to convince one's reasoning rather than one's emotions.

We should, nevertheless, since this is a world of action

Isaac Goldberg 81 as well as of thought, be on guard against the numbing influence of too much philosophical paradox. Let me give an example or two of what I mean.

In a brilliant book, Reality and Illusion, by Richard Rothschild, I find the statement that ". . . a spirit of 'free and unprejudiced inquiry' in any field is possible only within a framework of basic premises no less arbitrary than those in such fields as politics, art and religion. Thought can never be more than rational, that is, it can never be more than a process of relating things to each other so that the totality represents a consistent and unified whole. And religious systems may in this sense be no less rational than scientific generalizations, as witness the elaborate and thoroughgoing rationalizations in Roman Catholicism." Again, a few pages later, Mr. Rothschild writes, "Now, in the midst of a world crisis, we are asking ourselves what it has all been about. With all our knowledge of means, we have no large ends to which to be loyal. All we can think of is to redistribute the world's wealth, prevent war, and such proximate objectives. This is not to say that economic and political reforms cannot profitably be inaugurated. Yet it is difficult to identify such reforms with what anyone would call a 'fuller' life for the average man. To eliminate the fear of hunger and the fear of war may be worthwhile ambitions. They have however very little to do with the fulfillment of life itself."

Now, I think I know what Mr. Rothschild means. Yet I cannot see why, because no single system can hope to be perfect, we should yield to the thoroughgoing rationalizations of Roman Catholicism or Capitalism, and why, in the interests of a world more worthy of our larger loyalties, we should not prefer the thoroughgoing rationalizations of Atheism or Collectivism. We proceed from superstition of one sort to superstition of another; let that pass. Is this any reason for not preferring the less obstructive superstition-the "superstition" of science to that of religion-the superstition of collectivism to that of rampant individualism?

It is possible, in one's highly laudable desire for fairness, to lean so far back as to become unfair. This may correspond, in its way, to the propagandist who achieves his special brand of unfairness by leaning too far forward.

I cannot understand how Mr. Rothschild, writing in a world that has become, willy-nilly, more and more socialized-in a world whose parts have become more and more interdependent, can state that the elimination of the fear of hunger and the fear of war have "very little to do with the fulfillment of life itself." How, in a world such as that in which we live today, are we to make the first steps toward such a fulfillment if we are blocked by agencies that make life itself so insecure? I understand very well the resources of the spirit, the beauties of an integration that is based upon one's sense of belonging

to a meaningful universe-a universe of deeper realities than those represented by tangible things. 

Life fulfills itself in many ways. Its fulfillment, however, becomes an ironic mockery when it is frustrated at the very first step, which is to preserve life. Must we not make sure that there will be a life to fulfill? 

4. ARTIST AND EVANGELIST 

It will be guessed, then, that I do not agree with Mr. Kenneth Burke's statement (page 202 of his recent, and highly stimulating, Permanence and Change that "The artist is always an evangelist, quite as the religious reformer is. He wants others to feel as he does." The sentence, indeed, coming in the midst of a philosophical treatise of which the purpose is to clarify rather than to obscure the process of thought and emotion, struck me with its ambiguity. 

It will be worth while to analyze the statement, for, if it means what I take it to mean, it is unfortunately misleading. 

The very phrasing, with such absolute words as "always" and "quite," suggests a momentary lapse from the author's severe discipline over his material. 

An evangelist, literally, is a bringer of good tidings.

Those tidings are of a theological or a religious nature. They concern not only, in fact not chiefly, the experiences that we call artistic; they deal with new convictions and the necessity of convincing others. Thus, though the evangel may be sourced in an emotional overturn, or inward turn (this is the idea at the root of "conversion") it eventuates in a new doctrine. The evangelist looks for new converts in turn, to share his conversion.

The point of contact, then, of the evangelist with what we call the artist is the communication that must be established between himself and his hearers. In that sense we are all artists; it is possible, by defining a word so as to include everything and everybody, to make the meaning of the word evaporate in the atmosphere of our all-inclusiveness. 

The matter and manner of the artist, however, compared with that of the evangelist, are different in an important respect. It is this difference, indeed, which prevents an equation between them.

The evangelist has a doctrine to "sell." He aims at effecting a change in our beliefs. In order to effect this change he will play upon our emotions. The illusions that he induces in us, whether by the enchantment of his voice or the magic of his vision, he asks us to accept as actual. We do not, in the famous phrase of Coleridge, voluntarily suspend our disbelief, ready to take it up again when we return to the world of prose. 

We surrender that disbelief.

The artist does not, as artist, seek to win conviction for

Isaac Goldberg 

83 a new belief. He does not aim at effecting a change in our beliefs so much as at deepening our emotional, our sensual response to the universe within us and outside of us. He induces illusions, to be sure, but he does not ask us to accept them as actual. We suspend our disbelief in the confidence that it is not surrendered-that, having rendered unto Art that which belongs to Art, we may return to Caesar and render unto him that which is his.

To re-enforce his statement with the clause, "as the religious reformer is," makes Mr. Burke's equation the more false.

One of the results of art may be to reform the appreciator; when we shoot an arrow into the air, it may fall to earth we know not where. The ultimate target does not necessarily implicate the aim; the religious reformer, for example, may by the excess of his zeal act upon certain personalities like a boomerang, making Atheists instead of converts.

The nature of artistic communication, moreover, is not the nature of evangelical communication. To much of the artist's work there is the suggestion of a soliloquy overheard. The evangelist chooses his audience; in a deep sense the audience of the artist chooses the artist. The artist is interested, first of all, in expression, not in annunciation. He does not bring tidings; he tries to be them. The reformer-and the evangelist with him-narrows the area of free experience. The artist widens and deepens it. The evangelist's good tidings derive their goodness from the acceptance of an unquestioned external; the sanction of artistry derives from within, and to accept that inner sanction is not, thereby, to deny the validity of any other sanction or of any other like or unlike experience. 

The ultimate effect of art is to free one of shackles; the ultimate effect of evangelism or reformism is at best to exchange one shackle for another. 

Similarity of psychological process does not spell identity of end-products. That is why I consider Mr. Burke's statement an example of ambiguity that should be made more clear, more cogent. In the U. S. S. R. today, as a result of such doubleinterpretations as this, evangelism-reformism and art have been so confused that art, for the moment, has been corrupted. 

The danger of explaining one process in terms of another is that one may be taken for the other. This Mr. Burke himself most pertinently shows in his section on "Argument and Analogy." It seems to me that he has fallen into the very snare of which he was warning us, and made of an analogy an identity. 

It is not the chief business of the artist to exhort. No one will deny that to many scientists, in their work, comes a moment that might be likened to conversion. A chance discovery throws an entire constellation of data into a new interrelationship. As a result, a new integration of the data becomes mandatory. The scientist "sees a new light." He publishes a report out of which he cannot keep his enthusiasm,

much as he should like to render it in impersonal terms. He has been "converted" to a new view of the phenomena. Are we therefore to say that "the scientist is always an evangelist, quite as the religious reformer is"? Is not this a needless misuse of paradox, about which, again, Mr. Burke writes very fruitfully?

An equation such as this, depending upon similarity or even identity of physio-psychologic process, and disregarding or slighting the intentional direction of the process, could end up by equating about every human being in the world to every other human being in that world. Which would bring us back to the fundamental biological relationships, rather than forward to any useful-if not demonstrably perfect-distinctions, Scientist, artist and evangelist are all phases of the human being in quest of knowledge and wisdom of the self-same human being, if you will. Precisely because these differentiations have been developed it is false to say that "the artist is always an evangelist, quite as the religious reformer is," or that "the scientist is always an artist." This becomes a sort of slight-of-word, to which philosophy has been too much addicted. 

To use the words "always" and "quite" in such connections is as misleading, as wrong, as it would be to substitute the word "never." Shall we say, because art may have a propagandistic aspect, that "art is always propaganda"? Or, because propaganda may have an artistic element, that "propaganda is always art"? The question is very pertinent, because the evangelist is always-always, by very definition—a propagandist, and the artist is not always one. The greater the evangelist, the greater the propagandist; the greater the artist, the less the propagandist. 

To me this distinction happens to be a comparative evaluation of experience. I prefer, without desiring to impose my opinions upon others, that type of experience and that attitude toward experience which do not eventuate in hard-and-fast rules of life. The distinction, however, need not exalt art over propaganda. It is enough that the distinction is made, for the very real uses which it can serve.

To argue from emotional origins, and to make similarity of crigins stand for similarity of results in much like arguing from the basis of etymology. The word "theory," according to 

Mr. Burke, means "a vision of God"; the Greek term is defined, in the dictionaries, with special reference to the seeing, not to what is seen. It means, simply, a spectacle, and is cognate with our word "theatre"- -a place where things are seen-spectacles beheld. Let the word mean, however, what Mr. Burke wishes it to. The scientist develops a theory; does this mean that he has a vision of God? Not unless one wishes so to distort the meaning of God as to make it signify any principle of unity, Would the theologian accept that definition? Would he "de-

Isaac Goldberg 

85 grade" his vision of God to the status of a mere theory? The basis of similarity at bottom of the concept is important to the psychologist. To the scientist the difference is not only important; it is vital; it excludes the theologian's God altogether, not as a militant Atheist excludes [banishes] God, but as one with whose universe that particular concept does not happen to be congruous.

To say, then, that "the artist is always an evangelist" is to say, for some of the less discriminating Soviet theorists and practitioners, that "all art is propaganda." The first statement is precisely as true as the second-and as dubious. 

It is the function of propaganda-outside the rather inert definition to be found in dictionaries-to minimize the virtues of its opponents and maximize their defects; on the contrary, stating the case for its own ism it naturally reverses the process. Such a process is acceptable to men in the need of, and the heat of, action. The intelligence resists this threat of self-annihilation or, at least, self-crippling. Even with its body in a uniform, it strives to keep its spirit free. For it knows and feels that action may be a way of blinding oneself to inconvenient truths-that actions are irrevocable while thoughts may be altered-that, though the exactions of life compel us to act as if all men were responsible for what they are and what they do, these men are linked with us in a mesh of determinism. They are our other possible selves. Toleration, in the long run, is not alone the supreme virtue of putting up with those who differ from and with us; it is a wise, a farseeing form of self-indulgence.

Propaganda, at its worst, is malicious misrepresentation. 

At its best, it becomes largely a more or less unconscious rationalization, from which true reason need not necessarily be absent. It is sharply distinguished from the arts, however, by its use of emotions to induce action, not experimental contemplation. Its basis is utilitarian. Just as it is human nature to regard one's own mythology as a religion, and the other man's religion as a mythology, so do we look upon the defense of our own opinions as a reasoned presentation, but on the other fellow's defense as propaganda.

VII. Dialectic Materialism and Theories of the Arts 1. DIALECTIC MATERIALISM 

In earlier English, as one may learn from consulting the Oxford Dictionary, dialectic or dialectics was a noun used synonymously with logic, and therefore bound up naturally with the idea of disputation. Max Eastman, in his illuminative book, Artists In Uniform, devotes a chapter to the word, as both adjective and noun, tracing its history from the Greek significance of conversation. For Plato and Aristotle, and for each in a manner appropriate to their respective types of character, this conversation or discussion assumed an ordered form of give and take. The identification with logic was a later process, and still later was its identification with research. 

Modern usage, which has come in chiefly through Hegel and Karl Marx and Kant, nevertheless goes back to Plato, in whom, as Eastman shows, existed tentatively a "process of affirmation, contradiction and reconciliation of the opposing views." This process was applied by Hegel in his idealistic philosophy; it might, therefore, have been known as dialectic idealism. The contradictions are known, respectively, as thesis and antithesis; their resolution into a higher truth that includes them both is called synthesis. To Hegel this was not simply a process of thought; it was a process of the world itself, in its development. The world thought in this manner; life was not only logic but, so to speak, dia-Logic; its Creator was a thinker in terms of dialectics, much as the Creator, in Mr. Jeans' concept, is a mathematician going through the mo tions of cosmic Q. E. D.'s. 

This Hegelian system was the one in which Marx was trained; as a result, even today in his dialectic, with its emphasis transferred to materialism—that is, history and the workaday activities of man-there is unpleasant evidence of the theological and wish-fulfillment element in his philosophy. 

For the world is viewed as progressing inevitably toward the ends previsioned by the proletarian philosophy of Marx. 

Let this not be interpreted as an argument against the deep feeling for social justice embodied in the Marxian doctrine of the practical aspects of that plan. This is not the place to expatiate upon the bases of scientific Socialism. What 

I am interested in is to suggest the arbitrary, unnecessary, 86

Isaac Goldberg 87 bureaucratic misinterpretation of dialectic materialism as related to activity in the arts. 

When I was a novice in the ideals and realities of Socialism we heard little about dialectic materialism; the phrase then was "economic determinism." I believe the second phrase is, as phrases go, the better. Its very sound emphasized the economic nature of the social structure as determining the relationship of the other factors. It placed its emphasis upon the ultimate determinism rather than upon the dialectic character of the process.

Whatever the name employed, however, the essential meaning is this: in social organization, as in physics, action breeds reaction. When an historic form outgrows its usefulness it sets up opposition; the system of feudalism, crushing individual significance, leads eventually to the rise of a bourgeoisie, determined upon reasserting its individualism. In time, the bourgeoisie, developing into Capitalism, begins to crush individual significance anew, thereby leading to the revolt of the proletariat. Always the development of one power, the revolt of a second, and, as a result, the emergence of a third. Thesis, antithesis, synthesis. . . . Affirmation, contradiction, reconciliation.

In the Marxist conception, however, this triadic evolution, this progress by triple-forms, acquires a tendencious character. The tendency is toward a triad, so to speak, to end all triads a final synthesis or reconciliation of opposites that will do away, in economics, with the class features that have been at the root of the triads. Always, under whatever name, there has been an expropriating class, an expropriated class, and a middle class oscillating between one and the other. Always the Haves, the Have-Nots, the In-Betweens. The Capitalists, the Middle Class, the Proletariat. 

Marxism foresees, then, in the final victory of the proletariat, the end of classes. For classes depend upon ownership by individuals, and exploitation for individual profit, of the social means of production. One owns those means, one works for wages, or one serves as middleman. The proletarian—that is, the working-class-revolt is against ownership, by individuals, of those productive means which receive their significance from, and derive their profits from, the labor of the proletariat. 

All must own, collectively, that which all produce and all must have. There are to be no parasites upon society-no persons who live without working and none who work without in reality living. The proletariat is to capture the state and rule itas other classes have done in the past-until the transition is made to a society in which there are no economic classesnone, that is, who by power over socially necessary means of production exercise power over the lives and destinies of others.

The historic destiny of the proletariat, then, is to intro-

duce the classless society. It is the class to end all classes. 

By ending the private, individualistic regime of economic control and establishing the collective regime it does away with the numerous harmful, wasteful, hypocritic accompaniments of the Capitalist system, which lead inevitably to war. makes for permanent peace. 

This is all much too schematic, of course. For our purposes, however, it will do. I am interested particularly in the effects of class-rule upon freedom of expression. The arts and even the sciences, according to Marxian theoreticians, reflect the underlying prejudices of the economic system under which they operate. They partake, then, of a class character. By that same token, an emergent class, or a class in revolt against its oppressors, will produce an art, a culture, marked by its origins in social rebellion.

The application of this point of view to the study of the arts and the sciences, and particularly to the arts, has proved a most valuable and most enlightening discipline. All the more reason, then, why we should beware of falling victims to a false proportion rooted in the natural enthusiasm for a new and revealing method of approach.

Since truth is most scientifically considered as a complex of inter-relationships, and not as an Absolute, it is not likely that any single method can explain everything. Life is not all politics and economics; it is not all logic; it is not all art. 

The materialist dialectic must be measured against life, not life against the materialist dialectic. One of the pitfalls of the propagandist is that he is tempted, unconsciously, to reverse this process. One of the virtues of the artistic experience is that the artist tends to maintain the all-inclusiveness of life as against the wilfully narrowed area of the propagandist and the zealot.

Capitalism has indubitably produced a type of art that bears the mark of its epoch. The Socialists and the Communists are not in error when they speak of capitalistic-i. e., bourgeois-literature, or drama, or even music. But just how true and valid is the adjective? And wherein may it go wrong? 

2. ASPECTS OF ART 

Art-using this term to include all its manifestations in the various media-may be regarded from various aspects. 

We may consider it genetically; we may consider it as a body of documents; we may consider it as a social force; we may consider it as an individual expression. In other words, we may regard it from the standpoint of its evolution; we may consider it chiefly as a collection of texts; we may study it with special reference to its social implications, thus linking it up with economics; and, finally, we may regard it particularly as psychology.

Isaac Goldberg 

89 People change under changing conditions; hence they have different thoughts and feelings to express; hence they embody those differences, in forms that themselves reflect the newness of the content. The essential structure of the human body-its biology, remains constant; whatever the conditions under which we live, whatever the economic and political forms, we must eat, we are impelled to mate. Hunger and love ---the numerous ways in which they may manifest themselves in art-will always strive for expression. 

The evolution of the arts depends upon the economic structure of society to a very great extent. The Marxian interpretation, though not the only one, and though not explaining everything (what one explanation can pretend to cover everything?) is of primary importance, especially when the arts are considered from the evolutionary point of view. When Upton 

Sinclair, in such studies as Mammonart and Money Writes!, makes out the artist to be a paid perverter of ideas, he exaggerates a theory of which his own life is the best contradiction. 

It is true that writers, like other human beings under Capitalism, are impelled by self-interest to adopt an attitude most in harmony with current notions. This they do chiefly, however, not as intellectual prostitutes but because they absorb, naturally, the atmosphere of their surroundings. A few of us, like 

Sinclair himself, acquire insight and rebel. Capitalism is the thesis; our rebellion is the antithesis; the attempt to make a new literature is the synthesis.

This is important as history, as evolution; it does not confer upon the individual piece of work an esthetic merit, simply because that work seems to be prompted by advocacy of the “right” cause.

Literature considered as a body of texts ceases to be primarily literature; it becomes a sociological museum, and, as such, possesses importance. A novel may be, as a story, execrable; as illustration of a social epoch, or of any other theme, it may be highly significant. The creative writer labors, not to produce a museum piece but an expression of personal or communal passion.

A work of art may, again, contribute appreciably to a social change. Uncle Tom's Cabin, let us say. It may achieve highly beneficial results in the fields of morality, politics, ethics. This makes it significant-to ethics, politics, morality. 

But not to art, unless, in addition to its secondary effects it possesses those beauties that belong to the arts. 

We must not confuse the position of a work of art in categories such as economics, morals, social progress, ethics, with its value as an esthetic creation. Shakespeare may appeal to the psycho-analysts because his plays make excellent material for psycho-analytic exegesis. What makes him a great playwright, however, is not his adaptability to this thesis or that, but precisely his transcendence of all limited theories.

In other words, the work of art, in addition to its serviceability as an illustration of formal evolution, or as a document of social progress, or as an exhibit, possesses esthetic autonomy-possesses selfhood, just as the artist himself, in addition to being a member of an economic group, a special type of psychologic and biologic structure, a male or female, is also an esthetic entity, playing with possibilities, devising patterns of imaginary experiences, speculating with thoughts and emotions, attempting to see them for values not immediately translatable into use, contemplating rearrangements of life. 

An older sociology isolated a so-called "economic man” from all the other aspects of mankind, creating an intellectual symbol that worked only on paper, and not always even on paper. Man cannot be thus isolated without destroying in him a dimension of living. To fetter the artist, to enclose him in an arbitrary category of experiences, is to rob man of his first freedom and his last: the freedom of thought and of imagination.

To force upon him, whether by open declaration, by innuendo, by the implication of ostracism, or by the silent compulsion of bureaucratic power, a restricted course dictated by a questionable extension of Marx's materialistic dialectic, is to redefine the arts as a division of political expediency. 

This, unfortunately, has happened in the U. S. S. R. 

I say, unfortunately, and most unfortunately, because there was no reason for looking to Germany and Italy for a new intellectual freedom. There was every reason to look for what we have received: intellectual and artistic collapse. There was every reason, on the other hand, to look to the Soviet Union for freedom of the artist, at least. Instead, he has been shackled to a theory. And there is no theory in the world that is so all-excelling as to ask, or to receive, exclusive allegiance. 

3. MECHANIZED ARTISTRY 

The entire concept of artistry has become so mechanized that the artist is rechristened, by Stalin, as "an engineer of souls." The Soviet Union, in its passion for industrialization, exalts the machine. Very well; for, once the U. S. S. R. is adequately industrialized, the machines will become the slaves, not, as in the United States, the masters, of man. If some 

Russian artists, inspired by the spectacle-and it is a grandiose spectacle that may well inspire any artist-choose to turn their talents toward expressing the soul of the materialist dialectic, there can be no valid objection. Their products will be judged, however, as art, not for political loyalty, but for esthetic virtue. It is not the subject imposed upon the artist that rankles; it is the imposition. "Engineer of souls". . . . How can one be an engineer of souls when, in order to qualify for the decoration, he must

Isaac Goldberg 91 give up his own soul? If, to this strangely unmaterialistic word there remains any valid meaning, surely it is that of individuality, of personality. . . . Yet Soviet theory of the arts frowns upon individualism, in the mistaken notion that the individual exists for the collectivity alone. It was possible, indeed, for a reviewer in The New Masses to speak of "stinking" individualism, as if the purpose of collectivism were not to provide a society out of which true individuals would arise. 

An excess of conformity leads to malformation. The history of the Soviet attempt to strait-jacket its authors, to turn them into glorified propagandists, has already begun to reveal the futility of such methods. And although the dissolution of the RAPP (The Russian Association of Proletarian Writers) 

was hailed as epochal, and the date (April 23, 1932) as a redletter day, the reform has been, thus far, more nominal than real.

Russian propaganda is adapted to a nation that still consists largely of illiterates. Why, then, the uncritical haste of American Communists to adapt Soviet methods to the radical literature of a nation that is predominantly literate? Why the indiscriminate acceptance of Soviet movies, whose goodness had as much to do with photographic skill as with proletarian subject-matter? Indeed, that matter, intended for slowly emerging intelligences, was paced so slowly as to irritate any but the hardiest advocates. anti-subjectivism; Anti-individualism; anti-estheticism; anti-sexualism; all these Soviet attitudes have been projected upon artistic theory. Whatever is regarded as evil is assigned to bourgeois influence.

Yet, in order not to deny the continuity of life itself, some of the bourgeois accomplishments are carried over into “proletarian" artistry. In music, for example, one critic reveals a certain indulgence toward Beethoven. He would retain, for example, the first, the third, the fifth, the first two movements of the ninth symphony. Why? Because these represent the tumultuous Beethoven, and the music of the proletariat, supposedly, is to be characterized by tumultuousness. Wagner, however, according to the same theorist, is frowned upon as suggesting too strongly the sexual. The antipathy of proletarian theory to engrossment in the sexual life is symtomatic of all movements with a puritanic, self-sacrificial foundation. 

The Stalin formula for all the other arts holds for music, too: "national in form, revolutionary in content."

This slogan summarizes the approach of Stalinism to Socialist strategy. With Stalin, Socialism ceased to be, in practice, a purely international effort, as planned and prophesied in the writings of Karl Marx. It became a goal that might be achieved in this nation or that, there to be consolidated even at the cost of the revolutionary movement in other countries where it had fewer chances of immediate success. Not being

competent to voice opinions upon such strategy, I merely indicate the fact and its effects upon artistic theory. For Communist doctrine, the arts exist as propaganda; for propaganda, content is far more important than form; all the arts, including even music, become "a weapon in the class struggle." 

As Charles Seeger writes in Modern Music (March-April, 1934, page 126) "Art, then, is always and inevitably a social function. It has social significance. It is a social force. It is propaganda: explicit, positive; implied, negative. The better the art, the better the propaganda it makes: the better the propaganda, the better art it is." 

Another commentator, Hanns Eisler, who has acquired fame as perhaps the foremost composer of "proletarian" music in this day, discovers that the concept of "pure" music is a bourgeois symptom. Writing in the first number of the recently founded magazine, Music Vanguard, on the history of the German Workers' Music Movement from 1848, he says (page 45) "The supremacy of music without words, called (even popularly) 'absolute music,' the separation between music and work, the distinction between serious and light music, between professionals and amateurs-these are typical features of music under Capitalism."

Once again one detects, beneath these half-truths, the passionate desire of Communist strategy for content, for substance of appeal, for a message to be transmitted, for the propagandization of all activities. As impassioned Marxists, in the political field, strive to show that universal history has been working inevitably toward the triumph of the proletariat, so impassioned theorists under the Marxian spell strive to show that the evolution of the arts has been pointing inevitably to the exemplification of the "proletarian" interpretation of artistry.

Such partisan explanations have their uses. They are valuable more for the details that they gather than for the general principle that they seek to establish. They suffer, naturally, from the defects that accompany all consciously partisan thinking; they are much too schematic; they tend to overlook or slight data that might weaken the case; they use terms carelessly, and the employment of adjectives like "bourgeois" or "capitalistic" ceases to have any scientific meaning; proof of social origin is taken as proof of exclusively social significance. 

4. ART IS INDIVIDUAL 

This last point is of extreme importance, and it brings us back to the anti-individualism and anti-subjectivism of the Soviet theorists. Let us consider a few general principles, and reserve for later the manner in which Soviet critics misapply their partisan zeal. 

Art is, as Mr. Seeger says, always and inevitably a social

Isaac Goldberg 93 function. Does this mean that it may not be, always and inevitably, an individual function as well? Can social mean anything without the concept individual, of which society is made up? Art has social significance; of course it has. Does it therefore have to lack individual significance? Art is, in the final analysis, a communication from one individual to another. Art, however social in intention, is the conception of an individual and finds its goal in the individual. 

De Gourmont once said that Flaubert's impersonality was Flaubert's way of being personal. One might with equal truth remark that the anti-subjectivism, the anti-individualism of Soviet theory is the Soviet way of being subjective and individual. It is the rationalization of a temporary economic strategy into a permanent canon of art. In the same way the intellectual poverty of the proletariat has been rationalized into an over-simplification of art-forms. 

That the proletariat is intellectually impoverished is surely no fault of the working-class. It is a condition to remedy, not to rationalize. When a composer like Hanns Eisler sees the "light," and turns from his studies under Schoenberg to the simplicities of music for the workers, he performs a service not lacking in nobility. But does this mean that 

Schoenberg must therefore be abjured as a "bourgeois" composer, and that the new, reactionary simplicities of "proletarian" music must be glorified as a conquest of the future? 

I open a copy of The Daily Worker for March 1, 1935, and read of Eisler, in an article by Joe Foster. "When he realized that all music, all culture, could have a future only when identified with working class interests, he decided to take instruction from recognized masters. For in composing for the working class, only the best traditions in art were good enough. On this basis a superior, newer revolutionary technique could be built up. . . . ." Eisler became Schoenberg's favorite pupil, and drew regard from bourgeois critics as the most promising of the younger composers. "But despite these honors, and critical approval, he hated the music that he wrote. It was terrible, without social content, and was received only by the social elite. It was music in a vacuum.' 99 Now here, I submit, is musical criticism in a vacuum, even if that vacuum is a proletarian vacuum. It appraises the value of music not as music, but as a social message. How this is to be adapted to music that carries no words with it, I do not know. Or, rather, I do: the sounds will be rationalized into signifying a program that translates into tone the Communist Manifesto. It will be difficult, but manipulation of words can work wonders when joined to fanatic zeal. 

Does individual life really cease with the triumph of the proletariat? Or does it only then truly begin? If, as one has always hoped, it only then truly begins, we have every right to look for a new flourishing of individualistic art. And Wag-

ner is too sexual, is he? As if any five-year plan of any regime could do away with Nature's little nine month's plan! 

Since when are such terms as individual and subjective mutually exclusive? Since when is it impossible, especially in a proletarian state, to re-blend them into the harmony that Capitalism did so much to turn into a dissonance? 

We shall see presently that the propagandistic attitude toward music reveals all the inconsistencies, all the partisanship, all the wilful half-thinking that characterizes this same attitude toward the other arts. 

First, however, let us see how "proletarian" criticism, in literature, drama, and other verbal pursuits, works in specific instances under the pens of specific critics.

VIII. 

Materialist Dialectic: Chiefly Practice and Criticism 

1. FORMULARY CRITICISM 

Let me give an example of what I mean by the deadening effect of formulary criticism. I open a Communist weekly and begin to read a review. I encounter this phrase: ". . . if all the 17 women had been as remote from reality as Emily 

Dickinson. . . ." I believe I know where the reviewer got the adjective; she probably knows by heart Mr. Granville Hicks'

The Great Tradition. Mr. Hicks is a critic whom I read with pleasure and instruction, but I cannot get over the feeling that inside of him is still waging a powerful conflict between what he feels as esthetic truths and what he has come to feel as doctrinal duties. His treatment of the selfsame Emily Dickinson is a case in point. 

He finds Emily free of sentimentality in her love poems; "she wrote of birds and flowers as if no one had ever written of them before. Literary conventions did not exist for her; as a poet she was quite untroubled by public opinion. Everything in her work is immediate, personal and honest. ... "And yet the poetry of Emily Dickinson is undeniably fragile and remote. The discovery of her work is, for any sensitive reader, an exciting event; one responds eagerly to so complete a revelation of personal experience. But close as her life comes to the lives of her readers, it touches them at very few points." Emily, it appears, was narrow, and "the future was not with her."

In a word, Emily was not a sociologist. She was not, like 

Mr. Hicks, a social prophet who knows what the future is and with whom it is to be. To write of natural beauty with virginal insight to revolt by the fiat of one's nature against convention-to flout the public opinion of a Puritanical day-to achieve immediacy, intense integration of personality, honesty ... all these are fragile and remote! Remote from what? From the tragedies and comedies of human existence, which will assail us and console us, whatever the social scheme under which we exist? From the experiences of the body and psyche that are based upon bio-chemical organization, upon psychological realities, which are quite as fundamental as economic configurations and often underlie them? How many poets we have today who, in Mr. Hicks' phrase, are with the future! And how many of these estimable ladies, with all their sociological stur- 95

diness and closeness to life, will prove themselves to be worthy companions of the remote and fragile Emily? Or is poetry itself to go by the board, especially in its more personal aspects?

Part of this doctrinaire aspersion of the "romantic” personality derives from the not necessarily logical opposition, which we are forever encountering, of mass to individual. As if the necessity for mass action abolishes-rather than subordinates for the time being-individual psychology. The 

Marxian philosophy, brought to bear upon the genesis of the arts, is full of revealing inter-relationships. But it is as true of Marxian criticism, as of psycho-analytic criticism and any other doctrinaire approach to the arts-nor can we repeat this too often-that to establish a source is not therefore to establish a value. To prove that art-forms have their roots in social forms, or that art-forms have their roots in psycho-analytic sources, may be possible. Let us assume that it is. Is this therefore to test the esthetic worth of a product by its exemplification of that origin? In art, streams rise higher than their sources.

In art, the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. The artist is anti-mass in a very special sense that Socialists and Communists, of all people, should make sure of understanding. 

He is the protection of that mass against the anonymity of numbers; he is the resurgence of that mass; he is the quintessence of its more sensitive qualities. He is mass made individual, resisting as well as confirming his source. He is even, and especially in a Communist economy, something of a Devil's 

Advocate, lest one good custom should corrupt the world.

The propaganda theory of art narrows one to dimensions even narrower than those of Emily Dickinson. It makes for a species of fragility that Emily's artistic spirit, so blithely dismissed as fragile and remote, never knew. It makes for rigidity, and for humorlessness. I knew a disgruntled critic whose chief interest in the books he read was not so much their substance as their indexes; he would turn at once to the back of the volume to see whether his name appeared in the list of authors quoted, and, if it did, would turn to the pages indicated, muttering, "I wonder what they have to say about me." Doctrinaire critics are in much the same case. What has this to say about Psycho-analysis, Behaviorism, Communism? 

And on this basis alone the book is viewed. This sort of thing, however broad the terms in which it is promulgated, makes for intolerance. It makes the critic, in fact, as a critic, just a little remoter from reality than he had been before. For it implies, even demands, the arbitrary exclusion of much human experience; it attempts, as it must attempt if it is to be true to the accepted doctrine, to impose upon the vital inconsistencies of living an intellectually satisfying consistency. 

Another Communist critic, the other day, made the belated

Isaac Goldberg 97 discovery that Gilbert and Sullivan, whom once he sang and admired with such uncritical enthusiasm, were after all the products of a bourgeois culture. Recanting his childhood adherence to these Victorians, he announced, " in as unamiable a spirit as is possible," that the Gilbert and Sullivan "fans" were a bunch of "bourgeois culture-hounds who want to avoid all reality and strength in art."

The humorlessness of Mr. Michael Gold's diatribe, and the suspicious over-protest of that word "unamiable" as if Gilbert and Sullivan still worked their charm upon him, but he felt that he mustn't allow it, lest he prove recreant to aims that other radicals can hold without surrendering their right to enjoy any form of great art wherever they find it, and by whomsoever produced-that humorlessness and over-protest are beside the present point. If Mr. Gold knew the history of these Victorian twins in England, he would have recalled that 

Gilbert was accused of subversiveness, of blasphemy, of ungallantry, of being a menace to the purity of Victorian youth. 

If his mind was really free to operate in the field of criticism, he might also have permitted himself to realize that reality and strength are by no means synonymous in art-that Gilbertian fantasy is often more real, and strong, than the most brow-furrowed, avowedly propagandistic play. For, I take it, the attack--at this date!-upon Gilbert and Sullivan is chiefly an attack upon Gilbert. Though I doubt not that there are plenty of distorted critics who could show, in the space of a 

32nd note, that Sullivan's melodies are social-fascism of the most insidious type.

It is too bad. To lose one's sense of humor is a species of amputation. The selfsame diversion (H. M. S. Pinafore) that was the cause of Mr. Gold's unamiable recantation brought down the ire of Queen Victoria upon Gilbert's playfully libertarian head. By which token Gilbert was something of an independent, cantankerous, rebellious personality. And such a personality has a habit of breeding these same qualities in others, whatever the direction in which those qualities will be exerted. Was Mr. Gold ruined for radicalism by Gilbert and 

Sullivan? We see that he was not. The Victorian pair have done far more than any doctrinaire demagogue would suspect toward introducing gentle natures to satiric understanding and independence.

The arts, one had thought, were chiefly for emotional expansion, not for a species of intellectual ratification that places them on a level with the homiletic Sunday-School tract. They were, among other things, for the compensations and the realities-yes, the realities-of vicarious experience and imaginative participation. It might even be shown that Mr. Gilbert, in Pinafore, Trial by Jury, and Iolanthe, was a pretty valuable ally-let's call him a poputchik, a fellow-traveler, a road companion-in the cause of liberation. But, no; he doesn't rhyme

the Communist manifesto. Ergo, thumbs down, and let's lose a lot of good fun. 

2. MICKEY MOUSE-GERTRUDE STEIN 

I spoke in a previous chapter of the childish standards of the movies. A superficial criticism would include, in these standards, without differentiation, that strange child of Walt Disney's imagination, Mickey Mouse. It would be a very simple matter to level, at this international creature, the same blasts of scorn that Mr. Gold unleashed in the direction of Gilbert and Sullivan.

For is not Mickey the very synthesis of the fairy-tale and the wish-fulfillment? Is he not open to the same condemnation that the widow of Lenin visited upon the head of the foremost 

Russian writer of tales for children, Kornei Chukovsky? 

Chukovsky, declared Krupskaya, was "non-political" and "individualistic"; he did not indoctrinate! Yet had not her husband himself wisely said that "the theater is necessary not so much for propaganda as to give people a rest from daily work. . . . "For theater read art in general; perhaps rest is not the right word, either. There is no mistaking the wisdom of Lenin's suggestion, however; and of his practice, for that matter. He read with delight the "bourgeois" Russian classics, recognizing instinctively that art is something more than the expression, implicit or explicit, of views upon economic problems. He listened with refreshment to Beethoven.

Yes... Mickey Mouse, with sufficient imagination upon the part of the critic, and with insufficient response to the wider areas of living, could be made out as an enemy to the children. For he is "individualistic"; he is "non-political." He happens also to be a number of other things that will never cease from appealing to the child and to the child-in-the-adult. 

He does not, like the more "grown-up" film, ask belief or ratification. His wish-fulfillments are plainly wish-fulfillments. 

His absurdities are patently absurdities. For this reason there is far more sense to his nonsense than there is to most of the "wisdom" that we get in the regulation opus of the cinema. 

Mickey is the conquest of the fancy over the solidities of matter, the vastness of space, the dimension of time. He is man's revenge upon the immutable and inexorable laws of nature. By that selfsame token he is a critic of our unfettered hopefulness—a satirist of our own Icarian flights into the empyrean. That this latter effect is so largely an unconscious influence does not make it the less potent. Since the world of ideas is round, one of the effects of "wish-fulfillment" and "escape" art may be, strangely enough, at once a satisfaction of those impulses and a criticism of them. By reducing our desires to the absurdity of their extreme statement they put those desires into their proper places, and thus give us a firm-

Isaac Goldberg 99 er grasp upon "reality." If one has been tempted to tilt at windmills, one is not so likely to try it after having read Don Quixote. He is the more likely to realize that there are more efficacious ways of confronting life. Let us hope, however, that laughter-irresponsible, unrationalized, and especially undoctrinary, laughter-will never cease to be one of the most important of those ways.

Another example of formulary criticism: a writer in The 

New Masses for April 9, 1935, reviews Lectures in America, by Gertrude Stein. With this book I haven't the slightest sympathy; I have been poking fun at Gertrude Stein ever since I read her Geography and Plays, almost 20 years ago. No underground bourgeois wires connect me with her elucubrations. "The book," winds up the reviewer, “can be recommended to psychiatrists as an example of the final, paretic stage of bourgeois culture."

Just what is the value of these words as economic or literary criticism? The bourgeois culture happens to be one of the great cultures of which the world yet has knowledge. It is the culture in which the writer of those very words was trained. That culture, if Capitalism is really in its closing historical stages, will gradually evolve into another type of culture, partly not entirely-conditioned by the new economic configuration. New economic configurations add their words to the language, too; but this doesn't mean that the language surrenders its vital continuity and undergoes a violent change. 

If there is something psychopathic and not merely charlatanic about the writings of Gertrude Stein, that something is far more likely to be rooted in her personal psychology than in any metaphorical syphilitic end-products of the Capitalist system. After all, most contemporary writers do not write like Gertrude Stein; after all, most readers, if they have ever heard of her and opened her pages, do not care for her. They laugh at her; she is a show; she is just as much a fake, or a spectacle to them as to the critic of The New Masses. Bourgeoisie hasn't a thing to do with the case, except to give to a doctrinary critic a chance to drag his ism in by the hair. One might just as well accuse some of the Soviet writers of infantilism, because of their desire to write down to the peasantry. 

And it would be easy to proceed from this accusation to some pseudo-impressive chatter about the relationship between proletcult and senility.

If a Gertrude Stein is truly the product of "the final paretic stage of bourgeois culture," there would have to be plenty indeed of her to substantiate the diagnosis. The very fact of her uniqueness argues against the facile ascription. 

3. JOHN STRACHEY Mr. John Strachey's Literature and Dialectical Materialism

should be read by every person whose public profession or whose private pleasure is the evaluation of books. 

Strachey has some extremely pertinent things to say about such writers as MacLeish, Hemingway, Spender, Auden, Granville Hicks and others in the contemporary limelight. He reveals the "unconscious fascism" in MacLeish-the phrase originated with Mike Gold-and points out that "there are many MacLeishes amongst the middle classes of the world, many men, that is, who feel the necessity for some revolt against the existing situation, but who also feel that it is quite impossible to identify themselves with the only people who can, in fact, undertake that revolt-with the working masses. "" Nor is Mr. Strachey, like so many other critics of his persuasion, undialectically unjust to the bourgeoisie. In fact, he cautions his fellow-critics against mistaking the political ignorance of their opponents for a vice. Even Lenin knew the virtues of patience, as when, asked in April, 1917, what was the main duty of the Bolshevik, he replied, "Patiently to explain." Now, Mr.

Strachey explains patiently, and with humor as well as with an understanding of human frailty. So humanly, indeed, that he becomes unconsciously condescending. ". . . It would be indeed a blunder if we tried to pretend that a man was a bad poet because he was a bad Marxist, and a good poet because he was a good Marxist. If this were true, it, also, would make the world a nice, simple, easy and tidy place to live in. . . . And I would put in a plea for a certain tolerance to writers of merit, who are, in the broadest sense of the word, on the right side of the fence." Strachey's words imply, and imply powerfully, a criticism of Bolshevist political, as well as esthetic, technique. A man may be a very useful human being, too, without being a thorough-going Marxist, and should be accorded, even in Soviet society, that same "certain tolerance" for which Strachey makes his sensible plea. 

He points out, further, that "we cannot too strictly criticize the work of our own writers. Their political thinking, their knowledge of Marxism may be beyond question; but that, surely, should not free them from the sternest attempt to evaluate their work as literature." Strachey then proceeds to point out, in Granville Hicks ("the foremost Marxist literary critic of America") the selfsame weakness that I have commented upon in my own considerations of Mr. Hicks. Coming from a critic of authority among the Communists, though Strachey is not a member of the party, the observation has peculiar importance. It would be a pity, says Strachey-indeed, he actually writes, "a thousand pities"-if Hicks' strong sense of responsibility to the Communist movement "should force him to stifle his esthetic sensibility." This, precisely, is what has happened to Hicks, though his case is not hopeless. In even greater degree it has happened to those cocksure "critics" of inferior ability and inferior training who have fondly imag-

Isaac Goldberg 101 ined that revolutions destroy esthetic values and render artistic discipline superfluous.

Ultimately, we want a more equitable world—a world more free in the economic sense-because we want a world more beautiful. It is the Communist criticism of the bourgeois, industrialized universe that it vitiates culture that it puts a premium upon greed and the acquisitive powers. Such, in fact, is Mr. Strachey's introductory contention. Is it not, then, contradictory, not to say subtly dangerous, for the official Communists to throw beauty off its pedestal and proclaim a materialism, an anti-culturalism of their own, seeking to identify all culture with Communist propaganda? Against this danger 

Mr. Strachey inveighs. "It is indeed of vital importance that 

Marxist writers and critics should have mastered the very highest achievements of the art and literature which has been developed in the four centuries of the bourgeois dominance of the world."

To hate that culture, to misappraise it, is, as I have steadily maintained, to be false even to the very dialectical materialism invoked to justify that hatred, that misappraisal. . . . In any case, Mr. Strachey's essay should be read as intently by 

Communists as by non-Communist literati. It is an excellent portent of restored balance and should have important repercussions in the zones of "proletarian" literature. Who knows? 

It may even help some zealots to regard the intelligentsia as something nobler than a target for salivary ballistics. 

4. PLAYS, NOVELS AND PROPAGANDA 

Whoever visits the Soviet theater-I refrain from speaking of such entities as the Italian or the German theater, since Hitler and Mussolini have done to those respective institutions what Macbeth did to sleep-comments upon the passionate interest of the audiences and the technical skill of the producing forces. This is not, for Russia, anything new. Something in the Russian character inclines markedly to the arts of speech, to the fascination of color and movement, to the magic of plastic sound, to the diversions and divinations of the dance. 

The Western peoples know, as yet, too little of Russian opera, ballet, and painting. However great may be the difference in points of view between the old Russia and the new, the remarkable gift of this people for the arts will-if biological and historic continuity have any meaning-continue to create new surprises for the world.

It would have continued, from all the evidence, under any regime. Once the shackles of a narrow bureaucratic attitude toward the arts have been thrown off-and thrown off, let us hope, by the very jailers who, in an excess of partisan enthusiasm, locked then on-that gift should flourish with reborn vigor. Ultimately, indeed, the proletarian revolution in Russia

should produce a renaissance that must have its influence upon the rest of the world. That influence, to be sure, may at first be chiefly economic; in the end, however, it must be artistic, too. In the beginning, too; for it is easier to import a work of art than to import an economic program. 

For the moment, however, though the Western nations respond with readiness to the Russian dance, and to the new composers of the Soviet, they are much slower to take up the verbalized arts such as poetry, drama, and novel. The novel, always a bastard. form-this, need I add, is said genetically rather than morally-finds always a curious audience, since, even when it is ill done, it throws light upon new conditions. 

It is, by a good proportion, informative, and frankly so. 

Some impulse makes us resist the transmission of information through works of art. According to such close and sympathetic observers as Louis Fisher, this is true evenperhaps especially-in the U. S. S. R., where not even a prize fight can take place without its political speechifying between rounds. The Wild West type of film, which all but our rural audiences reject, comes with its speed, its romance, its nonpolitical nature, as a welcome relief to Soviet patrons of the movies. For these over-politicized Russians it is "escape"; and he who argues that art should have no element of "escape" [merely because some artists have specialized in it to the exclusion of its other elements] knows neither humanity nor art. 

For that matter, one is entitled to the suspicion that such a one may not even know precisely what "escape" may be. One man's escape is another man's reality. By that same token, the arbitrary abolition of "romance" by official fiat suffers from the same limitations.

Soviet theoreticians who imagine that they have put the quietus upon romance, escape, individualism, subjectivism, simply because they have introduced new conditions for the definition, are self-deceived. Nothing shows this as clearly as some of the plays produced under the recent laws laid down for Socialist Realism. That these plays do not yet reveal new dramatic forms is not to be held against them. Forms are the very flower of superior art; the content, on the other hand, however important to other phases of life, is secondary to art. 

This, by the way, is something that the more sensitive spirits among the Communist ranks are beginning to discover. Perhaps, rather, they knew it right along, but have begun to speak out.

Edward Dahlberg, for instance, in The New Masses for 

April 23, 1935, in speaking of Waldo Frank's novel, The Death and Birth of David Markand, writes that "There is, of course, no one way, or any one hundred ways, of perceiving or arriving at a new social and class outlook." I consider this a statement of the utmost importance for the new writer. Dahlberg goes on to discuss the proletarian novel-and, by implication,

Isaac Goldberg 103 the proletarian work of art in general-with wise words that come from the heart of a writer rather than from the head of a propagandist. "Revolutionary novels about the middle class in which the proletariat has no domniant role, or only an incidental part, have received a rather tepid reception. The technic of indirection, strikes, class-war murders, hunger, used as a dramatic cyclorama to heighten the torture and the awareness of the protagonist, the more peripheral and roundabout approaches to Communism in fiction, have been summarily dismissed or ineptly considered. Vacillation, conflict, anguish have been viewed as unrepresentative sick emotions of a sepulchral figure or milieu. This attitude is not without a touch of babbittry and a not too dim echo of the slogan: nothing succeeds like success. In short, is it essential that a character in a revolutionary novel walk out of the book or off the stage like a Marchbanks? Is it necessary that the David Markands or the Studs

Lonigans beset by the present-day Furies know all that the readers know? The poignant dilemma of poor Ernest in The 

Way of All Flesh is a case in point. Ernest, to the very end, jots down abstruse metaphysical insights and axioms; he never emerges triumphant from the clerical haze and aura of the church-but the reader does."

Coming after the lines I have quoted from Strachey's Literature and Dialectic Materialism this paragraph suggests a revision of Communist criticism-a revision in the direction of fundamental principles of artistry. The natural emphasis of propagandistic criticism upon subject matter has led, by treacherous degrees, to the evaluation of a work of art according to its acceptance of one's ideas. From this to the acceptance of a particular faction's ideas is only too easy. 

It is not important, in a work of art, that we-the readers or spectators-should believe in the ethics or the superstitions of a set of characters. It is important that they should believe in them, or struggle against them, in such a way as to become, for us, emotionally and intellectually convincing. 

It is a poor victory for an idea, even from the propagandistic standpoint, when that "victory" has been achieved by methods that are transparent to a cultivated audience. Soviet theoreticians, in their eagerness to introduce the principle of communal endeavor-a principle that belongs in the life of action and practical endeavor rather than in the highly individualistic craft of the arts-deny an individualism of which plentiful use is made in their drama. Piecès such as Bread, Fear, Squaring the Circle, Tempo, and Inga, all of them to be found in the collection of Soviet Plays edited by Eugene Lyons, are as thickly populated with heroes and villains as any cheap, pre-proletarian American melodrama from the days of the ten, twent' and thirt'. This should help to prove that correctness or nobility of ideas are not enough to make a good play. If

they were, our own American melodrama would be beyond reproach, since it stood for masculine courage, feminine virtue, uprightness, honor and the rest of those virtues that have been taken over almost bodily by the proletarian theoreticians, and enlisted in another cause.

I am not inveighing against the mass as protagonist; I am not opposing the proletarian revolution, and all that it can mean to the human race, as an inspiration for art. [Inspiration, like disinterestedness, and individualism, is another of those words that makes some Red theorists see red. I am afraid, none the less, that we shall still have use for it, no matter what the regime under which an artist produces his creations." It is not an accident," said Bukharin, in a speech on Soviet Realism delivered in Moscow to the All-Union Congress of Soviet Writers, "that one man becomes a philosopher and another a poet." Not an accident, and not a command.] 

No; I am trying to say, for the hundredth time, and each time from a different angle of approach, that the proof of the pudding is not in the recipe but in the eating-that a work of art is as great, or as small, as its author, not as its subject-that an inept author can spoil a great subject, and that greatness on the part of the author can confer, even upon seemingly ignoble subject-matter, artistic significance. 

However much it may displease anti-individualists and anti-inspirationists [especially the sort in the U. S. S. R., who insist upon turning the production of plays and books into a factory activity, and who, in their industrial needs, have reconceived everything in the metaphor of industrialism] the true subject-matter of a work of art is the author. It is he who fuses substance and style into an organic unity. 

Let me illustrate once more: Albert Malz's play, Black Pit, may have owed its origin to a didactic, propagandistic purpose; very well. I happen to indorse the general direction of that purpose, but this plays little part in my approval of the play as a whole. It matters not too much just what purpose fires an author to write; we judge the result, not the origin. The purpose may reveal him as a noble citizen, an exemplary comrade, an unparalleled lover, a what not else; the purpose reveals him as a person. The result reveals him as an artist, who, to be sure, is also a person, and is conditioned by whatever conditions the person, but is something in addition. Otherwise we should not have such a word as artist.

Maltz's play reveals the temptation of a hard-pressed miner to become, under the compulsion of his need for a wife, a home, food and doctor's care, informer against his comrades of the mine. His Joe is neither a hero nor a villain; he is you or I caught in the tangle of life's involved cross-purposes; he is the rope in a tug of war at either end of which are powers equally strong. Even as propaganda, our pity of that man works far more constructively than any enforced disapproval.

Isaac Goldberg 105 By revealing Joe, instead of filling him with doctrinary stuffing, the dramatist makes him more like ourselves, and hence more real. If we must have propaganda-and I believe that we must-one is the more inclined to consult it where it belongs, after having watched the progress of Black Pit. 

The same may be said for such genre pictures as Cliff 

Odets' Waiting For Lefty, with reference to certain of its scenes rather than to the hortatory purpose and practice of the whole. There is an episode in which an unlikely stenographer, taking sides against her employer in favor of a down-and-outer looking for a job in the theater, suddenly recommends to him the Communist Manifesto. Such scenes as this are automatic criticism of the sort of thing that hot advocacy lets itself in for when it approaches the art-forms. The teacher at the blackboard with a pointer is very good-in the class-room. 

He does not belong in the theater.

Consider the fairly palpable difference between Odets' 

Till the Day I Die and Awake and Sing. The first, anti-Nazi in a most propagandistic fashion, convinces those already convinced. It is pre-digested food that the healthy appetite rejects. One might, with little manipulation, put the martyrs in the place of the executioners, and vice versa. Consider what the Bolsheviki have done to their political enemies. On this human inversion of roles Anatole France has a very wiseand very sad-observation.

The effect of Awake and Sing is altogether different. It points not to a moral but in a general, unmistakable direction. 

It exhibits a fine sense of living dialogue, of differentiation between characters. It is by no means a great play; there is enough of the melodramatic to mar but not to destroy its inherent dramatic goodness. The artistic worth of the play is rooted in the author's natural gift for vivid speech and vivid characterizations rather than in any message. Till the Day I Die, on the other hand, lacks precisely these qualities, because Mr. Odet's ideological fervor burns at the expense of his people. 

It is true that the modern theater began as an adjunct of the Church, and that our first plays were moral illustrations of biblical tales. It is interesting that the recent theater in the U. S. S. R. has shown a tendency to revert to this process. Once again we see that an origin is one thing, and that a result is another. In art, as I have said, streams rise higher than their sources, and there is little to be gained by trying to make them flow backward.

IX. Music 1. MUSIC AND LANGUAGE 

Any method of communication is a language. The word language itself comes from the Latin word for tongue-lingua. 

Our own word tongue means, in addition to the organ itself, the speech that we have developed through the utilization, among other organs, of the tongue. Had it so occurred to our ancestors, they might just as well have named our means of oral communication dentage, after the teeth; for the teeth are an important element in shaping our speech. Or after the throat, or the larynx, or the palate, or the lungs, or the lips.

In slang, indeed, we say, "Don't give me any of your lip." A part, in the familiar process of linguistic and mental usage, has been made to stand for the whole. 

The status of music as a development and as an art is of the utmost importance in connection with the widely-ramified topic that prompted this monograph. The other arts, except painting, deal with words; and words, though one would not always suspect it when considering some of the more extreme forms that the poem, the novel, and even the drama, have taken, are inevitably associated with meaning. Thus they lend themselves naturally to propagandistic fervors. A word, however, is not only meaning in the sense of communicable dictionary significance. It is also sound-that is, in a deep sense, music. It not only denotes a thing or quality, but it connotes as well. That is, it calls up associations. If words, by themselves and in combination, had only dictionary significance, much of the pleasure of poetry and drama and novel and even oratory would be lost. Verbal style, in one phase, is meaning set to music-the music of words in succession, of their vowels and consonants, of the melody that they make as a group, the subtle matching of sound to sense in a harmony that enhances both elements. 

Painting has almost as powerful an appeal; with painting we group all the associated arts of sculpture, drawing, caricature, and even the more skilful type of camera photography, as well, of course, as cinematic skill. Whether it is the highest type of art for a picture to tell a story or advertise a program, these things unquestionably it has been made to do, with great practical success. Miss Dorothy Adlow, after a brief but concentrated tour of the Soviet art-galleries, remarked how naturally the Russian spirit took to the graphic, the poster elements, in painting. Music, however, though from the first it has been linked to words, possesses an independence that enables it to exist, at its best, without them. Even when words are joined to music; even when, for listening to a symphony, a program of imagined, accompanying action is provided, the music-as plastic, organized sound-exists for and by itself. We all have had the experience of listening to fine music sung to inane words; or we have heard excellent words subjected to most mediocre musical treatment. For all the joining of words to music, no good words ever turned bad music into good, and no bad music ever turned good words into bad. 

What has this to do with our subject? Almost everything.

Music is the "purest" of the arts because it lends itself least to the perversions of utility. One does not have to label a painting that is meant as propaganda; it tells its own story. 

The same with the arts of the word. Music, for propaganda purposes, requires a label; that label, of course, is the words cr the program to which the music is set. 

Such arguments as proving the social origin of music have little to do with the case. Nobody denies social origins, which, as I have already indicated, do not preclude individual contributions as well. Those social origins have evolved into highly individual (which need not mean individualistic, anti-social) forms. The quality of music as an art is not affected by the accident of its performance. Beethoven's third symphony possesses an intrinsic worth entirely independent of the quality of reproduction. By endorsing a number of his symphonies as exemplifying the proletarian outlook we hardly turn them into proletarian music; we do not alter their character in the least. 

At the basis of this endorsement, however, lies a focal error, which it is of primary importance to understand and to correct. 

Why, it is very pertinent to ask, are the first, third and fifth symphonies of Beethoven worthy of proletarian endorsement, and why should such endorsement be withdrawn from the symphonies of even number? And why should "much of

Schumann and Chopin, saving some of the fine tumultuous pieces," be laid aside for a while? Why this identification of proletariat with tumult?

Such criticism may have an historic interest, and even a value, but of what use is it esthetically? Even Nietzsche, that complicated weakling who wrote so herculeanly of strength, found a time for the warrior to rest from his labors and surrender to the blandishments of dalliance. It is bad psychology because it refuses to mankind the right to express all its emotions-the right to that healing utterance which lies at the core of the arts. It is bad propaganda because, rather than insist that the proletariat shall possess the great culture of the bourgeoisie, just as it will appropriate-not destroy or ignore the magnificent apparatus of production and distribution that Capitalism has developed, it heaps upon that culture a sort of green-grape scorn. It rationalizes the proletarian defect of mal-education in the arts, rather than insisting upon the completion of that education. I doubt seriously that the man who has no use for

They unify life; they do not categorize it. They proclaim and round out our being; they do not advocate.

It subjects it to standards that can be shown to affect the historical production of music, but which do not necessarily affect its esthetic values. 

2. "BOURGEOIS" AND "PROLETARIAN" MUSIC 

Nicolas Slonimsky, one of the most alert of contemporary musicologists, has pointed out that Soviet music shows a marked partiality for the major, as contrasted with the minor, mode. 

If this be so, it is further proof of an official attitude against what is regarded as "defeatism” in the arts. There are those of us, of course, who do not readily accept such elementary equations as "major equals happy" and "minor equals sad." Let that pass, however. Again, we are not so sure that all pre- 

Soviet music, in Russia, was sad, melancholy, introspective. 

Certainly, much of it was, when not in older national modes, in major keys. Again, let this pass also. The important consideration is this: one cannot, by fiat, rule out of art any of the fundamental emotions of life; one cannot create, by bureaucratic legislation, a national euphoria-a reign of optimism that is as false to the totality of living as is an unrelieved pessimism.

A resurgence of hope, such as seems to be characterizing certain strata of life in the U. S. S. R., naturally finds expression in the arts. This is of tremendous importance in many ways; but if mere joyousness of mood created great art, then 

Pollyanna would be among the classics of all time. Shostakovitch may christen a symphony with the title "May Day." It is not, however, in the potentialities of music to describe concretely. This same music, if it so pleased the French, could be called "July 14"; or, if the United States chose to do so, could be renamed "The 4th of July." The esthetic value of the music --as music-would not, in any case, be altered by a jot. 

Take, again, Mossolov's piece suggesting a Soviet foundry. 

It requires very little imagination to transfer the scene to any atmosphere where noise is naturally produced. A perverse listener, indeed, could easily transform the foundry into a counter-revolutionary demonstration! There is nothing in the music-as music-to prevent this. Music may be founded upon intellectual processes, upon intellectual convictions; it may exhibit, to the properly-trained listener, the marks of this intellectualism. Nevertheless, at bottom it is, like all the other arts, predominantly an emotional expression. Less than any of

the other arts can it be-without adding words or programs or forcing alien interpretations upon it-made into an instrument of propaganda. 

Chaikovsky is melancholy; Shostakovitch is optimistic, gay, satiric. Very well. Are these attributes sufficient for distinguishing between these composers from the standpoint of artistic values? Can any composer be superior to another solely because he is "optimistic" and the other is not? This would be too absurd to consider, were it not that some fine minds, in their surrender to a theory, are seen to be bogged in just such marshes of criticism as these. 

It would be a sad-an unbelievable-thing if the revolution of the U. S. S. R. did not produce, eventually, works of art more or less commensurate with the magnitude of the economic transformation. A renaissance is a rebirth engendered by the fresh convergence of new and old cultures, not by the doctrinaire, absolutist, self-blinding rejection of the old. Such a phrase as "bourgeois culture" may conceal much harmful error. First, because it judges art solely by its relationship to the economic structure. This is as much an error as to judge it solely by its reference to psycho-analysis, or to behaviorism, or to any other ism. Life and art are both too complex for such monistic, single-aspect interpretations. Life and art are not all politics and economics. Politics and economics are not all partisanship. The dictatorial cock-sureness with which doctrinaire Communists set up arbitrary standards for writers in the U. S. S. R., and the more or less subtle shifting to which they have been compelled to resort since the first days, might have taught them more humility than they seem as yet to have learned. By this same token, "proletarian culture" conceals much harmful error, too.

Beethoven's greatness as a composer has little to do with the society of his day, and much to do with Beethoven as an individual. The fact that there is only one Beethoven proves that however much he may have been, as we all are, the product of his times, he also transcended them, as every great artist does. Beethoven remains great beyond, above, outside of "bourgeois" culture. Beethoven, were he writing today in the U. S. S. R., would rise by force of his native ability, to leadership. He would not, in all likelihood, write stringquartets, or use other forms developed in an early day. Beethoven was a revolutionary personality, which need not mean revolutionary in economic thought. We do not ask symphonies of a Stalin, Lenin or Marx; why should we ask economic treatises in sound from our Beethovens? If they are revolutionaries in economics, so much the better, but we must beware of confusing categories. Wagner was an anti-Semite. Does that mean that I, a Jew, must deny his superhuman contribution to music? Not unless I desire to match his regrettable error with shortsightedness equally regrettable. And as for the "bour-

Beethoven applies, of course, to Wagner. If the U. S. S. R. produces a great composer he or she will be great beyond, above, outside of "proletarian" culture. To seek to practice such imposition upon music is to expose the fallacy of one-sided over-zealousness in all its nakedness. 

Is all this an argument against Communism? Hardly. I am not discussing economics primarily. I am discussing the abuse, by Communists in power, of a theory that may be applied to the history and evolution of the arts in their more practical aspects, but not nearly so much as they imagine to the individual, esthetic, centrally artistic problems involved. 

Similarly, to maintain the artistic supremacy of a Beethoven or a Wagner is hardly to endorse, necessarily, the special phases of Capitalism-or psychology or science or brick-laying or medicine-under which they happened to have been born. It is a world of complex interrelationships, but it is not of one piece, and no man or ism may hope to offer the one key to the solution of its problems. 

When the great "proletarian" composer comes along, he will be, in all human likelihood, a far greater composer than proletarian; if, indeed, he'll have any very clear notion of what the proletariat is. More: I predict and it requires little skill at prophecy-that when a truly Marxian proletariat has evolved in the U. S. S. R. (to think that one already exists is to mistake a blue-print for the building) it will look down upon the novels, plays, music and other art forms that are at present being prostituted to create a "culture" of indoctrinated spoonfeeding. Art, as Gorki and his co-workers recently discovered, does not respond to five-year plans. Nor does such a thing as inspiration (or whatever you choose to call it) vanish because it doesn't happen to fit in with bureaucratic impatience and incomprehension. Where art is not free, the mind is not free; where the mind is not free, all talk of freedom is an academic exercise. To throttle expression in the name of a brighter future is merely another version of "pie in the sky."

To enforce conformity is not even pie in the sky; it is to create hell on earth. A great man has said that man learns to use

freedom by having it. To take it away, in any cause whatever, is by that same token to atrophy one of the fundamental qualities that makes life worth the living. Part of that life is the right to, the need of, artistic expression. 

In no other sphere of art does the folly of regimentation and the reductio ad absurdum of doctrinaire theory appear as plainly as in that of music. Already, for example, through a moralistic application of Marxism, there has been a call from some Communists for a liquidation of jazz. Stupid is the one word for this fervor. There is good jazz and bad jazz. The bad-like most of the music mistakenly produced as "proletarian" art-goes the way of all mediocrity; the good remains good, regardless of its economic implications, because it is good music, not because "proletarians" can or cannot play it or like it. 

3. MUSIC MINUS PATRIOTISM 

Let us consider, as an object lesson in a sphere that is cverlaid with almost irremovable associations, some national songs. Let us, at once, dissociate the words from the music. No matter how good the words as poetry, no matter how noble the words as sentiment, they have no organic relationship to the music as music. And we are concerned, it must be remembered, with esthetic, musical values. It is difficult, of course, to free oneself from the associations that cluster about national hymns. We love them or hate them for what they suggest. By the same token, if an American musician, thinking only of music, dares to say that the Star-Spangled Banner is a mediocre tune, abominable for mass-singing, he is at once regarded as a dangerous Red, even by many fellow musicians. And, I fear me, if one were, in the same spirit, to question the musical value of The Internationale, one would receive white looks from red eyes. As a matter of fact, and of music, The Internationale is little superior to the Star-Spangled Banner. It cannot, any more than the American national anthem, compare with The Watch On The Rhine, or the magnificent national hymn of old Austria, or even the Russian hymn that it replaced. Neither the hymn of the U. S. nor of the U. S. S. R., is musically on the same plane with the Dutch national anthem or with The Marseillaise. 

It is manifestly silly to suspect a musician who delivers these opinions of sympathy, ipso facto, for the nations whose anthems he prefers and of hostility to those nations whose anthems he does not favor. Love of country, love of a cause, do not commit one-except under dictatorial pressure to love everything relating to that country or that cause. "Love me, love my dog" is a motto for distinctly inferior persons.

The social, propagandistic uses to which national songs may be put are tremendous. The effect of social conditions upon musical output may be vital. The esthetic value of the

Isaac Goldberg 113 product, however, cannot be foretold from the justice or the injustice of the social structure. This is surely no argument against a more equitable society. It is an argument, however, against a too facile equation between that equitability and esthetic worth. It is altogether conceivable that somewhere, in contemporary Germany, a lone composer is at the moment -in defiance of all conditions-creating a masterpiece of his art. The conditions of a society affect an art socially-affect its practice, its cultivation; they do not necessarily stifle the creative powers of the capable individual. If the careless reader takes this as an argument in favor of tyrannical government, I am afraid that he has missed my point entirely. 

We demand freedom for the artist because such a congenial social atmosphere favors the emergence of the great individual. That great individual, who is the flower and not the enemy of the collective group, may very subtly be subjected also by a mistaken conception of what the function of the collectivity should be. 

The Marxian criticism, as applied to the arts, is at best but a partial explanation. To apply it without skill or taste is to run all the dangers of Procrusteanism. It helps to elucidate the relation of artist to society. This is a primary function. 

It is not, however, and cannot hope to be, a sole criterion. Its emphases are inevitably economic and least of all esthetic. It clarifies historic and sociological inter-relationships; it cannot determine, of itself, artistic values. It may, indeed, lead to the neglect of such values by setting itself up as an only, uncontested standard. And such an esthetic dictatorship is the most humorless of all depotisms. It is all the more humorless for being unnecessary. 

Love even Soviet love-laughs at locksmiths. Great art -and music above all the other arts-laughs at dogmas.

X. Conclusions 1. THE "DISMAL" SCIENCE AND THE "GAY" 

Economics has been called the "dismal science." Poetry (and we may well extend the adjective to art in general) has been called the "gay" science. Yet, between the two lies no great gulf fixed, and a pliant mind may discover, even in the pages of Marx's Capital, a vein of masked poetry running through the statistics and the formulae. In the same way, the auxiliary studies in the techniques of the various arts have a "dismal" aspect that recalls, for many, the arid purlieus of economics. In a sense, this monograph is an affirmation of the "gay" science against the unwarranted encroachment of the "dismal." The arts are more inclusive, by very nature, than economics. Or, inclusive of distinctly different interests. This establishes, not a superiority but a difference. 

Such a term as "proletarian art" attempts to wed the "gay" and the "dismal" sciences. As we have seen, the offspring may suggest a misalliance. The trouble at the root of the forced union-for the impatience of the zealots is a hot-house process -is tacitly and perhaps unconsciously admitted by the nature of Communist apologetics. Let me offer a couple of examples from a recent issue of The New Masses, May 21, 1935.

Earl Browder is answering a question prompted by his series, "What Is Communism?" "Question: How can The New Masses demand a free press in America when in Russia, our only example of 

Communism, there is no such thing? "Answer: This apparently simple question is, when analyzed, a whole series of questions. First of all, for whom are we demanding freedom of the press? For the workers, for the people who perform the labor of society. 

And what is the situation of the workers in Russia (it is now the Soviet Union, by the way, no longer Russia!) regarding freedom of the press? Why, they have it—a glorious freedom, in which they have multiplied the issue of their press from the few tens of thousands per year of the pre-revolutionary times, to the 37 millions per day of the present! So we see, the moment we ask 'freedom for whom?' it becomes clear that we are demanding for American workers a little bit of what the Russian workers have gained entirely."

This is, to say the least, slightly misleading. I am not 114

Isaac Goldberg 115 apologizing for the craven American press, which will, as Browder goes on to say, be properly hushed at the first sign of real danger to our Capitalist structure. The fact remains, however, that in the United States of this moment The New Masses has more freedom than it would have in the U. S. S. R. today. 

The fact remains that the Soviet workingman has only the "glorious freedom" to agree with the faction in power. Once he sneezed when the Czar took snuff; now he sneezes when

Stalin takes snuff. There is no real debate, no permission for dissent to raise its voice, no existence of parties. The "gloriour freedom" reduces itself to a freedom of ratification. Freedom within the confines of a dominant faction that brooks no opposition is a parody, not a reality, no matter how rosy the future is made to appear. The American workingman has, at present, plenty of what the Soviet workingman has little. The case is the reverse of what Browder so glibly presents in his sophist manner. If our workingman does not use it, that is his loss and misfortune. But let us beware of letting the beauty of a theory gloss over the unpleasantness of harsh realities. 

In the same issue are reproduced, from "Crocodile," half a dozen caricatures, under the impressive title, "Self-Criticism In Soviet Cartoons." The implication is that the toilers of the 

U. S. S. R. have really taken their "glorious freedom" in hand and begun to talk out loud, instead of whispering their real comments to trusted foreign correspondents. What is the truth?

We discover that the freemen actually pluck up the courage to suggest that the pastry tastes like kerosene, that furniture drawers are recalcitrant, that Soviet hands can be dirty, that Soviet matches do not ignite. . . . To label this mild satire "self-criticism" is in itself a damaging confession. The Capitalist cartoonists of this country, by comparison, are savage denouncers. An editorial note speaks of "vigilant, ruthless 

Bolshevik self-criticism." If these cartoons are a sample, we shall have to alter our vocabulary. Could the cartoons of The 

New Masses, if directed against Stalinism with the same venom that they employ against Rooseveltism, appear in Moscow? 

Self-criticism, indeed!

Let us not add, to the economic injury done for so many centuries to the proletariat, esthetic insult. Let us cease from rationalizing their poverty, whether of material possessions or of cultural advantages, into a desirable status. It is not. 

For the proletariat, nothing but the universal best. . . . A best that they will share gladly with the rest of humanity. By universal I mean the best that any age or any class has ever produced. For, in art, as I must keep repeating, the great creation transcends the creator, the epoch, the social configuration. Art is, to phrase it in a crude metaphor, among other things spiritual sustenance. Ultimately, considering only its higher manifestations, it is no more a class product-solely—

than are the foodstuffs that keep both Capitalist and Proletarian alive. I deny categorically (the words of Mr. Hicks addressing the College Conference on English in The Central Atlantic States on December 1, 1934) that "any work of literature presents some sort of world-attitude, and that it must be judged in terms of the author's world attitude and his success in communicating it." Later in his speech Mr. Hicks himself subtly refutes his main thesis. "An author," he says, "may have formulated a world-attitude that, if he were a philosopher, would be admirable, and yet his poem or his story may be worthless. He may, on the other hand, have no conscious philosophy, or only a very banal one, and yet be a great writer." 

We judge a writer as a writer, and a carpenter as a carpenter. Why introduce confusing irrelevancies, or, if certain conceptions are relevant (such as world-attitudes) why make them the sole, obligatory criteria? The world is wide, deep, round. . . . Why seek to reduce it, particularly in its cultural aspect, to a single dimension? Is this a service to humankind?

Is it not, at bottom, a refusal to see all the elements involved? 

Is it not, indeed, a form of that very "escape" which some contemporary critics glibly condemn without thoroughly understanding?

Karl Marx rose to the glorious-if perhaps never completely practical-conception of the "classless" society. 

Let us have more appreciators of the arts, then, who can rise to the parallel conception of a "classless" criticism. . . 

2. LIBERTY, EQUALITY, FRATERNITY 

John Addington Symonds, in his monumental work on the Renaissance In Italy, has written a few sentences that may well serve as epigraph to these pages. Nor shall I allow his use of the word "God" to deter me from quoting the lines in full. The Renaissance was a long pageant of paradox that finds its counterpart in our own paradoxical day. As with us, so in the days of Leonardo and Michelangelo, the Medicis, the Borgias, the Popes with bastard children and of little faith, the Emperors with vast agrarian greed, barbarity and culture went hand in hand. War-Leonardo called it the "pazzia bestialissima": the most bestial madness-strode full-panoplied through the museums and the libraries; with the one hand men wrought imperishable masterpieces, only to lunge, with the other, into the monstrosities of maniacal contention. 

It is, unfortunately, a human bi-polarity-an ambivalence of moods and motives, for which the earlier and the more enduring name is paradox: the vital co-existence of contraries such as the passion for freedom and the cry for, the submission to, despots. 

Writes Symonds, "Whether the Utopia of a modern world in which all men shall enjoy the same social, political and in-

Isaac Goldberg 117 tellectual advantages be realized or not, we cannot doubt that the whole movement of humanity from the Renaissance onward has tended in this direction. To destroy the distinctions, mental and physical, which nature raises between individuals and which constitute an actual hierarchy, will always be impossible. Yet it may happen that in the future no civilized man will lack the opportunity of being physically and mentally the best that God has made him."

God, once a deity, has been demoted to the status of a metaphor. Symonds, however, like the movement of humanity from the Renaissance onward, looked in the right direction. 

He beheld-as wise men of all beliefs will ever behold-perfectability, not perfection-relativities, not absolutes. A properly constituted society, such as one hopes will evolve from that new Renaissance of blood, absolutism and hope which is called the U. S. S. R., should do much to mitigate, if not to destroy, many of the distinctions between one human being and another that formerly seemed to be an ineradicable visitation of the Lord.

Yet it is self-deception to confuse between equality and equitability-between an impossible absolute and a kindly, collective attempt to give one a fair chance in a non-competitive, non-profiteering commonwealth. Men are not, despite the 

Declaration of Independence and the noble motto of the French 

Revolution, born free and equal. Liberty, Equality, Fraternity. ... Of these, the first is surely relative and in part illusory; the second utopian; the third, alone, humanly possible of attainment.

It is easy for propagandists to speak too simply of a "classless" society. We cannot keep in mind too constantly that "classless" refers to economic strata. Despite all that a better social system can do to plane away the differences dividing humankind, inevitable and ineradicable differences remain ---superiorities or inferiorities inherent in the accidents of inheritance and environment, in temperament, nervous organization, mental and physical endowment, the incidence of disease —in a word, in all those factors over which the best aims of man have little or no control.

The "classless" society can insure only the end of exploitation for private profit, and the disappearance of the evils that are bred by such exploitations. And this is much indeed! It cannot reduce mankind to a dead, or even a live, level. It should not, and does not, wish to. Classes-and classifications --of non-economic nature will surely exist under any grouping of human beings, as they always have existed. Always there will be leaders, and always there will be led. The majority of people desire-consciously or unconsciously to follow leadership. A higher type of society will open to all the opportunities of developing their finest potentialities, but it will not cultivate in every individual the delusion of grandeur. Democ-

racies, of whatever type, have always erred in this direction; the United States has made a fetish of the notion that every boy has an equal chance to become President. At its most sentimental, the delusion breaks out in such ridiculous verse as "The Poor Man's Vote On Election Day." 

A "proletarian" society is by no means free of similar delusions. The U. S. S. R., rather than admit the low status of its peasantry-rather than accept and proclaim the necessity of reculer pour mieux sauter, of going back a few paces in order to get a good start for a new jump, officially accepts theories in art that flatter popular ignorance and make it the criterion of achievement. This is a subtle disservice to the proletariat and to the artist alike. It may be desirable to skip economic epochs, and to speed from agrarianism to industrialism overnight. Art-systems are not economic-systems, and may exist simultaneously. Each school has brought its contribution to the vitality of human expression. It remains for Soviet zealots to learn that not all differences are necessarily oppositions, and that it is short-sighted to evaluate a work of art solely, or chiefly, by a superficial reference to the social-economic system under which it was produced. 

At the Writer's Congress, which convened in New York toward the end of April, 1935, words of confirmed fanaticism were spoken; but also words of wisdom. Clearly, there are signs of a return to esthetic sanity. I refer especially to part of the address by Waldo Frank, as reproduced in The New 

Masses of May 7, 1935; the emphasis is Frank's: "The American revolutionary writer . . . to act his part, which is to create the cultural medium for revolution must see life whole. He will have a political creed; if he is a generous man, it will be hard for him to forego some share of the daily political-industrial struggle. But his political orientation must be within, must arise from, his orientation to life as an artist. Any course of action, any creed, lives within the dynamic substance of life itself: and this substance, in all its attributes, is the business of the artist. Therefore it is proper to state that the artist's vision of life IS the material of his art. "There is much confusion among us as to 'material' and 'subject.' The subject of a book is a mere label or container; it may mislead or be empty. Our poet or proseman, by his loyalty to the working class (whether born in it or not) and by natural selection of strong, expressive subjects, will write more and more of the struggles of farmer and worker. But if his vision be sound, it will make -whatever his subject-the material for revolutionary .art. The term 'proletarian' applied to art should refer to the key and vision in which the work is conceived, rather than to subject. It should be a qualitative, not quantitative, term. "We writers have two highways for reaching mastery of our material. We must go into life . . . in person and in self. These two ways are really one; and the writer must go them together, else he will make headway in neither. If we look upon persons or classes, save with the eye of self-knowledge, we will not see them; and if we look inward upon self, save with an eye disciplined by objective understanding, we will see only the mists of egoism which are the true self's denial. Even more complex is this double way we must take, and never cease from taking. If we look upon persons of one class, we will not know them unless we see the class opposing. If we look upon the present of any scene, we will not know it unless we see within it the past . . . and its dynamic direction: its future. This is the dialectic of the artist. "Because classes are in mortal conflict, and because we have taken sides, does not mean they have nothing in common: It means they have life in common. The class struggle, for us, is a focus of light, a modern form, by which timeless ingredients of human nature common to every person are revealed. It is not a substitute for understanding, but a kind of spectrum wherein hunger, passion, love, pity, envy, worship, dream, fear, despair and ecstasy, receive a dynamic modern order."

These are wise words that the growing intelligentsia of the proletariat should take to heart. I have been dwelling on the central quality of disinterestedness. Mr. Frank's address, for all its open taking of sides, exhibits that noble quality in its most humane and, at the same time, its most practical aspects.

Indeed, I have maintained that Marx's conception of the classless society is, basically, the very archetype of disinterestedness. Disinterestedness is receptivity of experience, eagerness for totality of experience, the meeting place of art and science, superiority to personal advantage. Disinterestedness in criticism is-as far as is humanly possible, and certainly more so than is possible in the harsh actualities of livingclassless, undoctrinated. If this be Utopian, surely the classless society is an even remoter mirage. 

Whatever, in art, is really true, will be true to all of life, not to a tiny section. It will be true to whatever is true in any ism, not only to a limited "truth" of a particular ism. 

Mr. Lewisohn writes a history of American literature and evaluates it chiefly in terms of Freud. Mr. Calverton and Mr. 

Hicks examine the same field with a similar reference to Marx, each with a personal interpretation of that reference. Mr.

Schmalhausen attempts to rewrite the art-science of psychiatry in terms of a proclamative Communism. Such partisan activities have their value; in themselves they may be not very evenly balanced, but they help, nevertheless, to restore balance to the consideration of the subjects as a whole. Marx is not infallible; Nietzsche is not altogether wrong. Even in the sentimental, intuitional, unscientific, aristocratic divagations of a Count Gobineau, there is an element of wisdom for a proletarian state, just as in some of the details of Pareto's re-discovered sociology there are valuable observations-the same holds true for such philosophers as Spengler-that may be utilized without in the least accepting the general thesis or any of its larger implications. 

The attitude of many Communist theorists toward "inspiration" and "pure" science or "pure" art is narrow and unimaginative. Any one who has the slightest knowledge of the history of science knows how certain investigations, seemingly removed by astronomical distance from the concerns of daily living, have led to such epoch-making revolutions as the entire age of electricity. The effects of "pure" art may be, in the sphere of the ever-expanding human spirit, no less revolutionary. The stream of life must not be allowed to become stagnant, no matter how lofty the cause in which this retrogression is invoked. 

In upholding the esthetic approach to the arts, to life as a whole, I do not oppose it to any other approach. It is not necessary to oppose Marx to Frued, for example; they may, with a little skill and patience, be integrated. It is not wise to reject, without thought, anything as a whole, or to accept anything in that same totalitarian spirit. The esthetic approach digests, so to speak, all the contrary or seemingly contrary isms of the historic, sociological, moralistic attitudes. Without depriving them of such special virtues as they may possess, it uses them for wider, deeper, happier living. It integrates them. 

It makes of them means; it becomes, through them, a higher means rather than an end in itself. Freeing men at last from bondage to rigid systems, it re-organizes life for the needs and pleasures of mankind. It liberates life for living. 3. ENVOI

In ostensible form, the book is an address given by Folly to her host of admirers (i. e., the human race) and pointing out the many blessings which Folly confers upon her devotees. Erasmus did the work of an intellectual and moral revolutionist.

But he wrote this book, in defiance of all censorship, which sheds light and joy to this day-a book which will always make men think, with the finest combination of sportive, jocose characterization and smiling wisdom fresh from contact with the common contrasts of life.
