because one believes that a divine being requires it, that honesty is related to destiny and eternity, then one's sanction for being honest is religion; it is also ultra-rational. If one is honest because one has discovered honesty to be the best policy, then one's sanction for honesty is philosophy; here the sanction is rational. Religion makes its way by appealing to the religious instincts; philosophy makes its way by argument. Religion speaks to the heart, philosophy to the intellect. Philosophy has been usually looked upon by religion as a helpful ally, but a poor substitute for religion. It lacks unction. It does not baptize in fire. It is morality; but religion is morality plus emotion. Those who have attempted most to rationalize religion seem to make the least headway. 

In its very essence religion is super-rational. 

Religion has not looked upon science so kindly. It has often been said that there is no conflict between religion and science. True, science has no quarrel with religion as such. It does seek, however, to solve all mystery, to discover natural causes for phenomena, to build an orderly world. In doing this, science has materially weakened the ancient foundations of religion. The modern man cannot believe in an inerrant Bible. We know now that we live in an orderly world governed not by caprice, but by law. 

Many like Matthew Arnold say that miracles never happen. Others like Professor Sanday tell us that miracles happened and that by taking a little off of both ends, and assuming that the wonder worker took hold of laws higher up than his contemporaries were able to reach, we can understand them. We

have no objections to the explanation but what is left is not a miracle, according to the old definition of miracle, but a new discovery in science, just as wireless telegraphy is. Our point is that when miracles are related to law rather than to God they lose much if not all their religious value. Jesus is represented as being able to do his mighty works not because he learned the laws of God, but because he found the heart of God. On the latter ground miracles have religious values, on the former they have scientific values.

We cannot help but feel that all miracles like the floating axe, the standing sun, the path through the sea with congealed walls of water, are tolerated by my lawyer friends rather than believed. Certain it is that no amount of evidence could convince them that any such miracles could take place now. To believe in something that under no circumstance could ever happen again may have some value as an exercise of faith but it can have no value as a sanction for conduct; that is to say, such belief has no religious value. It is only so much luggage. Our intellectual garrets are full of this kind of junk. We know it is useless but we can't quite throw it away. This is not to say, however, that this discard has not served a useful purpose nor that we do not need something better to take the place of the old. We fear in fact that our religious apartments are getting bare because we are discarding the old sanctions and failing to replace the old with new.

Not only has religion lost this super-rational sanction, but what is (perhaps) more serious, the sacred-

ness, the reverence, the mystery that things religious once had. Modernism stresses service, forgetting that religion without awe can do little for the soul in the great stresses of life. We once had a friend who was brought up a Catholic but became a preacher in a Protestant denomination. As a boy, it was his duty to minister in some way with the communion bread. To him it was holy bread that must not be touched, so sacred was it. By accident he barely touched the bread with one finger. He was greatly distressed. As he had only grazed it with the finger nail, however, he found relief in the sophistry that the dead nail at the end of the finger was in reality no vital part of himself and perhaps it was not so serious as he at first had thought. In the country church in which I was brought up, the communion service was held each Lord's Day. At the close of the service the children gathered about the table and the communion bread was given to the children to piece on. There is no thought of holy or sacred bread. The house of God, the Lord's table are commonplace. There is no awe, no mystery, no holy of holies, no reverence, no atmosphere of worship. "How can we sing the songs of Jehovah in a strange land?" The prying eye of knowledge has uncovered the mysteries that made religion a reality and a solace, a deterrent and an inspiration to the fathers. 

The fundamentalist is right then when he charges that criticism and science has undermined the urge of our religious sanctions by rationalizing our religion. The fundamentalist is wrong, however, when he defends an irrational sanction. For if a rational 10

sanction becomes philosophy an irrational sanction becomes hypocrisy to its priests and superstition to its votaries. What science has really destroyed is not religion but superstition. The net result, however, has been the same, since it has destroyed the sanction that superstition carried with it. Superstition agrees with religion in that it also compels by an unrational sanction; and one may be easily mistaken for the other. Superstition controls conduct just as religion may. There are good and bad superstitions just as there are good and bad religions. I think that I would be warranted in saying religion is only bad when it is tied up with a bad superstition. Many superstitions are bound up in religion. 

These superstitions are not always harmful. Indeed they may be useful as sanctions for right action. The myth of Santa Claus was at one time tied up with religion. It was so beautiful a sanction for good fellowship that we teach it to our children and keep up the pretense of believing it long after we have outgrown it. Of course it always was a superstition, but it was also a religion. The belief of a fire and brimstone hell was, a generation ago, a powerful deterrent from wrong and a motive for right action. 

The pulpit at times still tells us that it believes in this hell but not many take it seriously; it is still a deterrent, however, to those who do. But as much as we may wish it otherwise, like Santa Claus, that kind of a hell has become a myth, a superstition to many, and has almost no value as a deterrent from wrong or incentive to right doing. This fiery hell, this powerful religious sanction or deterrent from evil, this 11

tremendous motive that helped to lift the common good of the social organism above the individual good has become to many an irrational superstition with no value as a deterrent from evil or a stimulant for good; and rational motives such as law, philosophy, altruism, must bear the load that the dread of this particular hell shared in this particularly difficult task. While there are many other motives, to plead the cause of the social organism against selfish interests, yet so insistent is self that right and brotherhood can ill afford to lose a single ally. The kingdom will hardly come by the aid of these rational sanctions alone, they must be aided by these powerful allies holding unrational or ultra-rational sanctions, the greatest of which is religion. 

Lest someone should say that we live too late in the day to be swayed by the unrational; that man was given reason as his sole sanction for his acts, I hasten to say that religion is not alone in its urge of the unrational. Love and hate and their institutions, marriage and war are as unrational as religion. Patriotism is unrational. On learning that much of vice and many of our miseries are due to ignorance we jump to the conclusion that education and reason are our saviors. But education and reason hold their menace as well as ignorance. Dr. 

H. H. Laughlin, of the Carnegie Institute has worked out some alarming facts in his Fecundity Index. According to intelligence tests our native born whites have about four times as many men who are capable of producing a high type of leadership as our foreign born; but our foreign born have three 12

times as many children as our native born white Americans. But this by no means tells the story. The best tenth, he says, the graduates from our colleges, do not reproduce themselves. Of this one in ten today, there will be only one in four hundred in one hundred years from now. The lesson is this; instinct threatens to engulf the United States in one hundred years with a breed of inferior men. But education or reason holds over the United States the same threat by impoverishing the race. Instinct alone will engulf us from the bottom, reason alone will allow us to die at the top. Rational birth control can only postpone the day of reckoning. Where reason rules, birth control has already been pushed to the vanishing point. What is needed here is a superrational urge such as religion might give. Racial hatreds now threaten the world. Reason and philosophy tell us that these hatreds are puerile but these sanctions are too weak to save us from hatreds. If we are saved at all it will be through unrational love. We will not be saved from war by rational preparedness alone but unrational trust. We are not saved from our foibles, beastliness and passions by our reason but by our unrational aspirations of the spirit. Reason slows down our processes, it cools our temperament. Philosophy could give us morals but it cannot seem to generate passion to enforce them. 

None of the controlling emotions, love, fear, trust, hatred, these emotions that carry men off their feet, that mar and make men are rational. So religion must provide an ultra-rational sanction to lift the interest of the social organism above the antagonis- 13

tic individual interest, but in this it is not as unique as one might have thought. We reach this strange paradox, that the most rational being in the world is most moved by unrational or ultra - rational sanctions.

The Twentieth Century is witnessing two great disillusionments.

First, is the disillusionment regarding science. In the seventies we had a galaxy of brilliant men, scientists, who were the near-heroes of their day, eclipsing the poets, the statesmen, the philosophers and the theologians. Among these scientists were 

Darwin, Tyndall and Huxley. Hardly less brilliant was the school of the high priests of the scientists, we might call them, who became their interpreters and gave the results of science to the commoner people in popular essays and in systems of synthetic philosophies. Among these were Herbert Spencer, Matthew 

Arnold, John Morley, Frederick Harrison, and many others whose names were household words with the reading public a few years ago. These all told us that growth is unceasing: slow, to be sure, but irresistible in its subtle influence, carrying forward peoples and races, grinding in pieces and crushing old customs and laws as a slow moving glacier plows its plodding way grinding up and crushing whatever is in its path. They named this process "evolution."

Its movements were unfolding as the word signifies. 

The poet's concept of human progress was even more true of the universe where Nature, using her dead self as stepping stones climbs to higher and yet higher things; even evils that menace the present are 14

but struggling beneficences. Sin is but immaturity.

Onward, always, evolution moved toward complete development and an unmixed good, subordinating all petty irregularities, all recessions as an incoming tide subordinates each back-flow on the beach until the crest is reached. 

It was a fascinating faith, this faith in science and evolution, which saw greater beauty each day as some new harmony or incompleted process that must eventually arrive. And science has done much to justify faith for it often succeeded where religion failed. For centuries religion limped along making a scapegoat of devil or demon that was blamed for every ill. Was a man sick or crazy, a demon had taken up his abode in the body and the priest set about making the body as uncomfortable as possible to frighten the demon away. Science made the body as comfortable as possible so that it would have more reserve force to fight disease germs. When an epidemic came along religion gathered the people into the churches to pray that the plague be stayed, thus scattering more widely the disease. Science dispersed the crowds and sent them to their homes and quarantined the sick and sought for the cause and sent out sanitation squads. Religion has relied much on the magic of its ceremonies, science on the mastery of laws. It is common to hear religion given the credit for practically all the world's progress. 

This is a gratuitous assumption even for Christianity. Christianity has often done exactly the wrong thing as instanced above. It is only when religion links itself with science that progress is made. More 15

than two hundred years ago San Xavier mission was founded in Arizona. The big surprise to me when I saw it, was not the ancient mission that still stands but the little progress that the Indians have made under two hundred years of Christian training.

This I presume is true on every mission field where the missionary thinks his work complete when he has brought to heathen peoples the gospel without the arts that science has given us. 

But if Christianity without the aid of science has shown itself unable to develop a race it is also true that science alone is impotent. We will let Mrs. 

Beatrice Webb (for years a leader in English social thinking and nourished in this school of science and who cherished this deep faith in the science of the seventies of which we have just spoken), tell us in her own way her own disillusionment. We quote from her autobiography running in the Survey Graphic. "In these latter days of deep disillusionment," she writes, "now that we have learned, by the bitter experience of the great war, to what vile uses the methods and results of science may be put, when these are inspired and directed by brutal instinct and base motive, it is hard to understand the naïve belief of the most original and vigorous minds of the seventies and eighties that it was by science, and by science alone, that all human misery would be ultimately swept away. This almost fanatical faith was perhaps partly due to hero worship. For who will deny that the men of science were the leading British intellectuals of that great period; that it was they who stood out as men of genius with inter- 16

national reputations; that it was they who were the self-confident militants of the period; that it was they who were routing the theologians, confounding the mystics, imposing their theories on philosophers, their inventions on capitalists, and their discoveries on medical men; whilst snubbing the artists, ignoring the poets and even casting doubts on the capacity of the politicians ?"'¹

The second disillusionment concerns liberalism. 

Many have thought that since infidels, Jews and pagan philosophers all alike admire Jesus, the founder of Christianity, that the only thing needed to make Christianity acceptable to all was to rationalize it; to strip it of myths that still clung to its sacred Book, and of the magic that for some still clings to its ordinances; to revise its creed down to date, in short to divest Christianity of all that science makes irrational for our day and therefore superstition. But the surprising thing to many modernists is that when Christianity has been stripped of these impedimenta, these handicaps of the irrational, and is ready for the race it does not seem to have the power, or zest, or willingness to run. In some of these trappings laid aside we must look for the spur that urges to the goal. We believe this spur to religious activity is found in the unrational in religion, the very thing that modernism felt was a handicap in traditional religion. Paradoxical as it may seem we believe that the unrational in the traditional religion is both a spur and a handicap. To the traditionalist it is a spur, to the liberalist it is a

handicap. To the traditionalist this unrational in religion is still ultra-rational; to the modernist it is irrational. A man may find a spur in the ultrarational but the irrational is always and everywhere a superstition, therefore a drag.

When the ultra-rational becomes to us irrational it has lost all religious value for us. Storms, floods, famines, earthquakes, wars, victory and defeat, sickness and health, were all signs of God's pleasure or displeasure in Old Testament times. In a vague way they may still be to some now, but for the most part we now think of these things as unrelated to conduct or to God's pleasure or displeasure with men. All these so potent once as super-rational sanctions to conduct, for most of us, have ceased to have religious value. That is they are no longer related to conduct. The modernist was cramped mostly on the intellectual side and he made his adjustments by throwing over-board all those traditional sanctions set out in the old Semitic categories of an unscientific age. The result was that his religion drifted into a philosophy or what some would term a rational religion, though we believe this is a contradiction of terms. On the ethical side the liberalist has found the traditional religion too individualistic for this complex age. He saw a whole social order needing redemption. There are great problems in this industrial age that must be solved. How can industry be 

Christianized? Science has shriveled our world until every man is our back yard neighbor. Races, white, black and yellow touch elbows. How shall they live 18

together in brotherly love? How shall the world be saved from war?

All these and many more social problems the liberalist sees. Intellectually modernism comprehends its task, we think, as perhaps no other generation has. Here and there one has arisen and has spoken passionately of the "social gospel," but up to the present it has not developed real fervor for the ethics that stirred the heart of Jesus. It seems to see the problems that Jesus saw but it does not have the passion that Jesus had. It has accomplished its intellectual task but the greater moral task of awakening the social conscience and leading it out of the confusion of our day is a task hardly begun. Moral and spiritual progress is not keeping pace with the marvelous material progress of our age. Modernism is not agonizing over the social gospel as traditional religion did over the individual gospel. There is no such passion to bring the kingdom to this earth below and thus answer the prayer of Jesus and the vision of John who saw the New Jerusalem coming down out of heaven to earth, as there is to bring individuals to the kingdom of heaven above. Modernism has the new old gospel but it does not have the old "new birth." It has forgotten the words of Jesus to 

Nicodemus, "Except ye be born from above ye can not see the kingdom of God." Its high morality is cold and untouched by emotion. Having cast out the irrational sanctions the modernist is left limp with a religion that is little more than a philosophy, that satisfies well the intellect but does not satisfy either the heart or the emotions. Many modernists have 19

keenly felt this impotence of liberalism either to satisfy the heart or to do its work. We remember that several years ago R. G. Campbell of the City

Temple, London, wrote and spoke from his pulpit most frankly as a liberal but found it unsatisfactory to himself and that it failed to attract those who had clamored loudest for it. 

Lewis Browne in his Stranger Than Fiction, makes this same criticism on Liberal Judaism. He says, "According to this ideal the essence of religion lay not in praying for the good of one's soul but in striving for the well-being of mankind. The truest 

Jew was seen to be the person who labored most earnestly to bring on a Reign of Peace among men. 

Unfortunately, however, Reformed Judaism seemed unable to put its preaching into practice. In every temple in Germany and America there was fulsome talk of Israel's mission but little effort to carry it out. 

Perhaps the severest criticism one can make of the new movement: 'It knew exactly what the Jew ought to do but failed to induce him to do it!'" If we insert 

Liberal Christianity here in the place of Liberal Judaism we would have an equally fitting description. 

To all this the traditionalist would say since modernism is unqualified for its task then why not return to the fold of traditionalism? The answer is: first, we cannot understand the educated traditionalist who is able to keep two water-tight compartments in which his mind seems to work, one for his religious thinking, the other for social and cultural thinking. 

The modernist knows that there must be the will to believe, yet he can't force himself to believe as he 20

will. And the second answer is that the traditionalist is succeeding far less than the modernist with all literate younger people who insist that religion must not be irrational and who find the categories of 

Semitic religion of an unscientific age impossible.

Traditionalism is keenly aware of this and this is why it is attempting to bar and has barred in several states the teachings of such scientific conclusions as conflict with its cherished beliefs. But modernism does need and many modernists long for a prophet to come who can satisfy the emotions as well as the intellect. Who can touch the dry bones of intellectualism, clothe them with flesh and cause them to walk forth to conflict and victory? Who can and will tell us, not of the periphery of things, we can all do that -but of the great central problems of God and his relations to man? We need a great commanding affirmation of God, of immortality, of sin and salvation that has passion in it, and this too based not on the old irrational sanctions of the Sixteenth Century and beyond but on the ultra-rational sanctions of the 

Twentieth Century and beyond. Religion for our day waits on this man. Until he comes religion will not die but it will languish and function poorly. If we have been right in our observations then we will not be saved by philosophy and science for these are unmoral in themselves and appeal to no compelling sanction to enforce their obligations. If religion, which has appealed in the past to sanctions that did lift the social organism above the individual good, is to live and function it must not be built on irrational sanctions that offend reason, but on the ultra-rational 21

sanctions that are the natural and inevitable complements of reason. Religion for the modern man can no longer rest on phenomena peculiar to the childhood of the race. It can not rest on dogmas that are unreasonable no matter how venerable the creed or how sacred the book. Faith must always remain the groundwork of religion, but it must be a reasonable faith. And further, religion, philosophy and science are not natural enemies but allies and it is only as they coöperate and work together that they can best serve humanity. They are a trinity of complements like eye, ear and hand. They are all great and neither is independent of the other, and neither will accomplish the greatest good without the other. 

A new unrational, a new mystery must be found, for our languishing religion, in the field of human interest where, in the very nature of things, science can have nothing to say. To shift religion from the irrational sanctions of an unscientific age to ultrarational sanctions acceptable to the scientific age, or from fundamentalism to modernism without wastage or possible wreckage, is the delicate task of this generation. He who succeeds will need to call to his aid both philosophy and science. These will be his handmaids both to illustrate and to challenge. He will go about his task with an open mind. He will try all things and hold fast to the true. To this task the church is calling with a voice as loud as the apocalyptic angel, "Who is able?"

The writer does not flatter himself called to this high task. The most he can hope is not to complicate matters. The pulpit will solve its own problems in its 22

own way. It may not be amiss, however, to sound a warning. Any modern man with an opportunity, such as a minister has, to know the religious thinking of our times will often be surprised to find how thin a layer of culture is spread over the mental processes of essential barbarism even in so intellectual an age as this. In spite of this veneer of culture the average man still craves the indulgence of his primitive tastes cultivated by the practices of thousands of years of magic, miracles and spiritism of all the lower religions. Advancement is slow, painful, and each step of progress is filled with dread. It is only the more adventuresome that can cast off all moorings. It is well that this is so, for there could be no future were not the present rooted in the past. Under these circumstances the temptation of the pulpit is to reverse the motto of Phillips Brooks, "Say nothing you no longer believe in order to be helpful and keep nothing back you know is not true for fear of doing harm." The pulpit is under a strong temptation to say things it no longer believes in order to be quieting, to take the easy way, to compromise itself, to commit an act of treason against its intellect, and against progress by exploiting these primitive human wants that the intelligent pulpit has cast off and that progress demands that we leave behind. We hear but few sermons but that cater more or less to these primitive wants. The pulpit no doubt feels justified in this way of accommodation because it seems harmless and leads to apparent success, but we wonder whether in the end it is not final degradation to its clientele and a deception to itself. It only puts off the 23

day of final reckoning and readjustments. The way out is straight ahead. We must move cautiously but we must move forward. In the long run the only true policy is to give the people the highest and the best that we know, limited always of course, not by the wants of those who hear, but by their capacities and their needs. This may give one a minority to speak to but there are plenty of men who can speak to the crowds. All truth has been held by the minority at one time. If it is true that the church must not lose the masses it is even more true that it must not lose the thinking classes. There is no leakage so ruinous as the leakage of the serious top. It is imperative in our day, as in no other day, that religion, without the slightest surrender of her faith, make ungrudging and generous room for modern knowledge and ways of thinking. CHAPTER II Spiritism and the Bible 

PIRITISM is one of those recondite subjects Sh that is perplexing to the pew of modern mind that has learned to think of an orderly world. 

Of course the pew has no objections to the belief in spirits, human, angelic or demoniacal. But conscious encounters such as Luther had, conscious forgiveness of sins, conscious leadership, conscious impacts of any kind such as the pulpit seems to urge that all may have, to many is a mystical experience devoutly coveted but never realized. The pew knows the value of quietness, of meditation, it understands gladness, joy in sacrifice, the approval of a good conscience when duty is well done, the exuberance now and then of exaltation, the thrill of springtime when we seem in tune with the infinite, the melancholy of autumn days and much more that is too subtle for utterance. Especially do we know that religious faith has always been and must always remain an experience, which in all really great souls has been wrought out in solitude, rather than a belief resting upon the testimony of another: but what the pulpit seems to try to describe is more sensuous, more evidential, I shall say more objective than this. It tells us to cast ourselves on God, to let the Holy Spirit have His way with us as though He were an elder brother in the flesh, 27

whose hand we could grasp and to whom we could speak face to face.

We know that the pulpit has plenty of Scripture precedent for these exhortations, such as "the spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit that we are the children of God." We are told how Paul was hindered, led and guided by the Spirit; how officials in the Church were appointed by the Spirit and much more. We are also told how the devil often appeared to Luther in a material form and how Luther one day flung his inkstand at him. But neither these passages of Scripture nor these physical encounters of Luther disturb us. Both Luther and the writers of the Scripture lived in a world that was believed to be full of devils, that these spirits were able to materialize themselves, that they caused sickness, madness, and men often became the unwilling slaves of demons and spirits. On any theory of inspiration, but the most wooden one, we would expect that a man's beliefs would color all he had to say about anything. 

We also know now, whatever be our conception of inspiration, that the writers of the Bible did not attempt to correct the erroneous conceptions on extraneous matters held by people generally or even the erroneous notions held by the writers themselves.

Thus when David related God to the universe, he related Him to the universe that he and the people of his day knew. So David does not say, "God made the earth an oblate spheroid and sent it adrift through unlimited space chained to the sun by a mysterious force;" and if he had said that no one would have known what he meant. It was much better that 28

Spiritism and the Bible 

David would say, "God has established the earth upon the seas and founded it upon the floods." This was not only good poetry, it was good science for it accorded with the best thought of that day, which was that the earth was upheld by a sea. It is still good poetry but it could hardly be poorer science, and no poet would even think of putting it in that way now. The poet of our day would synchronize his poetry with the knowledge of our day just as David synchronized his poetry with the knowledge of his day.

What we have said applies to the spiritism of the Bible also. Many of the beliefs about spirits, beliefs that colored all the writers said, are now known to be largely superstitions and whatever of truth remains in them is now studied under other names, clairvoyance, hypnotism, neurotics, and psychology.

Something remains under spiritualism, not very seriously considered, this phase being much more seriously studied by men who have scientifically trained minds, Sir Oliver Lodge, Conan Doyle, and others who have convinced themselves that our spirits persist after the death of the body and that under some circumstances communications are possible. The majority of the world remains unconvinced that either has been demonstrated. While it would seem that the pulpit ought to be greatly interested in these demonstrations, in reality it has been indifferent, seemingly preferring to walk by faith rather than by sight. As a matter of fact the evidence is not appealing to the average mind. Most people have quite a definite conception of what their friends in heaven ought to say 29

and the way they ought to say it, and if they do not say that in the way they feel they ought to say it, they doubt the evidence and much prefer to rest on the statements of men whom the church has believed inspired than to risk a doubtful experimentation of scientists. I feel quite sure though that the pulpit will gladly accept any demonstration that can be passed on to an audience convincingly. In the meantime the pulpit needs to synchronize its spiritism with the knowledge of the times. It needs to give us a great constructive work of new categories for our own day that will make Christian theology and pulpit phraseology intelligible and satisfactory to those whose education is of the modern type.

Spiritism is really a phase of animism which, in some of its forms, has dominated man from the lowest savage to the most civilized. It has been closely related to both religion and philosophy. 

Spirits have perhaps influenced the lower levels of civilization more than the gods, for in the large, the gods have been the friends of man while spirits have been their enemies and since fear was a stronger passion than love, the gods could be neglected, while the spirits must be appeased and thwarted. 

Animism projected itself in several forms all rather closely related. 

1. Spiritism, or the belief in spectral or spiritual forms, distinguishable from the body. Objects and animals as well as men have these spiritual forms corresponding to the physical forms. The soul of man existed after the death of the body. With some it wandered through a maze of difficulties to a rest- 30

Spiritism and the Bible ing place; with some it passed to another life and thus became the soul of some other creature higher or lower depending on the life the spirit had lived. 

With still others, the spirit might enter into things, persons and animals, for weal or woe. These spirits might inspire or they might cause insanity, sickness and death. 

2. Fetishism. Here the spirit takes its abode in an animal, tree, river, spring or other things and these become objects of worship. Almost anything may become a charm or talisman. In its lowest forms literally hundreds of charms are worn by the believers in fetishes. 

3. Magic is very similar to fetishism. It is the belief in rites, ceremonies, charms and fetishes which give power over evil and over the spirits themselves. 

This is the field of the medicine man and the priest, Christian or pagan. 

4. Ancestor worship is that phase of animism so common in Japan, China, India and among the Romans and the Greeks. They worshiped the spirits of their ancestors usually supposed to be good but sometimes malign. 

5. Nature worship is a sort of refined animism which gives to every fountain, river, spring, mountain, waterfall, storm; wind, to heavenly bodies, to groves and to the forces of Nature a spirit, and all become objects of worship and veneration. 

Animism which began with the lowest primitive race is still with us in all of its manifestations. Spiritism remains in the belief of spirits of any kind and until recently in demons and haunted places. Magic 31

remains in holy water, charms, sacred relics, lucky coins, charms against warts, omens good and bad, unlucky numbers, throwing rice at weddings. Ancestor worship remains in the worship of saints, in the feeling of the presence of a sainted mother. Nature worship remains in our emotions when we see Niagara Falls, or great mountains, or stand under the stars at night; in what we feel when we talk of the spirit of the desert, and in what Emerson felt when he walked through the primeval forest with bared head. It is what we all feel on the shore of the sea, but even Tennyson must confess he could not frame into words. "Break, break, break, On thy cold gray stones, O sea! 

And I would that my tongue could utter 

The thoughts that arise in me."

"We know there is a real devil," he said, "for Jesus tells us He saw him fall like lightning from heaven." The devil was a personal rival or adversary of Jesus, and Jesus must meet him alone and match His strength with his. So the "Spirit driveth Jesus into the wilderness," where they engage in a sort of duel. The devil tried all his wiles on Jesus and like Abraham who wrestled with the angel, the devil went limping away. Many excellent lessons were wrung from the story. This is a very common view of the story and it has its values.

The objections to accepting this story as literal are first that it lacks reality and is unrepeatable. To be of the most value to us an experience must be repeatable. An unrepeatable experience has value only as an exercise of faith and is more likely to become a hindrance than a help to us. Then if the story is to be taken literally, the contestants do not seem to come to real grips. It would not be difficult for any of us to refrain from casting ourselves down from the pinnacle of the temple. Most of us would have done as Jesus is represented to have done. We would have come down the safe way. And third, this 155

method of exegesis overlooks an almost universal fact and that is, in describing spiritual experiences, we speak like Peter on Pentecost in another tongue. 

It must be so. Practically all our words are names of material things. They are concrete. When we have a new idea or a spiritual experience we do not coin a new word, we conscript a word from the material world and make it do service in the spiritual realm. We are so used to this that for the most part we immediately translate these words from the material to the spiritual without conscious effort. We never think to literalize them.

Here are a few examples from a single religious service, the most of them taken from one prayer. "Lord, we are bowed before Thee"; but we were standing. "We have seen Christ face to face today"; no, we had only studied the transfiguration. "Thou hast been a pillar of fire through all the years"; no, there was no fire. "We would wash our hearts and make them white like wool"; but we could not wash our hearts. “We are unworthy for we have laid down 

Thy cross"; no, we carried no cross. Then we sang, "Lead Kindly Light 'mid encircling Gloom"; but there was no light and no gloom for the day was clear and the sky was blue; and so on through the entire service. All the finer spiritual experiences were expressed by words and phrases drafted from the material world. The preacher was speaking in symbols. 

What then are symbols and why and when is symbolism used? Symbolism is indirect description in which we use a system of equivalents that do not call up the features of the object or expression used but suggest or call up the features of some other idea usually abstract and much more subtle and important than the symbol. Symbolism is used to describe that which is difficult to describe directly such as the person of God, the nature of God, spiritual experiences when the mind cannot form any picture of these as they really are. Primitive peoples that have not wrought out an expressive vocabulary for the spiritual and the abstract will fall more naturally into symbolism, but we all use it. An artist paints the face of Jesus. He does his best. He puts into it all the character, strength, compassion, pity, humanity and divinity he can, then he draws a halo about the head. The halo tells you that he gives it up. He could not paint all he saw. It was beyond him so he is trying to say in symbol what he could not say with his brush. You must use your imagination to finish the picture. Strange enough you never once think of what a fine halo the artist has drawn but by some subtle suggestion; the mind runs out in quest for spiritual ideals beyond his brush. When 

Paul Jones went to sea under a commission as com- 157

mander of the "Ranger," the young women tore their silk dresses into strips and made for him a flag which he hung on the mast. It is no longer a rag now or ornate drapery. It is the emblem a proud nation bears, that will not be content until it makes England consent to her independence. The flag went down with the "Richard," for Jones could not take it from the men who had given their lives for it. 

Each year this flag, the symbol of our liberties, gathers more and more meaning into its folds. He who sees only strips of silk sewed together sees little; he who sees the thing symbolized, sees much.

Now the Bible is full of symbolism. Here all the conditions meet that give birth to symbolism. We have a primitive race trying to utter itself about God and spiritual ideas with a language inadequate. 

So they say the "Arm of the Almighty," "The eye of the Lord, "The voice of God," "And the Lord spake unto Moses." All of these are symbols and the person who would think to measure the biceps of the Almighty, or the quality of His voice or insist that God spoke to Moses as a friend speaks to a friend, has missed the purpose of the writer as far as the man who sees only the beauty of the halo about the head of Christ, or sees in the flag only a silken spread pieced together from the skirts of the flappers of continental days.

In the great religious leaders this experience has been a storm that uprooted them and made a readjustment that amounted to a revolution necessary. Perhaps the most outstanding examples in recent years of remarkable religious experiences are those of Tolstoy and Mahatma Gandhi, the most potent spiritual forces of modern times.

Of the travail of soul, the struggle, the pain, the deep searching of the heart Tolstoy leaves us in no doubt for he bares his soul to the world in the most pitiless manner. His self analysis is honest and merciless. He was born a noble and was an heir to both the blessings and the curse of good fortune. He ran the whole gamut of life from a well-intentioned reformer on his estate to that of a common gambler, libertine and drunkard. Of his debauches he writes in his confessions, "I cannot think of this time without fear, repugnance and heartache. This spiritual experience and readjustment Tolstoy graphically describes in the following symbolism. "I, like a boy, was put in a boat I know not when nor where. Some were throwing away their oars in despair and some were struggling against the stream, others drifted along. The more I drifted, the more I forgot the shore which had been pointed out to me, until at last I let go the oars in utter helplessness. Suddenly in my agony I remembered the other bank and pulled back to it although the wind was against me. The shore was God, the oars were the liberty given me to find my way back to the shore and be with God." How shall we regard this story? I am keenly aware of the temptation and the danger to sane exegesis there is in allegorizing, in typology and symbol, but the abuse here is no reason for ignoring the universal tendency of symbolizing spiritual experience. In fact we hardly realize either the extent to which symbolism is used throughout the whole of the Bible from beginning to end and how important a factor symbolism is in harmonizing the ancient and the modern, the old and the new, of which the story of the temptation is but a single example.

It is difficult to put in a paragraph the social, religious and political condition of the little world into which Jesus was born. So small was it, so much out of the great currents of life that so painstaking a historian as Herodotus never mentions it. This comes as a surprise to Christians, many of whom know little of ancient history except that of the Jews. But this little, out-of-the-way nation made up in intensity what it lacked in domain. Although Rome had a genius for governing peoples she could not understand the Jews. N old college friend, a good student, a great reader of books, a clear thinker, and for a AN long time a professor in a good college, wrote me a letter a few days ago in which he asked me three questions: 

1. Is there any real historical evidence that such a man or superman as Jesus ever lived? 

2. Is there anything original in his teachings? 

3. Can any person or thing stand between a soul and God?

I think perhaps the friend's aim was more to put me on the defensive than to get information; but assuming he was serious there lurks here in these questions a strange but very common inconsistency, a paradox in evidence, a mystery of faith, that calls loudly for an explanation in this investigating age. 

It is this: the questions the friend asks proof for, namely, the historicity and originality of Jesus, are proved beyond a reasonable doubt; while the facts he takes for granted, the soul and God are most difficult of proof. Yet we think, barring a generation that was mightily influenced by the French revolution, the belief in the soul and in God have been practically universal save in a few of the most backward peoples who seem not to have arrived at the point where they can conceive of God and a few highly cultured people 219

who claim they have gone beyond God. With wonderful unanimity the rest of mankind has accepted the soul and God as truisms. In a recent questionnaire, a none too reliable method of getting information, ninety per cent of the one hundred and twentyfive thousand who answered, believed in God.

Of late years, however, we are all obsessed with a questioning spirit. We question the most sacred things and institutions and they are scrapped if found wanting. Even youngsters in their teens are beginning to ask whether God may not be a myth, a brain child of savage men in the jungles, which we have inherited from the past. Queen Silver's

While practically all great scientists believe in God, yet many of them smile now when they hear the theologian call theology "the queen of all sciences." The scientist tells us the subject matter of a science must not be assumed, but must exist and be accessible before one can build a science on it. 

Thus chemistry is built on matter which is accessible.

The Mystery of Faith statement but it is not so bad as it sounds; for, after all, the scientist does not know what gravity or electricity or mind, or matter is, yet he goes right on building his science on them. The physician has never been able to isolate the germs for many diseases. The germs causing hog cholera, for example, have never been isolated. They are not accessible nor subject to experimentation. The scientist has no empirical knowledge that they are, but each year we go on spending millions to fight the germ, and fight it successfully. The scientist tells us that great glaciers once moved slowly from north to south over Indiana and died near the Ohio River. We look in vain for these moving glaciers now, but the scientist is as sure that one passed that way as a hunter who sees the trail, that a deer crossed his path. So the theologian does not feel so abashed as might be expected when told that the speculative theologian is telling us what God is and yet does not know that He is; or that the mystic is telling us that God is but is not sure what God is. In fact God is so much more evident than gravity, electricity or germs, that now hold such large places in science, that long before these were dreamed about, God was everywhere acknowledged.

That some sciences which deal with rocks, stone walls, mountains and trees have the edge on theology, we might as well admit; but while God may be hard to corner, and while empirical experimentation with God is most difficult, the evidences of God are traceable everywhere. Though God has not yet been isolated from his works any more than some disease germs from their effects, still, one is no less 221

evident than the other. The physician prescribes his remedies on the presumption of the presence of a certain specific disease germ and it works, and because it works he is sure the germ is, not because it is accessible. Both science and theology must walk much of the way by faith.

Some of the arguments are pure reason, which the unexperienced find difficult to follow. There is the ideaistic argument that is, all physical things, like a watch or a house, are ideas that have been materialized; so there must be mind back of matter. It is even less difficult to conceive of a universe without a cause than God without a cause. The more complex, the more intricate; the more august the thing or being to be accounted for, the more need to assign an adequate cause. If, then, all the arguments yet deduced leave something yet to be desired; if human reason is so fallible that we can never be quite sure of results where empiricism is impossible, is there any other way by which this universal faith in God can be accounted for, and is there something more dependable as a basis for our belief in God than reason? There is something which, in its own restricted field, is more dependable than reason. It does not take the place of reason and experience but may be tested by them. 

This more dependable thing is native instincts, native intuitions, innate ideas, native faith. All are mysteries. All life begins with these native instincts, and is dependent on these mysterious assurances. They 226

THINK, if I were selecting a passage to prove "plenary inspiration," I should select Paul's statement, "The things that are seen are temporal, while the things that are unseen are eternal."

It is true, comparatively, Paul has been gone but a few years and his statement can not be verified by experience but all science since the days of Paul tends to prove his statement true.

First there is a seen world, a world of sense and this world of sense is passing. The flowers fade; the forests of yesterday are gone. "Man's days are like a tale that is told." Civilizations older than 

Egypt have passed away leaving but bare traces of their existence. The earth is dying, though so verdant and full of life today. Convolutions heave mountains up in the sea, while other mountains have become the floor of the deep. The tooth of time eats away the granite hills and they are no more. Change and decay are all about us. There is not a thing we sense that is eternal. "The things that are seen are temporal." Paul is right here. 

Second, there is an unseen world; a world untouched by sense; a world ethereal that is also real. 

Sir Oliver Lodge says the ether is the most important reality of the physical universe. "Life and mind are associated with matter only indirectly 242

The Seen and the Unseen through the ether," says he, and the ether is primarily man's habitation, not pseudo, solid matter," earth, houses, automobiles and money that we call the real world. True there are scientists quite as great as Lodge who doubt whether there be such a thing as ether but there is no one who doubts the reality of an unsensed world. We are even now citizens of both worlds, depending on both for our happiness; though we are unconscious of how much we are dependent on these unsensed realities. Indeed, just as we are, when in health, unconscious of our most vital organs, so we are unconscious of the existence of many of the most important realities of the world in which we live. There are realities that appeal to all our senses. We meet them on every plane of our sense life. Thus we see, feel, taste, smell, and hear water. We may see, taste, feel, and smell an orange. We hear, see, and feel a musical instrument. We feel and hear the wail of the wind. 

We may see the pale round moon. We can neither see, taste, hear, smell or feel gravity. Thus the seen world fades out and is beyond the ken of any sense, so attenuated and ethereal are some of the realities of our universe, that our senses neither test their reality or measure their value to man or their importance in the universe of entities. What an important factor gravity plays in our world! Men built their houses by it. Every time they arose, sat down or took a step they had to reckon with it and yet, for thousands of years, nobody suspected that there was such a force. There is no music in the deaf man's world; no stars stud the skies of the blind. There are 243

the violet rays of the sun and perhaps other rays that we know nothing of, that are so vital to the health of man, that are beyond any sense; they belong to the unseen world. The air is full of voices, music, speeches profound and speeches light, but we are all unaware of them until we tune in and those mysterious fingers of the radio untangle the bedlam of noises, sort out just the words we want and give them to us in gruff or soft and tender tones just as they left the speaker's tongue miles away! If one can live in this world all unconscious of gravity, electricity, the sun's healing rays, the various vitamines that mean life or death, the music and noise-laden air, who knows how much more there is in this world that we do not sense; for in reality there is but one world, we only know that part of the world that we sense. How feeble these senses are, how little of the world a sense takes in even in its own field amazes us. We have eyes to see. Yes and we thought we saw all there was to see until the telescope and the microscope were invented then we discovered there was a world under our feet and a world above our head both out of sight. Of how little use our olfactories are we may guess when we see a dog trail a fox that crossed his path yesterday!

The microphone enables us to hear sounds of the fly's feet as he goes lumbering along. And yet, poor worms of the dust, we thought we knew what was going on in God's world!

It is the Christian belief that the soul or life belongs to the unseen world. No man ever discovered life. We do not know what it is, only what it does.

The arm left behind, the crutch on the wall, the body in the grave were all parts of a movable tenant house. The feet it used on errands, the hands it used in deeds of mercy, the ears communicated music and other voices, the eyes were windows through which the soul looked out into the world, the tongue spoke words of helpfulness and cheer. These organs are all attuned to the world that we see, a world of matter. The body with its organs returned to the earth as it was and the spirit to the God who gave it, to the world out of sight.

T is difficult for moderns to realize the deep mystery and awesomeness that blood held for the ancients. Bloodshed meant the letting loose of soul power with potencies for evil most uncanny and fearsome. It is not killing but the shedding of blood, the letting loose of this soul power that constitutes the danger. Some African tribes cover up blood that had been accidentally spilled. Some stone or strangle their cattle so that no blood may be spilled. We wonder whether stoning, as the method of capital punishment among Hebrews, is not an echo of this dread to shed blood.

But the potency of blood for good was also great if properly manipulated and used. Placed on the door lintels it guarded the home from harm. It was powerful stimulant and the old drank the blood of the young to give them back their youthful vigor. 

It was the food of witches who were believed to live off of the blood of children.

It was craved by Vampires, a kind of ghost which during the night leaves the grave and maintains a semblance of life by sucking the blood of men and women. It is still the custom of some of the backward peoples of Europe to drive a nail through the head of the corpse into the coffin to keep the dead from wandering. The earth demons were appeased by blood and the Arabs offer blood to these earth 253

demons by pouring blood upon the ground even today. 

The dead in Hades crave that renewing of life that blood can give. Odysseus tells us that when he went to "The land of the dead" that he took his sharp knife and dug a pit about a cubit either way, and around the edge he poured an offering to the dead. Then he took of the choicest of his sheep, cut their throats over the pit, filling it with blood. "In crowds about this pit the dead flocked from every side with awful wail." It was only by his sharp sword that Odysseus was able to fight back the famishing dead. His mother came among the rest. She did not know him but when he let her eat the blood she remembered the past and foretold the future.
