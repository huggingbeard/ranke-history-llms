How to Bid Contract Bridge CHAPTER I Differences Between Contract and Auction Bridge 

At Contract the play of the hands is identical with that at Auction, though it is simplified by the information gained from the additional rounds of bidding, but even expert Auction players must add materially to their knowledge of bidding, if they want the best results.

The Opening Bids of One and the Overcalls are practically the same at Contract as at 

Auction, but the Contract player must realize that to make game he must contract for it, that to gain the slam bonus, the slam must be bid, and, therefore, that if there be the possibility of game or slam, the full and exact value of a hand must be shown to the partner, and the bidding kept open until the best eventual bid for the two hands has been found. 

Lacking probability of game or slam, an advanced score is always of value, but, if there be no game in sight, the minimum should be bid, as over-tricks in the honor column count more than tricks bid and scored toward game.

The correct estimating of a hand's value, and the giving and receiving between partners of accurate information as to the location of top cards and the distribution of suits, is of greater importance in Contract than in Auction, where a bid of one may give a slam, or an overbid result in only a small penalty. 

The penalties at Contract are very great, especially when "Vulnerable" (one game in). For this reason weak denial bids can be made only with extraordinary distribution, as the partner relying on each bid for certain tricks played at any suit, may contract for game or slam on the next round.

The success or failure of the eventual contract depends upon whether or not the best bid for the two partners' hands has been found.

In Contract every bid made must be a constructive one in the effort toward game or slam and should give the partner as accurate information as possible as to the location of top cards and the division of suits. 

A bid showing one's own suit or the distribution of one's cards does not necessarily mean a denial of partner's suit. 2

DIFFERENCES-CONTRACT AND AUCTION 

In this showing of one's own suits and the distribution thereof rather than in always supporting partner's bid, in the practical elimination of weak rescue bids, and in the use of Command Bids, lie the chief differences between 

Contract and Auction bidding.

CHAPTER VI The Overcall or Defensive Bid 

Any bid made after a bid by an opponent is called an Overcall or Defensive Bid. An Over call may be made on a much weaker hand than an Original Bid-a fact which the partner must bear in mind.

After an Original Bid the opponent on the left of bidder may overcall holding at least 1½ to 2

Honor Tricks. As a rule it is inadvisable to overcall with Two on a four card suit, or to overcall with a No Trump bid without a double stopper in suit bid by opponent.

It requires a stronger hand to overcall an 

Original No Trumper than a suit bid. 

Do not bid over an Original Bid of Two except with great strength.

Sitting on the right of the opening bidder, the two intervening players having passed, it is inadvisable to bid unless having probability of game in own hand, or to save game when the opponents are "on score". If the bidding is kept open in this position without a strong hand, the original bidder can re-bid, perhaps 18

The Negative or Informatory Double 

There are several conventions for the Negative Double, the one most generally in use being that any double of any bid of One or Two of a Suit, of One No Trump, or of an Original Suit Bid of 

Three, unless the partner of doubler has bid or doubled, is a Negative Double and demands a response from partner.

Many seasoned players feel that a lack of the suit bid by opponent is sufficient justification for a Negative Double. In Contract even more than in Auction, for a Negative Double there must be top cards (22 to 3 Honor Tricks) as well as a distribution that will either support the take-out imposed on partner or justify a bid by doubling hand in case of a re-double. 

If there be no intervening bid, the partner of 

Negative Doubler must bid his best suit, a major always being preferable to a minor if holding at least four. With opponent's suit stopped twice, bid No Trumps. A Jump bid in this position shows a very strong hand. 

Over a Negative Double, the partner of the 

Original Bidder may pass, deny his partner's 39

suit with Overcall values and a good suit of his own, or with four Trumps give limit raise of his partner's suit. Or he may re-double. 

In this position a re-double means general strength rather than support for the partner's suit.

Over a re-double the partner of doubler should not bid without some high cards. or unbalanced distribution of suits. Any bid made in this position is a "free bid." With a weak, evenly balanced hand, pass, permitting the doubler to rescue himself.

The Penalty or Business Double 

Any double made after the partner of doubler has bid or doubled or any double of more than Two in a suit (except an Original Three Bid) or of 

Two or more No Trumps, is a Penalty Double made with the hope of setting the opponents. 

In making a Penalty Double the player must estimate the number of taking tricks in his own hand, one of which should be in suit doubled, plus those tricks to be inferred from partner's bidding. He must also take into account the probable distribution of suits, lest his honors be trumped by opponents. 

Do not double unless there is the probability of setting the opponents at least two. 40

CHAPTER XII Slams 

Contract offers huge rewards to the successful Slam bidder, a fact offset by the big penalties which are the price paid for overbidding.

In contracting for slams there is involved much card judgment and still more luck. 

Comparatively few hands are "lay-down" slams. If all the finesses "hold-up", and certain suits "break well," a slam may be made where it should not have been bid. On the other hand a bad distribution against the bidder may cost a loss of tricks when there was every justification for a slam bid.

In bidding for slams the player must try to visualize the partner's hand, to judge from his bidding what top cards partner must hold, and then make allowance for all possible losers.

Slams are a matter of Aces and Kings. Gen erally unless the opener has bid Two originally there is no slam in the hands without a holding of at least 4 Honor Tricks by partner.

CHAPTER XIII When Vulnerable 

The values required for Original Bids should be slightly greater when the player bidding is vulnerable, and raises and re-bids must be safe and sound when there is one game in. 

The first game is worth a penalty of 400 points, that is, when neither side is vulnerable, if certain that opponents can make that game, an overbid of three tricks is permissible. 

When the Contract player is vulnerable, he enjoys increased bonuses for slams and for overtricks if doubled and successful in making his contract, but he is also subject to increased penalties. 

The rubber game is worth 600 points. Even to save the rubber it is unwise to overbid more than two tricks when vulnerable. A set of a thousand will not be balanced by the bonus for rubber, even if rubber be won eventually.

Perhaps the one exception is when opponents have bid and seem certain of making a slam. 

Under these circumstances a thousand point set might prove worth while. 

Fourteen and eighteen hundred sets are very, very rarely justifiable. 

Do your overbidding on your first game, but when you are vulnerable watch your step. 45

CHAPTER XIV Advanced Score 

An Advanced Score is very valuable in Contract, a fact not always recognized by novices. 

When on score", a contract for one or two means game, while the opponents may be driven to overbid in their effort to save that game. 

At an Advanced Score, Original Bids of One or Two are the same as when there is no score. 

Remember that any bid for more points than necessary for game will be taken as a slam signal by partner, so when "on score" eliminate 

Original Three Bids unless three are needed for game, or a "shut-out" bid seems imperative.

Watch the Score, and do not bid more than is required for game unless there is hope of making a slam. 46

CHAPTER XV 

Goulashes

In Goulashes lies the greatest gambling element of Contract. So popular have they become that an explanation of their peculiarities demands admittance to any book on Contract bidding.

After all four hands have been passed, no one having bid, a Goulash may be played. Any one of the players has the privilege of objecting, in which case the deal oautmatically passes. 

If a Goulash is agreed upon, the same dealer re-deals. Each player arranges his cards in sequence and in suits. The dealer puts his hand face down on the table, and the other players, beginning with the one on his right, place their cards in turn on his. Without shuffling, the deck is then cut once, twice, or three times at the discretion of the dealer. Five cards at a time are dealt to each player in turn for two rounds, and then three cards to each. 

Almost invariably the resulting hands have an extraordinary distribution, those consisting of three or two suits being the rule rather than the exception. Six and seven card suits, so impressive in an ordinary deal, are not at all 47

remarkable, and every missing card of an apparently big suit may be held by one of the opponents.

Only when absolutely certain of the required number of tricks in own hand should opening bids of Three or more be made.

Unless holding a solid suit of eight cards, the 

Approach Bid, that is the Opening Bid of One in a Suit, is usually best in a Goulash. 

Side Aces and Kings have not the same value as in ordinary deals, as they are very apt to be ruffed. Void suits, singletons and doubletons are very common in Goulashes.

Five card suits should not be bid with less than three to four Honor Tricks in hand.

A No Trump is seldom a good eventual bid in a Goulash. It is hard to set up suits, and difficult to get back and forth from one hand to another.

If partners cannot agree on a suit early in the bidding it is wise to lay back. Eventually one may be able to double one of the opponents and his partner, the other. 

Of course with a solid suit a game or slam can often be bid, but a famous hand will serve to illustrate how freakish a Goulash can be. 

Holding eleven Spades from the Ace to the 48

Replete with such tragedies are Goulashes! 

In Goulashes there is one Command Bid that does not exist in other deals. Any bid of One

No Trump demands a take-out by partner. 

One No Trump may be bid on a hand containing three suits only, because of the difficulty of showing all of them, and in the hope that the partner's forced response will be one of the three suits held by the No Trump bidder. 

Another convention peculiar to the Goulash is, that holding an Ace-King, the Ace is led instead of the King. This is to guide the partner should he be devoid of that suit and uncertain whether or not to ruff.

One's own suit should be led rather than the partner's, if partner has denied that suit. When in a quandary, lead a long suit which has not been mentioned, in the hope of a ruff by partner.

CHAPTER XVI 

Passing Goulashes 

A player with a quick eye and a retentive memory is often able to place certain cards in a Goulash because of his knowledge of the arrangement of the cards in his hand of the deal just passed.

On account of this the Passing Goulash has become the custom in many clubs. 

While there is some element of luck, there is also science involved in the passing. 

Probably the most popular of the Passing 

Goulashes is the One-Three-Two pass.

After the Goulash deal has been completed and before the bidding starts, one card is exchanged between partners, then three cards, and finally two.

The first card passed must be the top card of the longest and strongest suit, a major taking precedence over a minor.

On the next pass of three cards, the card which partner has passed should be returned. with another of that suit if possible. If on the first pass the cards exchanged have been in the same suit, the partner receiving the higher card 51

returns it and the hand that originally held that higher card collects that suit. Should there be any possibility of building up one's own hand, on the second pass there should be returned not only the partner's suit but also a losing card or cards in a short side suit so partner may know what suit not to give on the third round of passing. Convey this information as early as possible to the end that the side suits may be massed for defensive purposes.

On the third pass of two cards continue to build up the partner's suit, finish clearing own hand of losers, or pass a small card of some suit headed by an Ace as a card of entry.

Should partner have passed an Ace originally, often it is well to keep one small card of his suit in order to get into his hand in the play unless sure of some other means of entry. 

After two passes a player should have gained a fair idea of what the partner wants or does not want and can be guided on the last pass by this information.

Three passes should give each partner a general idea of the distribution of the other hand's cards, and sufficient knowledge of the high card holding to enable him to judge whether the two hands will warrant a game or slam bid, or play better on the defensive. 52

Another form of Passing Goulash now very popular is the exchange of four cards between partners.

In this passing it is important to show partner any Ace held, and at the same time give a card of entry into one's own hand. To show an Ace pass any singleton of that suit between a six and a ten inclusive. 

With a big hand try to clear it of losers. With a worthless holding try to improve partner's hand. Holding only three or four cards in a major suit pass them in the hope of filling out that suit for partner.

CHAPTER XVII General Rules 

Look at the Score before bidding.

An Original Two Bid shows a very big hand with Slam possibilities.

A Jump over call is always a plea to partner to use his imagination and re-bid his hand. 

Be sure to keep the bidding open in response to a Command Bid by your partner.

A bid for more than is required for game is a 

Slam Signal.

Weak "rescue bids" are almost always losing ones. 

Overbidding when vulnerable is a costly practice.

A certain game is preferable to a possible slam. 56

DEFINITIONS OF TERMS ORIGINAL BID-The opening or initial bid. 

SECONDARY BID-Any bid made by a player who has either bid another suit or passed on a previous round of bidding.

OVERCALL OR Defensive Bid-Any bid made after a bid by an opponent. 

RESPONSE Any bid made after a bid by partner.

ASSISTING BID-A raise of the suit bid by partner.

MINIMUM RAISE-A raise of one in the suit bid by partner.

DOUBLE RAISE-A raise of two in the suit bid by partner.

DENIAL BID-A bid of another suit than the one bid by partner, when made to show weakness in partner's suit.

JUMP BID-A bid of more than is necessary to cover last previous bid made. 

COMMAND BID-Any Jump Response to a partner's bid, with a change of suit. An Original 

Bid of Two is also a Command Bid if previously so arranged between partners. 57

FREE BID-Any bid that is not a forced response to partner's Command Bid or Negative Double.

SLAM SIGNAL-Any notification during the bidding that there may be a Slam in the two hands. Among the Slam Signals are the Original 

Two Bid, any bid of more than is necessary for game whether there is an advanced score or not, and any Command Response. 1

LAWS OF PASSING GOULASHES CONSENT OF PLAYERS 

1. The consent of the four players is required in order to play a Passing Goulash. Such consent once given is binding for the duration of the rubber. 

PICKING UP OPPONENT'S PASSED CARDS* 

2. (a) If both sides are offenders, the deal is void and the next dealer deals in the regular way with the still pack. (b) If only one side is the offender the opponents have the option, after consultation, of declaring the deal void or letting it stand. 

In the latter case the passed card or cards picked up in error must be restored to the proper player, and the Passing Goulash shall continue in regular order. may

By consultation is meant that either opponent decide that the deal is void or shall stand, or may say: "Partner, will you decide or shall I?" or words to that effect; in the latter event the partner must decide or pass the decision back to the player submitting it. No further exchange of information is permitted.

BIDDING BEFORE THE PASSING IS COMPLETED 

3. A bid by dealer before his partner has completed the passing of cards is a bid out of turn subject to the penalty for that offense. 

However, a bid by dealer after his partner has completed the passing, but before an opponent has finished, is subject to no penalty. 61

LAWS OF PASSING GOULASHES WRONG NUMBER OF CARDS 

4. (a) If a player passes more cards than permitted and his partner picks up any of them, the opponents after consultation may declare the deal void or let it stand. In the latter case the cards incorrectly passed must be returned to the passer, who shall correct the error and the Passing Goulash shall continue in regular order. (b) If at any time after the passing of cards. has been completed a player holds too many cards and his partner too few, the opponents have the option, after consultation, of declaring the deal void or letting it stand.

If the deal stand, play continues until the player who is short has played all his cards; his partner, who is long, then passes to him the one or more cards needed to enable him to play to the remaining trick or tricks. It is not a revoke if a player who is short refuses when without a card of the suit led and consequently, in accordance with the provisions of this law, receives from his partner a card of the suit he has refused. (c) If a player on each side has an incorrect number of cards the deal is void and the next dealer deals in the regular way, with the still pack. 

FACED CARDS 

5. If during the passing one or more cards be exposed, such card or cards may be passed again, but shall remain faced during the passing and play and may be treated as exposed during the play. *A card (or cards) exchanged between partners is called a passed card (or cards).
