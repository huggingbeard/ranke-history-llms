BEING GUIDED 

It is assumed then that you have had a home of some kind, and that you have had some direction, whether or not you noticed it. It is only when you stop to think it over that you realize how much your parents did for you in your home, and in how many ways they were directing your doings. They led you in your times of eating, sleeping, working, studying, playing, visiting. They planned for your education in the public or private school. They selected the clothes you wore, influenced you in the selection of companions and playmates, placed about you things which gave you interest in games or music or art or science or business or study or what not. Even when you visited friends or relatives you were under the care of your parents, and often they were with you. They of necessity assumed all of this planning and doing for you when you were a helpless infant, but they still continued a large part of it through all of the years of childhood and youth. You were insensible of the fact that you had asked advice from them hundreds of times, and sometimes took the notion that they did not know as well as you did yourself what you should do or what you should have. You may have thought them "old fogies" sometimes when they restrained you a little in some activity or other.

A NEW LEADER 

Now all is changed. True your parents may still be supporting you and in their writing may give you more or less of advice. They may even think they are directing you as before, but that is clearly impossible since they cannot be at your side all the time as they were when you were at home. They may Page six

regret, too, that they cannot give you minute instructions any more, but it is the natural course of events that you should sooner or later assume responsibility for yourself, and without this assumption it is impossible for you to have full development.

The power to direct and to discharge responsibility cannot be attained all at once. There must be a training time and a developing time. This time of assuming responsibility comes to you in an intensive form when you leave home. If you have been well trained you have had this and that responsibility put upon you when you were at home-you were gradually given more and more opportunities to make decisions for yourself. You have had to buy yourself a hat, or work on a committee, or plan a social event, or care for younger brother or sister, or devise a plan of doing some work-anything to develop a little the powers within you. Now, all of a sudden, you are put in the place of directing nearly all of your own activities. Of course your times of eating, your times of attending class exercises or your hours of business and possibly a few other things are decided upon by others, but all the rest of the things you do, and the character you manifest, and the kind of person you are to become as you develop, all depend upon you. 

You are the sole arbiter of your physical, mental, social, moral and religious life. The experiences of your earlier life, if they were good, will be useful to you in this crisis, but they cannot save you unless you are willing to help. The people about you will help make or mar your life, but you are the determining factor. It is possible for you to permit all these other influences to decisively affect your life for good or evil, but you can also rise above all these and make the decisions for yourself.

YOU MUST TAKE CHARGE 

But you may say that you do not care to assume this responsibility. Here is the crux of the whole matter. You are under the necessity of assuming it. You will take charge of Page seven

yourself whether or not you want to do so you cannot get out of it. Of course you might go back home and stay there as a child and be as a child while your parents lived, but the only way to escape would be to die before they did. You may push off part of the burden upon others and follow the direction of some companion, but that does not take away the responsibility, nor does it relieve you of the charge; you are only making a poor boss and spoiling the job, for no friend can take the place with any degree of success, and, at best, his charge must be temporary.

MANY THINGS TO DIRECT 

It remains then that you must take charge of this person you call yourself. You must direct him in the minutest details.

You must tell this person when to get up and when to go to bed; when and how to bathe or comb his hair or clean his teeth; how to dress and how to care for his clothes. All this is for you. You must decide on such things as correcting your gait, if it is clumsy or shuffling; modulating your voice, if it is harsh or grating; improving your manners, if they are awkward or bashful; finding proper methods of study; acquiring the ability to dress neatly; making good use of your time; choosing your friends; directing your moral and religious life; selecting your language; caring for your money; choosing recreations and amusements; developing traits of character; improving your habits-all these and many more. It is surprising how many things are to be taken over by you when you leave home. Mind you, it is not a question of wanting to take them over, you are under the imperative necessity of taking them over and assuming their direction; you cannot escape it.

Here you are, then, in charge of this person you have been living with all these years, but who all that time had the constant help of some other person or persons, but who is now wholly thrown upon your resources. It is true that you may have many opportunities to get advice and help from others Page eight

but you must be the arbiter. You must choose between good and bad advice.

We will have just a little talk about how some of these things may be done in such a way that the effort may prove worth while. Through it all it is assumed that you have at least a little desire to make some sort of a success in life, and that you are willing to take under consideration any suggestions which might help you to attain success.

Physical Life A PLAN NEEDED 

ERE you are in charge of a body. What are you going to

HE do with it? You can just let it drift along without attention and it will stand up fairly well, even for some years, if it has been handed over to you in good shape. Of course you know it must be fed with some regularity, and allowed to rest or have sleep with a less degree of regularity (at least, many seem to think that there is less need of regularity in sleeping), and it might be well to give it some exercise now and again. 

If you start out with a plan of this kind and persist in it, you will have a failing body before the age of forty-five is reached. 

The body must be fed with suitable food and at regular intervals. It must have quite definite amounts of rest and at regular times. It must have suitable exercise, and this, too, must be given with regularity. It is just as harmful to stay awake all of one night and then try to catch up in sleep the next night as it would be to eat no food today and then eat enough for two days tomorrow. It is just as harmful to take no exercise this week and then over-exercise next week as it is to eat in the same way or to sleep in the same manner. 

NATURE MUSICAL 

This physical frame of ours is worth while. It has certain well-defined wants and these may be met in a very definite and accurate way, and the body will respond to such care in a surprising fashion. A tuneful rhythm given to the activities of the body brings a wonderful harmony in its development, and, when maturity is reached, gives it glorious strength vibrating with magnetic power. The soldiers of the camps, and athletic teams in civilian life are illustrations of how to Page ten

care for the body and make it strong and vigorous. The soldier follows a clock-like schedule. He is careful about the food he eats, he exercises every part of his body, he sleeps like a childhe is planning to be fit in every particular as far as his body is concerned. The good athlete avoids late hours, sweetmeats, drinks, tobacco and all other injurious things. He eats selected food, schedules his time, and regularly exercises all of his muscles even those not used in his particular game or event. 

All of this requires thought and some study on the part of the one in care of the body.

RETARDERS 

The above remarks are mostly on the positive side. They tell us to give the body certain definite treatment and it will grow and thrive and develop into a strong and vigorous body, and one which will give a fine dwelling place for a good mind. 

There is, unfortunately, another side to the subject. It is so easy and so common for young people to develop some little habit that will hold back this development of a strong body just a little and prevent the securing of the results which might otherwise be obtained. it may have

A stalk of corn may be cultivated in season, rain and sunshine just when needed, the fertile soil may supply all the substances needed for its growth; but a very small worm is able to seriously mar the product. A very small handicap may decidedly hinder the full development of an otherwise fine specimen of humanity. I am not referring to such gross vices as the use of intoxicants or opiates, or the sexual vices. If you have enough sense to be sent to college or to be given a job you must know that taking part in these vices entirely unfits men for life's duties. Every college and university should make a careful examination of those presenting themselves for entrance and refuse entrance to any who have been guilty of the vices just named. This is done by many business organizations. Such young people are not of the caliber to place in the classrooms or business offices with real young men and women. In giving such persons further oppor- Page eleven

tunity for mental development they should be placed in institutions provided for this purpose, and away from physically and mentally healthy people. They should be separated just as we separate or quarantine those who have contagious diseases. 

MILDER HANDICAPS 

But there are other habits which are only mild in their retarding action. They act as handicaps, just as though a racer should put a beautiful, heavy, golden anklet on his foot as he enters the race because it is a costly and beautiful ornament and one which gives him great pleasure as he looks at it. 

Who would expect such a racer to win the race? So, in the race of life why should you put on a habit which in itself may not be particularly wrong from a moral standpoint but which handicaps in the race you are running. An example is the use of tobacco. Let us suppose that the use of the weed is allowable from the moral standpoint, and even the physical standpoint. 

Its use gives one an odor which is objectionable to some people and therefore limits the usefulness of the individual while in contact with those people. Its use makes one less careful of the niceties of good manners, leads to dirty rooms and careless habits, and in older age makes one a great charge to those who have to care for him. Select an otherwise gentlemanly man and watch him carefully as he smokes in a public place. This will make quite apparent the unfortunate little details. But tobacco is certainly not helpful in the securing of a strong body, and many good physicians think it is really harmful. Why should you take any risk with this body of yours by taking up the use of something which is at best possibly a little harmful to the body? You are trying to find out the things which are helpful, not those which may possibly be harmful. There is no authority anywhere who claims that the use of cigarettes is helpful, and but few, if any, who claim that tobacco in any form is helpful in any way. "Can a man be a Christian and use tobacco?" someone asked the evangelist 

Williams. "To be sure he can, but he will be a dirty one," was the reply.

DOPES 

Another harmful modern custom is the overuse of soft drinks and, so-called, patent medicines. Many of these are dopes and are positively harmful to the body. No such drink or medicine should be used unless the materials contained in them are known. It is very strange that we take into the body with so little thought things which we would not feed to a horse or a dog without knowing more about them. The young person of sensible mind will demand that the labels on remedies show fully the contents of the same. In the same manner the contents of soft drinks must be fully known or thence avoided, no matter if their sale is backed by so-called religious people.

EATING 

Possibly you have thought that there is nothing for you to manage along the line of eating except to manage to get enough to eat. It is worth your while to study your needs, the kind of food best supplying these needs, the amount of food which should be taken, how much water to drink and when to drink it. Many a young man is unfitted for study by overeating or by using a one-sided diet; while others are not in first-class physical condition because they do not drink enough water, or do not breathe properly. These things cannot be learned in a few minutes, but they are worth time and study. 

Some years ago a lawyer of note who seemed to have a strong body and excellent health said to the writer, "I would not know that I had a stomach except for the fact that I have read about it in the physiologies." He had no thought about his eating and no thought about exercising. He died a few years later from stomach troubles. When they were discovered he lacked the vitality to overcome them because, for all his years, he had taken no thought about his eating and his exercising. 

TAKE A BATH 

"Cleanliness is next to godliness." Being clean does not consist alone in avoiding dirty habits such as the use of to- Page thirteen

bacco or spitting on the sidewalks. You must bathe regularly and often. "Saturday night-tub-night" is by no means a clean schedule. Use soap rather than perfumes. The clothes should also be clean-get washing done. Care for your teeth and your hair and your fingernails. Brush your clothes and keep them neat. Shine your shoes. Make yourself feel like a man and hold your head up like a real man. Sit up, breathe deeply and fully. All this gives you courage and enthusiasm, as well as good health.

EXERCISING 

The majority of young men and women commit a most serious crime against their bodies in that they do not take proper exercise. The American method of the crowd taking no exercise, but sitting on the bleachers watching a few take too much exercise might be radically improved. All normal persons must take regular exercise, just as they take food regularly. You cannot expect to use your body many years if you do not pay heed to this. It may serve you awhile, but it will only serve you poorly, and, after a time, will keep you busy trying to find out what is wrong with it. Whatever else you leave undone do not fall into the habit of neglecting proper exercise. Study how to exercise to the best advantage; and, if conditions are not favorable, change the conditions until you find those which are favorable to good exercise. You cannot be a good student or give good service without regular systematic exercise.

GET EXAMINED 

A young man came to college with what appeared to be a strong, healthy body. He entered the athletic teams; made the 'varsity football team and the basketball team. He was clean of habit, keen of mind—a typical first-class man. The war came on. He was examined and refused entrance as a soldier because of heart trouble. He knew absolutely nothing of this until the time of the examination, although it had been with him for some time. He went on with his work, but soon found his heart giving him trouble. He was laid aside from work for a 

Page fourteen

time and there was some question about his recovery, but with the guidance of skilful physicians, and because of his former care of his body he recovered and is back at work. I recite this to suggest to you that you should have a most careful and thorough examination made of your physical condition, about this time of your life. You have just emerged from the period of rapid growth. Some organ may have gone wrong. You ought to know the condition of every part of the body. It is a time to take stock. It would be risky to start up in an airplane without knowing the condition of the engine. It is risky to start out in life without knowing the condition of your life engine the body. If the airplane engine is defective it may be replaced by another engine. If an organ of your body is defective it must be repaired-you cannot replace it with a new one. 

Hence the more necessity of knowing at once of any organ needing attention. Have a capable physician make a most thorough examination of the condition of the organs of your body.

II 

OF The Mental Life MIND LIKE BODY 

F COURSE you know that eating, sleeping and exercising must all come about in a systematic way and at regular intervals. Did you ever think that our mental machinery is very much like our physical machinery? The mind is capable of being trained; it is capable of development and growth; it may be made stronger or weaker-all depending upon how it is handled. We think of education as primarily a development or training of the mind. Your time schedule of the activities of life should therefore take the mental side into consideration. You cannot stuff and overfeed the mind for a time, and then starve it for a time in an irregular fashion and expect it to be healthy. It requires periods of activity and periods of rest, and these should come with great regularity. 

The mind will grow stronger as it is systematically given good mental food at studied intervals, and, at the same time, has regular periods of relaxation and recuperation. 

COMPOSURE OF MIND 

It is very essential also that the surroundings be the best while the mind is under activity. To exercise the body vigorously on a hot day, with coarse, heavy woolen clothes irritating the body all the while would scarcely be considered helpful exercise. Likewise it would not be helpful exercise of the mind if it should be irritated and annoyed while it is being exercised. 

Good mental exercise is brought about when the mind is ready to be fully devoted to the matter in hand and is not annoyed by any disturbing factors. In coming to a period of study, for example, one should be composed and free from disturbing thoughts about something which may have been Page sixteen

annoying, or about something which is giving extreme pleasure or joyful anticipation. If something of a disturbing nature is on the mind-and everyone will sometimes have such annoyances-it is best to first get rid of the disturbing matter and then come to the study. It may take time to do this. It may take a lot of thinking and reasoning, and possibly consultation with others; but first get this thing settled, if it is possible to settle it. The time so used will not be wasted time, it will only be time gained, for the mind when freed will give full returns. Just as it is not well to take a bath when overheated, or to eat a large meal immediately after strenuous exertion of the body, so it is not well to attempt a new mental task without composure of mind. 

This suggests an excellent method of beginning the study of a difficult lesson or the attacking of a difficult problem. It is a good practice to sit down and read a few minutes in a paper or magazine, just to get the mind composed and at ease, if you will, then the difficult task will be undertaken with full concentration of the mind. This same method is of value when the mind is distracted by pleasures. 

MIND AND BODY WORK TOGETHER 

Happily the physical and mental natures supplement each other. In nothing may this be better illustrated than in the taking of physical exercise. Physical exercise is necessary for the man who is engaged in mental pursuits, but that exercise which will be best for him will be an exercise which entirely frees the mind from all thought of mental cares. Any exercise which one takes when alone will ordinarily not do this, and men wonder why they do not get much out of gardening, or swinging Indian clubs or dumbbells, or individual gymnasium exercises. In all of these they are getting only one part of the real end of exercise to such persons; the other part is just as important as the physical. In fact the two must go together or there is but little return from either. The store-box loafer rests his mind in a way, but the sum of the physical and mental gain is very small. Anyone who has tried gardening 

Page seventeen

for exercise knows that he has solved some of his most difficult problems while at this work. Real exercise must be in the company of others; and the exercise must be of such a character as to take the thoughts away from other cares. In this way the mental being is at rest while the physical is at work, the one being the complement of the other. 

MENTAL SCHEDULES 

The character of the mind then calls for a scheduling of your time for the day. A lesson studied at the same time each day will soon give one interest in, and love for the subject, even though at first it may have been a distasteful subject. A subject so studied soon ceases to be as difficult as it seemed at first. After eating at a certain hour each day for a time, we only get hungry when that hour arrives; or if we go to sleep at a certain hour each night for some weeks we get sleepy when that hour arrives. In exactly the same fashion we acquire mental habits. We eat better when we are hungry and we sleep better when we are sleepy; in like manner we are better able to study a subject when the regular period for that study arrives than we are at some other period. The necessity of making out a time schedule for each day of the week cannot be over-emphasized. The simple fact is that it works, while an irregular, haphazard use of time begets poor work and lack of interest in the work.

You must make some attempt to schedule your time. It is not necessary that you work out the schedule to the minute and write it down on paper, although it is a very good plan to write it down at first and follow it as closely as possible. 

After awhile it will not be necessary to write it down, any more than it is necessary to write down the eating hours or the sleeping hours. When once the scheduled habits are established it will not interfere with them to change the hours a little any more than it will to eat a little early or a little late now and again. The main point is that continuous irregular eating habits or sleeping habits will soon play havoc with the physi- 

Page eighteen

cal health; and in the same way irregular mental habits will soon play havoc with mental health and vigor.

SLEEPY STUDIES 

The way will not always be smooth; difficulties will arise even when one is conscientiously trying to carefully follow out good plans. You will come to a study period and soon find yourself sleepy-you just cannot keep awake and at the work. Do not make the mistake of trying to continue at the work because it is in your schedule that this is the time for this study. Lay aside your work and take a nap; or thence take some exercise out in the open. Nature is telling you that you are not in proper condition to study just then. Get in proper condition and the work will be done and no mental effort will be needed to keep awake. The experience may suggest that your schedule needs revision; or it may only tell you that you were out late the night before and needed some more sleep; or it may tell you that you have been failing to get the proper amount of exercise at regular intervals; or it may tell you that your eyesight needs attention. Both the body and the mind keep you informed as to their condition, if only you will familiarize yourself with their indications and take heed when these indications are given. 

FROM PHYSICAL TO MENTAL WORK 

Another difficulty meets the student who comes from the farm or other physical work in the open. The change of habits in coming to close study without much physical exertion will cause trouble and sometimes discouragement. It will take some days or weeks to get the body and mind adjusted to these new conditions. Do not get discouraged while this process is in progress. Help it along for a time by taking an unusual amount of exercise in the open air. Adjustments like these must be made in some similar fashion for one who makes any decided changes in habits. This condition does not ordinarily arise with those who have just come out of the high school, Page nineteen

but the condition may be produced to a degree by the work of the summer vacation.

PRIMARY EDUCATION 

Before leaving the mental life it might be well to suggest a few practical things along the line of mental growth and development. Many of us are too prone to forget that our primary education was undertaken for a good purpose. Perchance you have not been proficient in every part of this earlier education in the public schools and the high school. For example, you may be a poor speller. It is not too late to learn to spell. If you realize that you are a poor speller, take a little time and just study spelling. Then watch your spelling as you are in other work. A little thought given to it all the while and you will soon remedy the defect. I have seen very capable candidates for positions thrown out of the competition simply because of two or more misspelled words in the letters of application.

Get a dictionary if you do not already possess one. Put it where it may be consulted with great ease. Consult it every day. Develop a larger vocabulary and use it. In your reading on any subject never pass by a word of which you do not know the meaning. Go to the dictionary with it. It is not wasted time to do this-it is time gained. 

Review other subjects of your earlier education. Fifty per cent of the students coming to my classes in Chemistry do not have an adequate conception of what ratio and proportion in arithmetic mean. These are most important subjects, not only in scientific study, but in ordinary practical life. Other subjects studied in the grades have little or very indefinite meaning to many students. Take a look over your text-books used in the grades and in high school. You already know the import of most of the subjects, but some should be reviewed from the standpoint of a more mature mind than you had when you were in the grades or even in the high school. This is a most interesting exercise and you will enjoy it, but best of all, it is worth while.

SIDE LINES 

It is good to have some side line which will get some of your thoughts now and again. Taking photographs, playing a musical instrument, singing, working with electrical devices, reading or writing poetry, collecting insects or flowers, and many other things may well be used in this way. This should not interfere with regular work, but it gives zest to the mind and skill to the student. These pastimes, as they are sometimes incorrectly named, must be kept wholly in hand and within proper bounds. It is easy to get so interested in them that regular work is neglected. By the way, this suggests a good method of preventing childishness in old age. As the years go by, one should take up a new subject about every fifth year, or at least one every tenth year. Learn that subject from the beginning, study about it and develop it in all of its practical bearings. After a number of years of search I have failed to find anyone childish at ninety years of age who has followed this suggestion. It is a recipe to prevent mental old age. 

GAMES 

Games which develop the mind are not wholly amusement. 

Chess and checkers, for example, require mental skill. To be a good chess player one must be able to keep several things in mind at one and the same time. He must be able to reason out a course of procedure and its results. This is all very good mental drill.

One time I had difficulty in catching a rat. Different devices were tried but always there was failure. Finally it occurred to me to set two traps side by side-the rat was caught the first night. The rat could keep one trap in mind but was not equal to watching and avoiding two traps at the same time. A chess player must have the ability to keep several things in mind at the same time. The game develops this power. Such games are worth while. What shall we say of games which are purely games of chance? They have no mental value and therefore easily degenerate into gambling devices. The very fact that they do not require any brains makes them the tools of gamblers.

TRAIN THE MEMORY 

The memory is a part of our mental make-up which may be trained. We seemingly often forget this. One person remembers birthdays of friends, another remembers names and faces, another dates of important events, another lists of facts. We get the notion that this just "comes natural" to these individuals. I once heard a county superintendent of schools call the roll of two hundred teachers from the 120 districts of his county, with only a blank tablet before him on which he wrote the names of the absentees. Quite a number of these teachers were newly employed in the schools within a few weeks before the time of the institute at which he called the roll. How did he do it? His memory was easily trained along such lines, but he explained that he could not do this without effort and training. Give the memory some systematic exercises -not haphazard committing. Any sound memory may be trained.

LEARN TO READ 

We all learned to read when we were children. At first it was word by word, word by word. As we were able to put those words into sentences the pleasure of it encouraged us to read a little faster. After years of such progress we reached a rate of reading just about equal to the rate at which we could clearly speak the words aloud. When this speed was once attained we ceased any attempts to acquire greater speed. Some teachers even discouraged us from trying to read to ourselves at a speed greater than that at which we could speak. Why should we not continue to read faster and faster? We proceeded word by word at first, then as we made progress we read by groups of words, but most of us stopped when this speed was attained. Why not go on and read by sentences or paragraphs? The reason is simply that we ceased to acquire any greater speed while we were yet in the grades, and we have made no further attempts since that time. Now and again you will meet someone who continued to learn to read after the 

Page twenty-two

days of the graded school. Joseph Cook, the great lecturer, could read a book at the rate of several pages a minute, and he could quote striking passages from the book after this one rapid reading.

All of these considerations suggest that we should never cease to learn, and that we should never cease to learn to do things in a more efficient manner than we have been doing them before.

III The Social Life SIZING YOU UP 

OSSIBLY you think you Poss will not have much to do with social habits, now that you are in new surroundings. 

People are strangers to you and they will leave you alone, so you may think there is no need of considering this part of your nature. Certainly people will leave you alone at first, until they get a little inkling of what sort of a person you may chance to be. This holding aloof of others gives you your opportunity to direct your course. Above all other things you will determine-yes wholly determine your future in regard to your social life. You will select your companions, although it might seem your companions were selecting you. You will decide on what is agreeable to you, irrespective of what that thing may be itself. You will make progress towards what is good or towards what is bad, just as you choose. You will have the selfish or the unselfish attitude as you desire. 

In no part of your activities is it so easy to make progress toward that which is good as in social matters, but you will find that the easy way to good things and improvement does not suggest itself as readily as the way to bad things and degeneration. Good social habits and good social standing and right relations to the rest of humanity require thoughtful striving, just like any other good which is attainable. None of these things come without effort.

WATER SEEKS ITS LEVEL 

First of all your personal habits give you personal standing. 

If you play skillfully on a musical instrument you will naturally be found with musicians. If you are a smoker you will likely fall in with smokers. If you are clean in habits and pure 

Page twenty-four

in thought you will, almost unconsciously, associate with those who are clean in habits and pure in thoughts. If you are careless about your dress you will likely be a companion of slouchy persons. If you are neat in dress you will attract neat people and will be attracted by them. If you are a disloyal citizen, you will find your comrades disloyal. If you are religious, you will join in with religious people. "Birds of a feather flock together" applies almost without variation in this matter. Do not forget for one moment that you are the one on whom the choosing depends, but the others are choosing also, hence likes are sure to get together. 

You should not forget that success right here calls for effort, not just for drifting. As soon as one has shown himself to be a drifter there are plenty who will essay to lead the vacillating victim, and they will not lead into right things either. Hence, go slow about making social ties. "Be sure you are right, then go ahead."

NEED OF IMPROVEMENT 

Let us assume that you want the best in the way of social development. You may think you have come from the back woods or the hills, and therefore cannot hope to keep pace with your more favored brothers in social ways. If any such thought is in your mind, dismiss it at once. The seemingly more fortunate fellow from the city or town may have the advantage of having many things which show polished manners, but he has the handicap of not knowing how he got these and of not knowing their value. You will get them by hard work and you will know what they mean. He has as much to learn about them as you have, but the sad part is that he is not likely to know that he has anything to learn, while you see the need at once. All of us older folks can tell you of the country boy who came to college wearing great, coarse boots and clothes that did not fit him, and who could not find any place large enough to hold his hands and feet while he was talking to strangers. That boy, later on, was a social leader and a business leader and a man whose gracious manners and Page twenty-five

high social ideals were the admiration of everybody. He realized the situation and set about the task of remedying it with all of his ability.

If you know you need to learn how to speak in a gracious manner to others, how to make your voice more pleasant, how to be quick to help others, how to sacrifice your own comfort at times that others may have comfort, how to win the admiration of your elders, how to command the respect of children -if you know you have something to learn along these lines, then you are in a most excellent position to take charge of yourself and begin directing your energies to find out what to do and how to do it.

CLIMBING THE SOCIAL LADDER 

A common mistake is made by young people of your age, and often by people a little older than you, in regard to the real character of social advancement. It is the mistake of getting the notion that social advancement consists in attending dances, dinner parties, and such like, with people who have money but who do not necessarily have character. Lightness of feet in the dance is no evidence of a weighty brain; pleasure in games of chance is no evidence of ability to win in the game of life. Nothing is farther from real social advancement than running after money. One only needs to look at the wrecks in the business and social worlds which are so commonly evident in our towns and cities to see how this will-o'-the-wisp deceives men and women. "Climbing the social ladder" has come to mean going to destruction, because of the frequency of this result. Social life is desirable, but the gay life means ruin.

NEW FRIENDS 

You have made a good start when you have acquired pleasing manners and clean personal habits. Everybody wants to be a friend to such a person. These give you the start in the right direction. Who is attracted by a surly answer, an impudent retort, a dirty personal habit, a questionable story, profanity, ill-bred manners? These do not attract. Find who Page twenty-six

among your new friends are respected by all, and cultivate the acquaintance of such persons. Take time to get acquainted with them. When it comes to selecting close personal friends seek the ones who will be helpful to you, not ones for whom you have to make excuses. After you have your own character well established it will be time enough to go after straightening out someone who has not been careful in selecting his companions.

AMUSEMENTS 

Find opportunities to have attractive social activities. Your time schedule must provide for diversions. Find amusements where others mingle with you. Do not be a recluse. Visiting your companions in their rooms is good, but is so easily overdone. Choose the character of the amusements in which you are going to permit yourself to engage. Let them be wideawake, but let them be pure. By all means do not simply take the amusements which are just at hand and easiest to reach, with no thought as to whether they are good or bad. To attend a picture show just because it is a picture show, or to turn on the radio just to have the radio turned on does not indicate the possession of brains. The proper choice of amusements and entertainments requires work on your part-direction, oversight.

A really valuable amusement must give recreation, relaxation and pleasure; it must not give any tendency to develop the lower parts of our natures. It must not give impure suggestion, tolerance of roughness, familiarity with base lives, excusing of sin. It should give pure pleasure, encouragement of real refinement, familiarity with noble lives, praising of righteousness. When one returns from a true amusement he should feel better physically and more buoyant mentally, and he should carry away a desire to make the world about him a little better and happier place in which to live. It should give him good cheer and good purpose. Get this young person-yourselfat the right thing in the right way and make amusements count, just as you make eating or studying count.

MONEY 

Your use of money may easily make you a poor director of your own activities. The American habit of treating to this, that or the other thing appeals to the ordinary young person as something which should be emulated. It is a foolish custom and has been the undoing of many a young man. Only buy things when they are really needed. Do not spend money for unnecessary things. In most cases someone has worked hard and saved that you might have the start which is being given to you. To use one cent of such money foolishly is criminal, and shows a disrespect for your parents. If you are a young man whose parents give you all the money you want, no matter how you spend it, the lecture should be given to the parents and not to the son. Ordinarily such sons stay in college only a year or two at most, and then drop out for various reasons based on various forms of inefficiency. The poor parents have their hearts broken just the same, but they themselves were the cause of the trouble. Do not misunderstand me about spending money for pleasures. A young man or woman must have pleasures, and pleasures which cost money too; but let them be real and wholesome pleasures and let them be purchased in moderation.

LOYALTY 

These are times when loyalty has been much discussed. 

Loyalty to one's country is a great virtue, but there are other loyalties which are just as high in the scale of virtues-loyalty to friends, loyalty to the institutions which have made your present life possible, loyalty to great virtues, loyalty to that which is right. When one is loyal to the things which are fundamental there is little fear of his loyalty being questioned in any sphere of activity, such as the state, the social circle or the religious life.

To sum up, be clean and seek clean people for friends; be punctual, thorough, persistent, and make friends of people who do things; be frugal, helpful, loyal, and thus become a 

Page twenty-eight

companion of those who are held in esteem by everyone who knows them.

REAL MEN 

Choose as your companions those who speak good English and who are not given to slang; those who put their work in first place and foolishness in last place; those who realize that their lives are only lived once, and who are making good use of the preparation period; those who have good cheer and a joyous life because their lives are clean and pure; those who can be helpful to you because they have made good use of their opportunities; those whose habits are the same in public and in private; those who are allied with the best people and the best organizations about the community or the institution; those who are neat in dress, clean in person and cultivated in manners; those who manifest consideration for and helpfulness to others. With such companions your social habits are in safe keeping and life will be to you a pleasure indeed.

IV The Moral and Religious Life MORALS AND RELIGION NECESSARY 

HERE may be those who think they can have good physical, mental and social habits without having good moral and religious habits. It is possible to do this for a short time, but I have not yet seen it last through a college course of four years. It is only evanescent because it is not a genuine life. It still remains true that you must take charge of your moral and religious life. You must make very momentous decisions in these particulars. If you are going to get the best out of your whole nature, you will have to decide on the things which are best and which belong to morals and religion. You cannot get away from this by neglect any more than you can get away from the physical side by neglect. You may neglect the body and come out with a very indifferent and inefficient body, but yet with a body. You may neglect the moral and religious side of your life and come out with some sort of a character, but it will be indifferent or inefficient. 

STEALING 

The Ten Commandments of Holy Writ are commonly called The Moral Law. Like other laws they tell what must be avoided. No one in his right mind would for a moment dispute the righteousness of these moral laws, but sometimes it gets into the minds of young people that some of them are of less importance than others, and, therefore, they may be broken with no serious consequences. They think it is all right not to worship idols, but it does not matter much if one steals small things or just "appropriates" the property of othersproviding one is not caught at it. The ten commandments do not make any statements as to differences of sins because Page thirty

they differ in size or amount. Neither is one commandment given a higher order than another. 

Stealing is taking the property of others, whether little or much. Young man! Young woman! that should be so thoroughly implanted in your mind that there never would arise any question as to property rights. The property rights of others should be sacred in regard to the most worthless of articles. "Swiping" is plain stealing; "appropriating" the property of others is plain stealing. The doing of such things puts one in the class with burglars and thieves. This applies with equal clearness to the property of corporations or institutions. Some get the false notion that because certain property belongs to a corporation or to a state, therefore it is perfectly legitimate to take that property for their own use. Unfortunately this idea has gotten into the minds of men who ought to know better, and we have the public graft and scandals as the result. The lower a country stands in the scale of the nations the more has this idea prevailed. Possibly the statement would better be made in the reverse form, namely, the more this idea has prevailed in any nation, the farther down that nation stands in the scale of national efficiency. 

A student sometimes has to make a decision on the basis of these principles in regard to honesty in examinations or written exercises or note books. The principles are basic and apply in all cases. Thou shalt not covet and thou shalt not steal are fundamental to organized society. Even thieves observe these principles among themselves. Be four-square on these things, and never let yourself deviate one iota from that which is right.

FATHER AND MOTHER 

We are not going to go over all of the ten commandments in detail, but two others should certainly be mentioned because of their peculiar application to young people. Honor thy father and thy mother. Remember the folks at home. Write to them often. Tell them about your experiences, your difficulties, your joys and pleasures, your surroundings-tell them much and tell them often. Never let mother wonder why son 

Page thirty-one

or daughter does not write. A good plan is to have a set time to write, and always write at that time. Send a card if there is not time to write a letter when the date comes around. Then sometimes write a nice letter of love and affection between times; one which will arrive at a time when they are not looking for it. You will never know how much good such a letter does until you are a parent yourself. Be careful in every way of the things which concern them. It may be you are using their hard-earned and illy-spared money; show them fully how you have used every cent you have used of it. Keep a careful account day by day and let them look over these accounts. When you contemplate something new, think of father and mother and ask yourself if you want them to know you are doing this. Make confidants of them even though you have to write to tell them what you have to say. Give them your companionship when you are at home on a vacation; they crave it and you will enjoy it, too, if you are right. "Be it ever so humble, there's no place like home." Do your part in making it the best home in all the land, because it is a home of love and affection, a home of trust and confidence, a home of purity and righteousness.

SEXUAL PURITY 

Thou shalt not commit adultery. Oh the wickedness and viciousness of the one who would attempt to lead a young man to transgress this commandment! The temptation is presented under cover of darkness and false pretenses. Society has been lax in taking proper precautions, and the results made evident when our young men were called into the World War startled us. No doubt the day will soon come when all young people will be subjected to a physical examination before being permitted to mingle with large numbers of others in any institution, and those who have been guilty of sexual crimes will be debarred. There is great inconsistency in placarding a house and quarantining it because some member of the family has mumps or measles, and not quarantining against one who has disease from sexual sins. Measles and mumps are short- 

Page thirty-two

lived diseases, and the patient is soon over them and back to normal health; but the other not only continues for years, but is commonly transmitted with dire results to the next generation. Ordinary knowledge, coupled with ordinary horse-sense would keep anyone from taking any chances of acquiring these diseases which nature, fortunately, has placed as a penalty upon those who disobey this commandment. 

That young man who would expose himself to the disease of leprosy or typhus fever would be considered a fool. He who exposes himself to the leprosy of sexual diseases is none the less a fool than the other. The pity of it is that there are those who try to persuade young men that there is no danger. Do not take your medical advice from a moral leper-it is likely to be poor advice. If you must have further advice, consult a real and reputable physician. Happy are those who come to the marriage altar with clean and pure lives behind them. 

WHOM WILL YOU SERVE? 

We have yet to write of religion. Is religion necessary? You will have some kind of religion, that is certain. It may be atheism or agnosticism or it may be some form of orthodoxy -you will have a religion of some sort. Here again it would seem to be the logical thing to make an intelligent selection of what is chosen as your religion. In this country a wonderful heritage has come to you through religion. Our schools, hospitals, rights of property, eleemosynary institutions, literature, science-in fact, virtually all we have that appeals to us as thinking beings has come to us as the heritage of the Jewish and Christian religion. He who would receive all this rich heritage and make no return is a most ungrateful member of society. To receive the blessings of Christian civilization and not make any return is not far from stealing, nay, rather, it is gross stealing.

There are other religions which have predominated in other parts of the world. If the results of paganism in Africa or 

Mohammedanism in Arabia or Buddhism in India appeal to you as better than those of Christianity, then it would be wise Page thirty-three

to go to that better country. If you want to be an agnostic or atheist, go to the countries of Africa and the East Indies, where these ideas are the prevailing ones, and where your ideas will be in harmony with the civilization already developed. If the Christian civilization of the United States appeals to you, then do your part and make some return for the benefits which have come to you from that civilization. Transmit these benefits to the next generation, but made a little better because you have had an opportunity to help make them better. The man who lives in a community or state and only receives from the community or state and never gives any return is a burden to the community or state. I am sure you are not such a member of society. You propose to transmit to the next generation a heritage which is yet better than the noble heritage which our fathers have transmitted to us. "Choose ye this day whom ye will serve."

RELIGIOUS GROWTH 

It is impossible to develop in religious things without giving time and thought and study to the things of religion. 

Many a young person has had religious surroundings in early life and has had faith in his religion, but during the period of study for higher education the religious side has been neglected and given no attention. It is small wonder that such a one has little interest in religious things when he gets his university degree. His notions about religious things are the notions of a boy and not of a man, for he has neglected to grow religiously while he was growing mentally. Any other line of endeavor which is given the same treatment would give the same result. A boy might have great interest in electricity as a boy; but if during the whole period of his education he entirely neglects electricity, he will have little interest in it, for his conceptions of the subject are those of the boy and not those of a man, and he is now a man with a man's attitude of mind towards things in general. Keep developing your religious life, for this is the most fundamental part of your nature.

SUMMARY 

Take charge of this body of yours and see that it is given over to the things which make for strength and health. 

Take charge of this mind of yours and see that it is made subject to the discipline and training that will give it a mastery over the problems of life. 

Take charge of this social being of yours and see that you are made a fit companion for the noblest of men. 

Take charge of this soul of yours and see that it is prepared to lead your strong body and masterful mind in rendering a worthy service to your fellowmen and to the Great Maker of us all. 

You are taking charge of your physical, your mental, your social and your moral and religious lives as you leave the home of your younger years. May you take over this charge with such a complete and successful leadership that there shall be produced a most useful, highly honored, respected and beloved citizen.
