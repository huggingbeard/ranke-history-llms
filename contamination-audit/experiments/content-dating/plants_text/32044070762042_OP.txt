Viewing these modern facilities against their historical background, one can predict a definite acceleration in each trend. For they have removed age-old obstacles to international intercourse and trade, obstacles to progress 578410

hitherto presented by great distances, and by mountains, deserts and seas-not to overlook hazards in unfamiliar customs of thought and action among isolated peoples. All this conquest of space and time has in effect bridged a chasm in the highway to tomorrow.

I. 

The Trend Toward Larger Combinations of Sovereign Power. 

The trend and its phases. A trend has been apparent for many centuries, though usually in its primitive phase of wars for empire, toward larger combinations of sovereign power. For example, European history from 

Charlemagne in 800 A. D. to Napoleon in 1800 A. D., has recorded a series of attempts through force, to obtain the advantages of the old Roman Empire. The Romans could communicate and trade with one another in security, without tariff or military restrictions, and over a large and economically integrated territory. Our United States of North America and the British Commonwealth of Nations for the first time have achieved these basic advantages since the days of Ancient Rome, and indeed to a much greater extent through cooperation instead of force —thereby introducing an advanced phase of this trend. 

Their examples have served as a steady impetus to the efforts of other peoples, and conspicuously the Soviet Republics, to reach the same result. This modern phase of the trend, emphasizing cooperation instead of force, has been considerably furthered by modern facilities of intercommunication and transportation. 

Germany and Japan. The new orders that Germany and Japan seek to impose by force in this war, are in reality reactionary attempts to use this trend, in its more

3 primitive phase, for their own ends. Their addiction to dogmas of master race and of divine mission to rule others, has exposed their purpose of world domination by force of arms, and has contributed as much as any other single factor, to bring about their military defeat in this war. Had these aggressors been able to obtain the cooperation of the countries they have overrun, they might have succeeded in establishing their new orders. Such a world would have been roughly divided into two empires, which by their very natures would come into conflict. 

There would not be room for long on the globe, for two such empires at the same time. But the conquered peoples have usually seen that they can by resistance wherever opportunity affords, aid the Allied Nations, and after the victorious peace, live under progressive governmentwhich is a far cry from a system of land bondage to the industrial conqueror. Too many among them have seen the highway to the future, not to recognize highwaymen when they see them.

But no one can afford to overlook the sinister fact, that military defeat of Germany will lose significance, if every new war of conquest is to leave Germany relatively stronger in population and resources than surrounding countries; which it has finally ruined by repeated devastations and depopulated by starvation and mass murder. 

Much the same may be said of Japan; as indicated by the boast of a commanding officer of its army to the prisoners of Bataan, that Japan would attack again and again if defeated in this war, until the moment of complete victory over America.

Another war fought on the soil of France will find that nation less able to defend its integrity. Nor will 

Poland, The Ukraine, nor Italy be ready in many a year for another conflict. It is authoritatively said that the

United States does not now have the oil reserves, to repeat lend-lease in another such war. Therefore, it is imperative in the interests of peace, that the Allied Nations favor a consolidation of said surrounding countries, particularly to the west of Germany. But no less important is the revival of state's rights in Germany, and the liquidation as a class, of the junkers and militarists. The peace conference will be advised to adopt a plan to divide Germany along the following lines. They can partition Prussia and the rest of the Reich into six. principal state sovereignties, founded upon the principal sovereignties prior to 1870: Austria, Bavaria, The Rhineland, Hanover, Brandenburg and Saxony. A confederation of these six states with a national government for national affairs and with full acknowledgment of state's rights, will be their choice. Austria is to have the right to enter a Danubian confederation instead, if it prefers. 

This plan eliminates the many smaller principalities. All 

Germany can be partitioned to form these six states; except the territories ceded to other powers. The Sudetenland and Upper Prussian Silesia are to be ceded to Czecho-Slovakia, because necessary to its security; East 

Prussia to Poland in order to solve the problem of the Polish Corridor; Alsace and Lorraine will be returned to France.

There are sure to be problems of population readjustment at the peace conference, such as moving the Germans out of East Prussia, the Sudetenland, and Upper Prussian Silesia. But these problems while highly important, do no more than touch upon the fundamental changes in the world picture, indicated by the trend toward larger combinations of sovereign power. 

A strong China is a prime requirement to progress in Asia. Japan dreads nothing more fatal to its ambi-

5 tions, than a united, progressive China; as witness the speed with which it burnt schools, and reintroduced the opium trade in conquered territories there. Over-population is the curse of China, making it a slow process to raise the standard of living. For the people cling to their ancestral ways of suffering, and endanger every betterment of their living conditions, by a corresponding increase in the population. The moving picture and radio have been effective means of mass instruction in Asia. 

During the slow growth of this great power, the peace of the Far East must be maintained by the Union of Soviet Republics, the British Commonwealth of Nations, and the United States of North America, cooperating with China and one another in their respective spheres of influence. 

A Japan stripped of its conquests at the peace conference, will no longer be a menace to the general security-unless it succeeds in its old policy of setting one of these powers off against another. These great powers should under no circumstances divide in the Pacific, so Japan may rule.

The modern phase of the trend. Voluntary combinations of sovereign power is the modern phase of the trend under consideration. This phase grows out of the frustration and anxiety that people feel, who live in a small country (and particularly in Europe, where the examples of the United States of North America and the British 

Commonwealth of Nations have special appeal), with the small country's tariff barriers to trade, its military conscription and high taxes; and who are obliged to bear the burdens generally of an independent state, too heavy for its strength. Often such countries seek protection and trade agreements from a great power, at the price of a great deal of their independence. They stand to gain by combining with the great power, where invited, as a com-

6 ponent state; or if enough of them are in proximity, by combining with one another. The incentive exists for this voluntary action; progress is making it feasible, to an ever broader extent.

Europe. Our original thirteen colonies were the first to succeed in a voluntary union of small states, to form a large and effective sovereignty. Their example did much to turn Europeans to thoughts of a united states of Europe. 

Napoleon had a plan to unite Europe, as appears in his Memoirs. He lost his opportunity when he ceased to be the symbol of liberation to Europe, and became an absolute emperor. If Napoleon had grasped the principles of voluntary representative government, the people might have continued to support his empire; in order to enjoy the prosperity and security attendant upon a united Europe. The British Empire presents a contemporary instance of continued popular support of such an empire. 

But Napoleon did not realize that the trend was entering into a more modern phase toward voluntary combinations of sovereign power, and not toward hereditary empire-which was even then on its way out as a less effective exponent of the trend, under the influence of improved transportation and inter-communication on the continent. The history of Europe since his fall from power, has been a story of constant revolutions and wars. of liberation from outworn limitations of government; and with the principle of the balance of power to maintain peace, a failure. That tangle of states shown on the map of Europe west of Russia, is desperately in need of a remedy for the ills expressed in constant wars that threaten to destroy their civilization. And from all the signs one remedy is the voluntary unions of these states into larger economically integrated combinations of sovereign power. Such unions are now possible as never be-

fore; because in this era, the country of today has but the size of the county of yesterday, in terms of transport and the exchange of ideas.

The Spanish Americas. The coming of the airplane and automobile, radio and moving picture, throws light on the future of the Spanish Americas. When Simon 

Bolivar freed the northern part of South America from

Old Spain, a little more than a hundred years ago, it was his dream that there should be a united states in the Spanish Americas, on the order of our own United States-perhaps even a Pan American union of the New World. Unfortunately, the means of inter-communication and transportation of his time were so inadequate, that they could not serve to bind such a large territory together. Not even the adjoining States of Colombia and 

Venzuela could stay long united; it took weeks in those days and indeed until comparatively recent times, to travel or communicate between these countries or between any of their neighboring states. There are no railroads to this day between these states, to serve as a binder for the brick in Bolivar's house of state, no railroads such as we have long had to build a nation from ocean to ocean. Nor is there even a system of good roads in these states today, nor a merchant marine; no free press with wide distribution facilities exists as in our country, nor even an adequate level of literacy, to establish and support steady inter-communication. The 

Spanish states to the south are more modern and developed with the exception of Bolivia, and consequently they will be the first to feel the strong influence of this trend of the times.

A cogent illustration of the fatal effect of poor intercommunication and transportation, is to be seen in the history of Central America following the expulsion of the

Spanish, also a little more than a hundred years ago. The 

Central Americas lasted for a few years under one sovereignty, but perforce fell apart into a patchwork of defenseless states, for lack of even a single highway to connect them together. The Spaniards had not provided them with the economic heritage, for the task of opening a way through the mountains. 

Today, airplane routes bind these inaccessible communities, and the building of the Pan American highway from Alaska to Argentina, to be completed by the middle of 1945, will open much farther the door to modern living for the populations of the Central and South Americas. The trend toward larger combinations of sovereign power, will be manifested in the Spanish Americas, which have in their favor the advantage of a common language and a common tradition. 

Examples of U. S. A. and U. S. S. R. in World War II. 

There is no force in the modern world equal in penetration and endurance to the force of example. For with each improvement in the means of inter-communication and transportation, the more clearly does a successful example appear. This war presents two very inspiring examples: first, the miraculous contribution of the industry of the United States of North America to the winning of the war-credited by Marshal Stalin at Teheran with winning the war; and second, the magnificent resistance to aggression shown by the Union of Soviet Republics. This industry and this confederated strength are the outstanding present day illustrations of what larger combinations of sovereignty can accomplish, both in raising the general standard of living and in establishing international security. If progress, demanded of men as the price of security, is no longer to be the flower on the battlefield of wars for survival, it must grow spon-

taneously in any soil watered not by blood, but by inspiration from the example of others. 

The contribution of our industry to the winning of this war has provided a powerful impetus to progress: first, because it furnishes the means for security in time of war; second, because of its well-known achievements in raising our standard of living; and third, because the process of building up our industry is also well known, thanks to the speedy dissemination of knowledge in our day.

Solomon, King of Israel and reputed for his wisdom, was unknown to much of the world of his day; which was separated by the wise men of old into the known and the unknown world. He had an industrial city and sea port at the head of the eastern arm of the Red Sea, which has only recently been excavated. Remains of a smelter using the Bessemer principle to purify the metal, principally copper, have been found there, with long air passageways in the walls to catch the strong and still prevailing wind; in order to direct a blast of air into the molten metal and burn out its impurities. Apparently, this refining principle of metallurgy was forgotten with the passage of time, only to be rediscovered by Sir Bessemer in the last century. Thus it may be seen from this single instance, how great discoveries have been made and then forgotten in the past; for lack of a wide-spread dissemination of the knowledge. 

Every country is measuring its opportunities to share in the wealth of modern industry, and realizing that to share, it must cooperate with other countries of complementary economy, as through reciprocal tariffs. Where such countries are neighbors, the incentive is present for them to go farther, and obtain security for their new coordinated economy by combining into a single sovereignty.

Rehabilitating the war-torn regions after this war, should further this attraction of small states to greater prosperity and security through confederation. Absent purchasing power on the international exchange, many devastated areas must resort first to gifts of surplus food and clothing from abroad to alleviate suffering from want; then in many places they will resort to barter and governmental enterprises, to build up capital. It is widely thought that private investors cannot be relied upon, and would not be equal to the financing of such enormous rehabilitation. The further method may prove practicable in some instances, of receiving concessions, to rebuild an industrial plant in a devastated city through a corporation organized for the purpose by the investing government; with the right of control and enjoyment of the proceeds of the enterprise vested in the said corporation, until repaid within a definitely determined number of years.

The employment of the local population in the production and sale of the product-preferably the product of a public utility-would at the same time show the population how to run and maintain such service for themselves. Air bases, either granted outright, or as security for an industrial investment as just considered, also would be attractive and feasible collateral in certain cases, particularly when the bases are on outlying strategic islands. All these and other ways of rehabilitation will serve still more to impress upon the people of the small states overrun in the war, the advantages of a large confederated sovereignty. 

The example of Soviet Russia's resistance to aggression by Nazi Germany, shows how a strong confederation can be accomplished under great difficulties. For the 

Union of Soviet Republics became strong while under the combined menace of Germany and Japan, and under the handicaps of seventy spoken languages and of many

races. Existing means of communication and transportation are certain to accelerate the development of this farflung union of republics, where distances are so extraordinary even by our own standards. 

Their full name is the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, and time will tell whether they are in the vanguard of progress because of their socialism. Travelers frequently observe that our countrymen and the Russians get along well together. Two fundamental differences between us are: one, they have a one-party system of government, in contrast to our two-party system, resulting in government by dictatorship; and two, they differ with us as to what property in the state should be considered public property of the state, expanding our doctrine of public regulation of private property devoted to a public use, to the utmost limit of public ownership. For in 

Soviet Russia the state owns practically everything, except a citizen's house and lot, his household furnishings and personal belongings, and his income from work in the services of the state.

The paths of these great powers will come together, for each will learn from the other's successes. Already, non-communists have been elected to minor offices in Soviet Russia, thus establishing a modest precedent for the growth of the two-party system. The Russian leaders are pursuing the policy of letting liberties and understanding of responsibilities coincide, as their people advance and overcome the handicaps of their history of oppression. Furthermore, they have come to recognize the value of the profit motive, to promote industrial production.

The United States is changing. Our enormous public debt will increase government control over our financial and economic structure, to prevent inflation and preserve

the national credit. A fundamental factor in our future development is the capacity of our industry and agriculture to produce enough for everyone's needs; so that the problem of poverty becomes essentially a problem of unemployment. Social values will center upon the preservation of this rare heritage for the common benefit. Particularly is this true, since more and more of the population are coming to depend on salaries or wages for their livelihood. There is already in evidence a shift in emphasis from values based on passive private ownership to values based on a record of active private productivity. 

Ownership in trust and individual talents in trust to be used for the common weal, are already recognized principles of big business, with corresponding financial rewards. The Supreme Court of the United States has announced in several cases, that the value of property is not ordinarily intrinsic but depends on its income. Income in turn, in our economy, depends on productive capacity. Not only is our national productive capacity to reshape our society along these lines, but also it is the foundation for an advanced economy, wherein it and not gold will support the currency. Keynes, the British economist, has won considerable support for this view of the diminished importance of gold, among those who plan how we may handle our staggering public debt and revive international trade. 

As Soviet Russia approaches our productive capacity, these same social and economic influences will predominate.

Access to the rest of the world by air can be expected to relieve considerably the pressure behind Russia's foreign policy since the days of Peter the Great, to obtain ice-free sea ports, through Turkey, Persia, or India. On the other hand, the small Baltic states, Lithuania, Latvia, and Esthonia, are once again to be drawn into the orbit if

not the boundaries, of this powerful sovereignty. If they are recognized as republics in this Union of Republics, they should be better off both from the standpoint of prosperity and security. It is very important that Soviet 

Russia use their prestige and power to stabilize foreign affairs and not to upset them. The dissolution of the Commintern is a hopeful sign. Great powers can best control international affairs, not by intervention, but by relying on their successful example at home. There is one clear exception to a foreign policy of non-intervention, in these days of powerful, swift armament, and that is when intervention is necessary to prevent a world war. Had France and England prevented the Nazis from marching into the Rhineland, many people think with considerable justification, that World War II would not have been. 

Predictable voluntary combinations in Europe. Barriers of language and race have largely prevented Central Europe and the Balkan states from confederating successfully, although these states are economically integrated upon the Danube River basin. The nucleus of a united states of western Europe may start here, instead of from a union between England and France. Soviet Russia's achievement in uniting despite these barriers, is a potent influence upon these states. Just as Russian is the official language among the Soviet Republics, so may these states adopt an official language; for illustration, they could adopt a basic language, on the analogy of Basic English, which is making a strong bid for adoption as the world language, for international purposes. It is true that a separatist tradition also obstructs such a union, yet growing inter-communication and transportation in this area, will gradually supplant this tradition of older generations, with new traditions of cooperation in trade and ultimately in sovereignty.

General Smuts of South Africa has recently suggested a union of England and France after the war. Ties of empire impede such a union; though one may not forget that following the Norman Conquest, these two countries were under a single sovereignty for the most part, for several centuries. It may well be, however, that England will find a more effective confederation with Holland and the Scandinavian countries, inviting these countries into the British Commonwealth of Nations; and that France will enter into a Latin union with Belgium, Italy and Spain. A restoration of six German principalities prior to 1870, The Rhineland, Bavaria, Austria, Saxony, Hanover and Brandenburg, and dividing Germany between them in the peace settlement as hitherto outlined, may pave the way for these states eventually to join the 

Dutch and Scandinavians in the British Commonwealth of Nations. Before this may be, these German States must definitely and finally reestablish their states's rights; so that, for example, Hanover would look to Holland just as willingly as to Brandenburg. The Slav peoples of Central and Balkan Europe, in the event of the foregoing combination if not before, may choose to seek admission as republics in the Soviet Union rather than to form a Danubian confederation. Thus, nearly all 

Europe could be organized into the Teutonic, Latin and Slav combinations of sovereignty. It is more likely however, that economic considerations will favor a Danubian confederation in this region; and that the states of Germany will form economic ties with Czecho-Slovakia, Poland, and the Danubian confederation-which will be steps toward a united states of Europe. 

League of Nations. When the short history of the 

League of Nations between World Wars I and II is con sidered, no one need feel dismayed that the League did

not prevent this second world war. For the society of nations did not yet have a strong enough foundation for such a structure. Here and there were communities prepared to accept international responsibilities; but the general level of deliberate thought on this subject in most countries had not risen to the point of action. It was thought dangerous, and it was, to enter into commitments of mutual trust, with countries not prepared for such moral responsibilities. Much development is lacking in the universal standard of living, to provide the background for an effective League of Nations. Just as Ancient 

Greece attained a civilization not equalled before or since in art, literature, and philosophy, yet perished in dissensions; so must any program such as the League, remain on paper when not generally understood and accepted. This is a true function of communication and transportation systems: that they spread far the grain, leaving to each people the separation of the wheat from the chaff; but furnishing successful examples of other peoples by way of instruction. The growth of air transportation brings with it the necessity to control air power for the general welfare, and keep it out of the hands of the aggressor in dangerous proportions. The Allied Nations should see to it after this war, that the Axis powers do not possess the facilities to put an aggressive force into the air nor on the sea; until they have earned the confidence and respect of the world, by contributing in good faith to international security in the ways of peace. This necessity for elementary security will pull for the establishment of a central world authority, to register and inspect the air fleets of all countries. The conquest of distance in our time has opened many new avenues leading to a future League of Nations, the ultimate phase of this trend toward larger combinations of sovereign power.

II. 

The Trend Toward Decentralization Within the Framework of a Large Sovereignty.

The trend. As in the case of any other movement of natural or social law, whenever the trend toward larger combinations of sovereign power came to realization once more in history, after the fall of Ancient Rome-(more modern examples of such combinations are the British Empire, the United States of North America, Brazil, the Union of Soviet Republics, and modern China, in their historical order of creation)-this movement was met and a counter-balance sought by another trend; in this case a trend toward the decentralization of local affairs within the large sovereignty. The greater the territory and population governed, the greater the need for local administration of local affairs. Failure to give due recognition to this need for decentralization, has often been a large factor in the decline and fall of empires. An important error of the Romans contributing to the decline and fall of their empire as recorded by Gibbons, was the excessive centralization of power and authority over local affairs, into the hands of an unwieldy and irresponsible bureaucracy in Rome; which over-taxed and over-governed the provinces. It is a common mistake in the history of empires; as witness the empire of Assyria, of Persia, and of Alexander before the heyday of

Rome; and closer to our own times to be observed in the example of France under Louis XIV, and of Russia under the Czars. For in all of these empires, the imperial court focused all powers of state upon the principal city, and governed badly the outlying sections by remote control, until it fell.

The individual. There is a definite democratic phase to this trend toward decentralization. Democracy rep-

resents the principles preserving the rights of the individual within the state, to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. These cardinal tenets of the democratic state closely depend upon and grow out of decentralized government, with local control of local affairs. For in no other way can these primary rights of the individual be safeguarded. Government by remote control is incapable of those often delicate local adjustments that protect and maintain these individual rights; since a national order must of necessity look upon the people en masse and not as individuals.

The privileges of Roman citizenship were not the least of the advantages, that have led to the series of attempts in history to restore the old Roman Empire. Yet 

Roman citizenship in reality was a guarantee of personal rights only in the days of the Roman Republic, and but sporadically under the Empire; the guarantee usually disappearing under emperors like Tiberius, and all too rarely appearing under such as Augustus, the first emperor, in the Golden Age of Rome, and as Marcus Aurelius of the

Silver Age. It was loss of this guarantee of individual rights under the absolute emperors, that was a great element in the decay of the military, political and economic strength of Rome.

Feudalism was the successor to the late Roman Empire of the West, bringing order and a measure of security out of chaos. The feudal system of government was extremely localized in contrast to the sway of imperial Rome, but it was also extremely disorganized in terms of national sovereignty. Feudalism was necessary to begin anew the building of the state, with protection of individual rights extended first to the feudal lords, then to their retainers and so on to ever greater groups of people. The individual was rigidly bound to his place by birth, in this society. For most people this meant serf-

dom, because they were bound to the land for life and their feudal lords had the power of life and death over them. There was no protection nor even recognition of individual rights, no citizenship, in this primitive society; hearkening back to the social organization of the Greeks at the time of the Trojan War.

However, upon the revival of the trades in the cities, guilds became a force to be reckoned with by the feudal lords; as illustrated by the history of London, Paris, and the Cities of the Hanseatic League, of the Low Countries, and of Italy. These guilds through their unity established the city state, and revived thereby the idea of citizenship affording protection to the rights of the individual. The 

Roman Catholic Church strove to protect the rights of the clergy against the turbulent laity, during the Dark and Middle Ages. Added protection for the residents of cities and towns from the feudal lords came from the royal power, in its own struggle with the lords to establish a national sovereignty. The advent of the printing press and of gunpowder accomplished a very great deal to reestablish the importance of the individual, who by his writings could influence the many, and who with gunpowder and shot could defeat the mightiest knight in armor.

Finally came the English Revolution in the seventeenth century and then the French Revolution over a hundred years later, wherein the rights of the average man sought violent expression. Yet the England and 

France of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, illustrate how protection by law was principally for the propertied man. The Industrial Revolution in the nineteenth century operated to spread the holding of property ever more widely, with the result that more and more people in the advancedcountries of Europe, came to enjoy the

protection of law in fact. This democratic phase is continuing today, with ever greater popular representation and individual rights.

This phase appears in government civil service. 

It is spreading into modern industrial relations; for instance, by virtue of federal and some state legislation in the United States, a majority of the employees mentioned in the legislation can bargain collectively with the employer, as to the terms of employment. 

Although the law has not laid adequate responsibilities upon the employees, commensurate with their new privileges of collective bargaining, the Supreme Court of the United States has upheld the legislation as constitutional; on the ground that it is a step, in a step by step solution of the labor problem to prevent strikes. This individual freedom brings its responsibilities to deal fairly with the employer and the government, so that everybody may benefit from a well operated business. Enjoyment of this freedom directly depends on acceptance of the obligations that go with every liberty. 

The State. No taxation without representation led to our own Boston Tea Party in revolutionary years, when we won our independence from England. This trend toward decentralization has long since been in evidence in the British Empire, which has developed into the British Commonwealth of Nations. But before that event, it had received recognition in the Constitution of the United States. The Supreme Court of the United States in many reported decisions, has ruled that under the Constitution it is for each state of the forty-eight in the union, to determine the wisdom of its own legislation; as distinguished from the question of the power of the State under the Constitution, to pass the legislation. This eminent tribunal has stated that the Constitution intends each state to

be free to experiment in its own legislative workshop; so long as the legislation does not violate constitutional limitations against arbitrary or discriminatory action, nor transgress as to those powers expressly vested in the national government. When the wisdom of state legislation has proved out successful, then the example is automatically carried home to other states for considerationand progress is furthered in this orderly way. Furthermore, our federal Constitution not only leaves the reservoir of power in the people of the component states, expressly granting to the national government only those powers essential to its unifying functions, but the Constitution goes farther and decentralizes the federal power itself, into the legislative, executive and judicial branches. 

The illustrious coterie of statesmen who drafted and supported this Constitution, headed by Washington, Franklin, Jefferson and Adams, saw an opportunity rare in history to lay the foundations for a new state, according to the wisdom and experience of the past. 

Montesquieu, not Machiavelli, and Pitt the Younger, not Napoleon, called the turn of the eighteenth century toward the nineteenth century that we know; because their policies of statecraft made allowance for this trend, and proved to be successful in that critical period. In our own century, a Germany favoring the policy of unlimited centralized power, has sought to call the turn for the next thousand years; by ruthlessly attacking the British Empire, Russia and the United States, both in World 

Wars I and II. Germany found that prudent decentralization of government in these sovereignties, is a source not of weakness but of popular strength; which can save a large sovereignty relatively unprepared for war, that would otherwise succumb to such an attack. What better proof of this, than a comparison between the performance of the Russians under the Czars in World War

I, and their performance at Stalingrad in World War II, in the Union of Soviet Republics. 

Nevertheless, Czecho-Slovakia quickly succumbed under Germany's attack in this war, although better governed; because of its small territory, population and resources. Hence, it will be seen that while on the surface, the trend toward larger combinations of sovereign power may appear to be in opposition to the trend toward decentralization within sovereignty, actually the two trends complement each other, and when properly adjusted, can create and maintain a sovereignty of continental proportions.

A prime law in decentralization of state government, is, that only so much power is to be placed in any one authority as is necessary to enable that authority to carry on the task assigned, by the governing body of representatives of the governed. Only those matters of state, in consequence, such as national defense and national currency, and interstate and foreign commerce, which the component states cannot independently control for the general welfare, are the matters of state to be vested in the national authority.

This same decentralizing trend is seen in the modern development of industry in the state. For example, industrial plants are nowadays being located or even relocated in whatever part of the United States of North America, where they can best enjoy access to the materials and labor required for their products, and at the same time, secure the maximum accessibility to their markets. 

The advent of good roads and railroads in our South have attracted there many mills of the clothing industry, close to raw materials and water power; mills that formerly were all to be found in New England. Iron and steel are made now in considerable volume west of the Rockies and in the Mid-West, and no longer exclusively

i in the East. Motor cars, for a clear illustration of mass production methods, are made by assembling the products of many plants all over the country, plants with planned locations that permit the greatest savings in production and distribution costs. Thus, industry can make motor cars at reasonable prices in mass quantities and still pay high wages. A large number of industries operating in this way, have brought up the standard of living of our population, to the extent that it provides by far the greatest part of the market for mass production goods; in comparison with which our foreign market is small. The airplane will vitalize this decentralization in industry still more. In fact, modern facilities of communication and transportation operate to spread out industry, since they make possible in this way greater savings in production and distribution costs. This process is also to be seen at work in the British Commonwealth of Nations, including India, in the Union of Soviet Republics, in Brazil and in China; wherever a large, economically integrated territory exists, with the requisite resources and under a regime encouraging free trade throughout the territory. 

Competition on the world market between the products of industries so favored and of industries of small countries, places the latter in the position of lowering their standard of living through lower wages, in order to place a product on the world market at a comparable price. They are unable to achieve the savings in production and distribution costs. Thus, the small state tears down on the one hand through low wages and resulting restricted domestic market for its products, what it seeks to build up on the other through an increase of foreign trade and a favorable rate of exchange; namely, its standard of living. Industry in small countries is also at the mercy of high tariffs barring economical access to raw materials abroad, and again barring economical access

to a market for its products abroad. The result is that such country sets up high tariffs to protect its small industry at home, from the influx of goods produced by more favored industries elsewhere; and to that extent hinders world trade and prosperity, including its own, all in order to maintain a small, defenseless but independent sovereignty. Industry primarily exists to improve the domestic standard of living and the national security; with only the surpluses of ordinary products, in which the country does not possess unusual economic advantage, properly available for foreign trade. This is possible in a large sovereignty but not in a small one. 

The real solution for these difficulties of the small state is to seek economic and ultimately political confederation with its neighbors, to form a tariff-free combination large enough in resources and population, to support a modern integrated industrial economy-but at the same time to reserve its state's rights. It is the fear of being absorbed by a larger state in the confederation and treated like a persecuted minority, that looms so large in the minds of small states. Still, Rhode Island confederated with New York, and The Ukraine with Russia proper, without this consequence. A study of the trend toward decentralization within the British Empire, and quite recently within the Soviet Union-where it was announced in effect that the sixteen Soviet Republics are each to have diplomatic status—should show small sovereignties that the trend of the times favors their entering into a confederation, and still maintaining their identities as states.

The city. This trend toward decentralization within sovereignty is nowhere more in evidence than in the modern city. The automobile and airplane, radio and moving picture, are factors just as active in furthering the

internal development of cities and states, as they are in furthering larger combinations of sovereignty. Cities are spreading out into suburban centers, where the workers can have a house and lot, and yet live near the place of employment. Modern city planning takes into account this tendency of communities, to spread out with each improvement in transportation and communication; a tendency frequently neglecting the downtown section. 

Careful planning seeks the satisfaction of this popular urge for better living space, by the development of the planned suburb. The acceleration of this tendency has been remarkable in recent years.

Henry Ford and other leaders of our industry have long advocated the establishment of industrial plants well away from crowded cities; so that workers can own their own homes and have a garden, the produce of which can add to their income and economic security. 

There is little economic security in an overcrowded city. 

People who live under healthful conditions free from the unnatural pressures of civic overcrowding, find it much easier to stay away from paths leading to poverty, disease and crime. Such people are also those who comprise the great markets for the industrial mass production of products. The overcrowded city with its daily chronicles of criminal waste and destruction, presents the most important domestic problem of contemporary civilization. 

The city of the future promises to be spread out and decentralized, retaining identity as a single city only to enable the well separated parts, to enjoy the benefits of cooperative local government.

New cities will grow up on the great international trade routes of the air now spreading over the earth. 

City planners have a real field here for their talents, setting up successful examples for older communities to fol-

low.

Our Department of Commerce is now concerned with the establishment of free zones in places like Fairbanks, Alaska, and Minneapolis, Minnesota; which are scheduled to be important international ports for the air commerce following this war. Passengers and cargo from the ends of the earth will be able to land and take off in these free zones, without customs restrictions for through traffic. New markets will come to these air ports 

The world's principal market for furs is expected to be located in Fairbanks, Alaska; with a fur trading territory extending far into Siberia and China, as well as through the wildernesses of Alaska and Canada. For Fairbanks is centrally located in this vast fur trading territory, and is on the great air route between the United States of North America and the Far East, besides being a northern terminus of the Pan American Highway.

The Arctic and near-Arctic regions should have a surprising increase in population in time, because of their strategic military and trading position. Since the Northern Hemisphere comprises by far the bulk of the earth's resources and population, the advent of these air routes places these Arctic regions right in the center of most of the habitable world. The continents in the Southern 

Hemisphere taper toward the south, so the Antarctic continent cannot compare with the Arctic in this regard. 

Ever increasing air traffic over these polar areas is sure to result in the establishment of communities in desolate places, that will be interested in developing the mining and other natural resources of the area, as well as in serving as weather stations and supply centers for air travel and trade. Such communities, aided by every scientific protection from the elements, may be expected to be fit examples of the age of science into which men have entered, and to illustrate well the trend toward decentralization, within the protecting framework of large combinations of sovereign power.
