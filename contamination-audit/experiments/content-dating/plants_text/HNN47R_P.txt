Gentz might preach as valiantly as lay in his power that true statesmanship at the moment demanded settlement of remaining differences between Russia and Turkey, since a new war would bring only a new disarrangement of European power. "I defend the integrity of the Ottoman Empire," he said, "not only because I think I have plumbed the depths and the danger into which the destruction of this Power would make Europe fall, but also, and perhaps more so, because of that sense of justice which does not allow me to have 'two weights and two measures'." In other words his argument was not only based on political realism, "but perhaps more so" on political legalism. "I abhor revolutions in whatever form they present themselves, and those who protect them and those even who excuse them; and although very indulgent toward individuals," he added charitably, "I am inexorable in the judgment which I reach concerning their actions."

The trouble with such a method was this: through over-simplification it was likely to caricature reality; and it was not merely that the caricature fooled the public; it sometimes fooled Gentz himself. 

In the case in point, Gentz had started out with the assertion that the Ottoman Empire was as legitimate as any other European state; it was founded on conquest, "and conquest at all times and in all places . . . has been considered as a title of indubitable possession."52 When the Greek revolt had occurred, it should have been dealt with, therefore, exactly as any other revolt against legitimate authority. It was a question of right, and sentiment should have been allowed to play no part. Statesmen ought to have taken into account that 

Turkish vengeance and punishment "carried habitually a ferocious character." They should not have lost their heads "at the sight of the first excesses which indignation . . . and fear caused to be committed at Constantinople"; and furthermore they should not have forgotten "that these excesses, deplorable as they were, were exercised by way of reprisal against excesses still more bloody and less excusable."58 But "the incapacity, the mistakes, and the fatal disdain"54 of Capodistrias55 led to the breaking off of diplomatic relations between Russia and Turkey, and thus added one more complication to the Eastern question.56 At the date of writing, however, the Russo-Turkish squabble appeared on the verge of settlement, and in all probability one would soon have only the Greek question to worry about.57 It was on this note that

A survey of the foregoing details reveals the fact that in his dealings with these Eastern worthies Gentz served Austria by serving himself. He would traffic with almost anybody. If there were two parties he would deal with both of them. This was unscrupulous, but Gentz was an old hand and could play the game to perfection. Sometimes he was doublecrossed, of course; but he could stand it now and then if his bread was kept buttered. There can be no question that he performed useful service for Austria in the Near East. No one could state the Austrian case better than he, and he stated it in quarters which otherwise might never have heard it at all. 

He took his job seriously. He buttressed his knowledge of 

Eastern affairs by intensive study and reading over a period of many years. "I must tell you that my favorite subjects at the present time are the history and geography of the East," he was writing in 1821. "A book like the newly-appeared journey of Jaubert to Persia keeps me awake until four o'clock in the morning."66 Through the pages of his diaries is to be found a truly remarkable record of search for information, and Thornton, Gibbon, Hammer, and Schloezer are but a few of the writers on the East whose works he is to be found reading.67
