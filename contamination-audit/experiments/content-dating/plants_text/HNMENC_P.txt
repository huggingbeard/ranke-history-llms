Five hundred and thirty-three students at Texas A. & M. belong to a cooperative which furnishes them meals for $2.00 a 3

week, and rooms for $2 a month, with a total saving of $80,000 for the students yearly. The University of Washington has seen a cooperative dormitory grow from a membership of twenty-seven men and a capital of $270 to a group of 320 filling eight boarding houses; while the University of California now has three cooperative dorms, one of them the largest apartment house in Berkeley, where students receive room and board for $17.50 a month. 

The University of Wisconsin has some of the largest dining clubs, cooperatives, in the country, dining clubs in which the members themselves do the work and supply themselves with meals for about $2 a week.

Harvard has the largest campus cooperative in the country, its annual sales in its book store totalling $980,000, with savings to the students of between seven and nine per cent. Two thousand students out of a total enrollment of two thousand, five hundred are members of the cleaning-pressing cooperative at the University of North Carolina, where the savings scale close to 50%.

All this has come from the less than humble beginnings of the union of 28 weavers in Rochdale, England-weavers so poor that it took them a year to save $140. 

That was in 1844. Today, out of their experiment, has come the largest single business in all of England, another business which is the largest in Scotland, a small off-shoot in the form of a bank that dwarfs 

J. P. Morgan & Co. by some tens of millions, an idea that has spread to every continent and to forty nations of the world. 

Out of their experiment has come the college cooperative, yearly growing as its sanity of approach to the economic problem, its dividends in dollars and in training, in broadened scope and logical interests makes itself apparent. The campus is a natural location for a cooperative -thousands have seen that before. Properly foundationed, constructively managed, the cooperative becomes a real contribution to college life and its living. It is in the hopes of diagramming that foundationing and that management that this pamphlet has been written.

It should act first as a study group to learn fully what a cooperative is, its history, its principles as formulated by that first Rochdale group, its world-wide scope, the factors necessary for its success, the experience of other campus co-ops, etc. 

Cooperatives are enthusiasm-inspiring, but enthusiasm can never take the place of a sound grounding in fact. More than an economic arrangement, cooperatives are actually a concretization of a way of living, and as such must be understood. Basic Study 

This study group should decide, after study, which types of campus co-ops (dormitory, cafeteria, bookstore, laundry, general, etc.) are most apt to succeed on your campus. Important factors to be considered are the services most needed by students, the strength of existing vested interests, the attitude of administration members, the 

- 6-

facilities and capital available, the number of prospective members, state laws and regulations. If a dormitory or cafeteria seems best, investigate the advantages of the work type (where members share all the work) as contrasted with the regular shop type using hired employees. While the final decision as to the nature of the co-op should be made by all interested, the founders should be prepared to explain clearly the possibilities of two or three feasible types.

In addition to such basic, background information, a great deal of detail will have to be covered: rent on available buildings, wages for prospective employees, success of similar ventures elsewhere, probable savings as contrasted with existing arrangements, cost of city licenses, etc. If possible, faculty backing should be secured, and certainly the original committee should gradually be enlarged to include key leaders, students whose influence and contacts will be valuable and who will be able to arrange for adequate publicity through clubs, fraternities, papers, bulletin-boards, dormitory representatives, religious organizations, and other associated groups.

A Superstructure 

Then, having laid the foundation, call a meeting of those interested. Some able speaker, thoroughly informed on co-ops in general as well as campus co-ops in particular would probably prove an attraction, and a representative of a successful co-op 171

on a nearby campus would be a valuable addition to this original meeting. There should be a great deal of time allowed for questions and for thorough discussions, two meetings if necessary. It is possible to high-pressure people into co-ops, but it is hardly profitable. For this reason it is perhaps more sensible to promise less than you think you can deliver, and to allow a certain amount of time for what may seem useless, time-consuming debate.

If the support at the initial meeting is adequate, the group may elect temporary officers-president, secretary, treasurer.

If it cannot be done at the first meeting, arrange for it at some future one. Remember that it took the Rochdale weavers well over a year to get their co-op started. After the officers have been elected, instruct them to arrange a permanent organization. Select or elect a membership committee (including anyone able to interest groups which have not yet been reached or moved) to list everyone present, and to secure names of others likely to be interested. These the committee can later attempt to enroll. If new or transfer students are expected soon, their interest can be obtained by correspondence. An Education Committee should begin at once to educate the prospective members on important phases of cooperation, making literature available, arranging discussions, making personal contacts.

Dollars and Development 

Equally as important is the Finance Com- 

-8-

mittee which must make provisions for obtaining the necessary initial capital. 

Sources from which this capital may be obtained are (1) Loan capital from members. 

If this is done, the principle of "one member-one vote" still holds; (2) Loans from schools. If such loans are accepted, they inevitably weaken the autonomy of the co-op a fact which should be clearly understood before they are arranged. (3) Actual purchase or rental by the school of the equipment necessary to the cooperative. 

This has been done in two schools of which we know, but ties the co-op even more closely to the school and further weakens the co-op. This and the preceding method do have the advantage of making any deposits by the members immediately available at vacation-time, a contingency which should be planned for in any student enterprise; (4) Fees, either a lump sum on entrance (these range from 50c to $5) or a periodical fee (the range is from $1 to $5 yearly); (5) Shares refundable on withdrawal. These range from $1 in some book stores and cleaning co-ops to $15 in several dormitories. They may draw moderate interest (4-5%) if the members' purchases vary widely, as in a book store. If all the members do an equal volume of business, as in a dormitory, interest payment to members would simply increase the room costs to members by the same amount, and would be a waste of effort and bookkeeping. 

We strongly recommend share capital in preference to the other methods of financ- 9- 

---

ing the co-op, for it increases the members' sense of ownership and interest in the organization's welfare, it decreases the tendency to regard the management as a clique profiting at the expense of the other members, and comes about as close to true Rochdale principles as can be expected in a campus co-op. Since all accounts are paid in advance or upon delivery, the funds thus obtained can, of course, be used immediately as working capital.

A Safe-Guard 

If investments or contractual commitments are necessary, the founders should guard themselves by requiring a deposit from prospective members as a guarantee of real interest. All officials entrusted with funds should be subject to audit, should keep simple yet accurate books, and, if large sums are involved, should be bonded. 

Above all, the cooperative's credit should be good. That means prompt payment of bills and the policy of "no-credit."

Having set up the skeleton of the organization, the temporary officers and the various committees, the next step is the election of the Board of Directors and the manager. 

The method to be used is naturally dependent upon the preferences of the organization, but it should be remembered that the manager is the hub around which your cooperative is going to center. He should be, not only a good, efficient business man, but also popular, tactful, a good organizer 

-10-

thoroughly imbued with the cooperative spirit and able to realize its potentialities. 

In the matters affecting the personal interest of the manager (e.g., rate per hour, number of hours to be spent), the final decision should rest with some authority who will not personally gain or lose by the transaction. Until the manager is tested, the Board might well set a sum as the maximum to be spent by him without the Board's express approval, and good permanent policy requires a periodical check on the manager. A maximum for the inventory should be set as soon as practicable, and some board member should familiarize himself with methods of interpreting financial statements.

The size and nature of the enterprise will determine whether the manager should be salaried or voluntary, and whether executive responsibility should be vested in one person or in a committee. Secure, if possible, an experienced executive, one who knows how to set up an efficient organization, who will see that each official understands exactly what his duties are, and who will hold that official responsible for proper execution. If the co-op is large enough to warrant functional division, activities should be grouped in logical departments and co-ordinated in their activities. 

A Few More

Key persons must be interested in the success of the venture, not merely to save 11- 

---

money, to create a job, or to make a reputation, but rather because they see the larger implications of the cooperative movement. 

The promoters must be prepared to give much time to the co-op, especially at first, and should be sufficiently good students to be able to afford the time. They should be understudied by lower classmen who will be able to assume full responsibility when an official graduates, and all employees or officials should realize that they are on probation for a time, and may be removed by decision of the Board. 

If you have decided upon a work cooperative, the distribution of duties should to some extent be arranged to secure valuable training for the members as well as efficient operation. Thus a shy man can be given a position in which people seek him out. If not overdone, this policy can greatly increase the value of the cooperative. When the cooperative opens, the manager can set policies governing many frequently recurring types of transactions, thus making them routine. Prepare an operating manual, listing the duties of each position, summarizing all permanent policies, and containing the constitution and by-laws with a statement of the objectives of the organization.

The policies of the co-op should be subject to frequent review in order to make the best use of experience obtained. Shape the policy as you go along; do not attempt to take over the detailed arrangements of some other co-op, however successful, but -12-

solve what you will find are peculiar problems in your own way.

A student group changes rapidly, and care must be taken to provide for a continuity of policy and service. Moreover, the co-op where outsiders hear one man constantly referred to is apt to be a weak co-op. Cooperation means working together; working under someone necessarily implies sub-operation, and that is death to the cooperative principle. Begin on a relatively small scale, working your problems out in miniature. You can always expand later. In setting up offices, balance the efficiency obtained by centralization and permanence against the values of cooperative sharing of duties and of training more men obtainable under decentralization and revolving management. 

A campus cooperative exists to save money and to serve its members in broader ways. 

While "cooperation" as a watch-word should never be allowed to destroy efficiency (cooperatives are a means to an end, not an excuse for a failure to realize that end), nevertheless, even for self-preservation, no cooperative dares rest content with money savings alone. There are almost unlimited possibilities for the training of co-op members-especially in a work cooperative-in democracy, cooperative attitudes, socialized behavior, and personal development. The cooperative fails in its largest purpose if it neglects these.

Watch Your Step 

As soon as a strong nucleus has been built, efforts can be made to expand. A buying club or a book store can be organized within a dormitory co-op, or a pressing and cleaning co-op may extend its services to the sale of clothes. BUT never become too ambitious. It is far better to start small and build solidly than to start large and fall solidly.

Part of that building solidly comes to nothing more than attention to detail, dull, relatively dry, seemingly unimportant detail. If, however, there were a stronger word than essential, we would be forced to apply it to that detail. If you dislike the microscopic, do not bother to read the rest of this pamphlet, for this, the final half, will concern itself with the minutiae of the movement, the technical details of operation and continuance as developed by the combined experience of the 160 cooperatives now in existence on campuses. 

Education will necessarily prove a large part of your co-op, but here we mean education in the root sense of the word, education as the preparation for living, not the all too frequent perversion which accepts the man with the crowded note-book as the man with training. In all educational activity avoid prima donnas, giving every member an opportunity to contribute. 

In most cases the group should not organize until preliminary discussions have 

-16-

been held, acquainting at least all prospective leaders with both wide and detailed aspects of the proposed undertaking. But even after this, the necessity remains to train new leaders, to enlist greater loyalty among the members by explaining the outreaches of the organization, to iron out the problems of the group, to consider expansion, to do the whole multitude of things basic to the cooperative. 

An Example The Kimbark Cooperative Dining Club opened on October 21, 1935, and began a formal education program after two months' operations had clearly shown the need for it. An education chairman was chosen and put in charge of an appropriation of onehalf per cent of the total, gross income. 

The program included prominent speakers, discussion groups, and membership meetings, but most of the appropriation went for literature. Most of the available pamphlets, periodicals and books were provided for the library, and free copies of a few especially good pamphlets were furnished all members. 

The very rapid membership turnover necessitated the preparation of a manual to describe duties of the various assignments, and to list the important facts necessary to intelligent membership. This saved time for the officers, and systematically explained the arrangements to the newcomers. -17- 

---

Probably the most effective activity lay in reserving a particular table for a discussion group.

By agreement, all remarks at this table during a given meal were directed toward some topic bearing on cooperation. While some groups asked a well-informed individual to describe a topic, the more successful plan lay in discussion by the group of a current problem of the co-op. Very often these problems called for facts, and a more natural and spontaneous delving into cooperative history, for example, resulted when a group began by considering the attitude of the co-op toward a proposed peace strike than when Cooperative History was the topic. The group, finding itself in need of knowing precedents, turned to history to learn what those precedents were.

A Result 

These discussion groups proved useful to officers as a sort of sounding board or sampling of the attitudes of members. 

When a new policy was to be formulated, a representative group was asked to discuss it, an officer presented the problem, and sat back to listen. Often new ideas and points of view emerged which needed consideration by the officers. After the proposal had been reworked in the light of such comments, it was presented to the whole group during a meal. The president gave a carefully worked-out statement of the facts, and, if controversy were expected, he also listed the arguments on each side. -18-

If the issue were sufficiently important, a mimeographed statement was given each member at the beginning of a meal, and each table was asked to discuss it. After a few minutes, the president had all tables report any new suggestion. The matter was laid on the table for 24 hours, during which time members continued to discuss it, and by the time the question was put, no member had any excuse for being uninformed, the matter had been thoroughly discussed, and the group judgment was intelligently rendered. This procedure developed as others failed, and proved thoroughly satisfactory.

This method is, in truth, the method of cooperative discussion; discussion, not to win a point, but to reach a common agreement. It is characteristic of the age that even conversation usually turns to competition, characteristic of cooperatives that even conversation is based on the ideal of harmony. The same technique may profitably be used in regular meetings. 

If you know in advance that important matters are to be presented, try to give publicity to relevant facts in order to permit discussion that can clear the air before the business meeting. Have discussions in the meetings, Robert's Rules of Order protest ing ineffectually, before you accept a motion. When several persons argue for one side, as soon as repetition comes into their arguments, summarize their position and ask for a spokesman for the opposition. If none appears, ask for a motion. If both 

-19-

sides are presented, try to get agreement as to facts, eliminating misunderstandings due to terminology, summarizing whenever agreement appears, and making clear the exact points of controversy. Make it evident that you wish full expression of all attitudes; even go so far as to ask questions in order to help someone "get it out of his system." Try to get agreement as to the course of action; then the motion will come as an expression of the will of the group, a summarization of the discussion.

A word of caution: never overdo your educational efforts. Students resent too much preaching, and forced feeding will result in indigestion or rebellion, if not both.

A necessary part of your educational program, however, will be the education of the faculty. While the faculty must never be considered as opponents to be vanquished, even those most seriously and sincerely interested in your welfare may protest the proposal of a cooperative. Perhaps it is because they are interested that they will protest. Evaluate their criticism; if 

-20-

they are right, readjust your plans accordingly; if they are wrong, convince them of their error. "Your group is incompetent to handle this project," they may say. Perhaps they are right. Have you sufficient upper-class backing and adequate leadership; have you a membership skilled in business management and capable of handling student groups? It may be possible to recast your plans or attract more students. Perhaps there are insuperable difficulties about which they can warn you; perhaps there is no need or no room for a co-op on your campus, and you would be foolish to attempt to force one. Of course, if a fair analysis of their objections leaves you sure that the project is feasible, try to get a faculty sponsor who can convince the rest of the faculty. Take their objections one by one, and do what you can to eliminate the cause. Perhaps if you ask them to solve your problem, they will agree that your solution is best. But never antagonize the faculty. Frankly, they can do too much to hamper you.

Stay With Them 

Perhaps the objection will be that a coop would reduce the school's revenue. Face this objection squarely. If you deprive your school of revenue, it will be compelled to curtail services, or to raise fees. 

If you reduce your room-rent, but increase your tuition, you have accomplished nothing. Be 

21-

very careful about attempting to divert much revenue from your college unless it is extraordinarily wealthy.

From a conservative member of the faculty may come, "Cooperatives are too radical." Find another conservative faculty member, board member, or business man who believes in cooperatives, and whose backing will be sufficient, or find another school known to be conservative, whose campus has a co-op about which its faculty is enthusiastic, and which can be used as a precedent. Perhaps you simply need another name, or will have to carry on no propaganda work. In this latter case, your educational program will simply put more emphasis on providing activities where people learn to work together than on promotional effort. A slight shift in the composition of your group, the elimination of notorious agitators, the addition of conservatives-these may solve the problem. 

Or the solution may lie in thorough explanation of the cooperative principles. In more than one school the students have had to teach the teachers about them. 

Be sure to make previous arrangements if your school property, commercially used, can be taxed, and thus obviate this objection. 

There Are Answers 

Against all of these objections, ask the faculty to place these values: (1) The reduction of expenses enabling more students to attend. At Texas A. & M., in four years, 22- 

---

co-ops have helped 1000 men, who would otherwise have been forced to give up school, remain in it. (2) Cooperative membership is often considered a privilege, and is granted only to those earning it by superior work and a wholesome attitude. (3) Work cooperatives quickly prove good men and detect weaklings, helping the latter to correct their faults. (4) Cooperatives give students training in business, administration, leadership and hard work that will stand them in good stead when they apply for jobs. (5) Students develop initiative, self-confidence, and social imagination. (6)

When students understand the difficulties in a situation or control it themselves, they do less complaining. Many a faculty harassed by complaints about poor food, for instance, could turn the problem over to students who would straightway learn that the responsibility rests on themselves, and that, if they want better service, they must arrange it themselves. (7) Students learn to work with others, to allow for their short-comings and idiosyncracies, and become more tolerant and companionable. (8) "Education is proportionate to participation." The whole trend of the modern psychology in education is to emphasize the value of purposive activity as contrasted with mere memorization, and co-ops fit in this trend beautifully. At all times present the case to them from their point of view, stressing, not what you gain, but what they gain.

Legal Legerdemain 

For instance, a word of warning might be given about the charter. Without a state charter, the organization will have no legal status, except that it can be considered a de facto partnership, in which all participants have made themselves liable for the debts of the group. Then any creditor to whom the cooperative owed $100 could sue, and be entitled to a claim against any member of the group for the entire $100 if he were not paid by the group. This becomes important only in the case of suit or of bankruptcy.

A legal organization which will become embarrassing through the rapid shift in membership typical of student groups should be avoided. For example, one group using a modified Massachusetts trust arrangement for a number of years, now finds that every one of its 70,000 one-time members is still a full member, and must approve every change in its structure or 

24-

any sale of its property. It should also be made impossible for the capital accumulated as reserves, depreciation, etc., to be divided for the benefit of members at any time. 

Provision should be made for some worthy disposal of any capital amassed by the group in case of dissolution. 

Sales taxes, special licenses by the city, county or state, property tax, income tax, etc., may be incurred by the cooperative, and should be investigated and understood before the cooperative begins business.

Again, if school property is used, be sure to investigate the effect that may have on tax-exemptions.

Check sanitary regulation governing such matters as personal cleanliness and physical examination of workers to guard against contagion, the proper care of food, beverages, and refuse.

Your Protection 

In a work cooperative, members might consider a provision absolving the group from responsibility for accidents occurring in the line of duty, or may provide insurance against such cases. Insurance should also be provided for inventories, other property, and any person entrusted with major sums should be bonded.

After the cooperative has been set up, there is still the matter of bookkeeping to be considered. Necessarily each set of books must fit the particular situation, and none generally applicable can be given. 

-25-

Get someone with accounting training, perhaps a student in the local Commerce and 

Finance school, to set up your books. The simplest set which will give you the information you need is best. Every transaction should be so recorded that an outside auditor could understand without asking the recorder. Each transaction should be so arranged as to minimize the possibility of dishonesty and also to prevent its being overlooked and not recorded. Comply with school regulations, if any, governing student groups' audits. Periodic statements, intelligible to members, should be publicized, preferably showing contrasts with comparable previous periods. Do not omit bills not yet due, or any other item in order to make a better showing-you fool no one, including yourself, and you make matters worse during the next period. If feasible, make your books analytical as well as historical, this in order to reveal operating shortcomings and financial dangers. A manual describing the books saves enough time when bookkeepers are changed to justify its preparation. If members share equally, as in a dormitory or dining club, dividing the total expenses for a period by the number of members may be all that is necessary for the books, but if the individual bills vary, a more elaborate set will be necessary. In a store where purchases are irregular, the amount of each purchase is often recorded on a slip which a member signs and deposits in a suitable receptacle. 

At the end of the period, slips for each 

-26-

member are totalled, and the dividends are computed on the patronage.

Demonstrable Democracy 

Finally, a word about the conduct of the cooperative SO far as its meetings go. 

Rochdale principles insist that the co-op be democratically controlled, but cooperators sometimes misinterpret the word "democracy" to mean that every member must be consulted on every detail of every transaction-a complete travesty on true democracy. Democracy requires that each person shall be fully informed of all important matters, shall have the right to express his opinion, and shall be consulted on all major policies. But the very purpose of choosing a few representatives as a Board and as managers is to shield the members from the barrage of unimportant details which constantly arise, and under which real democracy can be smothered. Democracy means that the management must realize that its function is to operate for the service of the group, and not to line its own pockets, or add to its own glory. It means that the management will attempt to discover the member's point of view, and will not allow rifts to appear between itself and the member. Its preservation is likeliest when all factions are always given an opportunity to participate in the setting of policies. Its preservation is guarded when meetings are frequent, when management positions are not too lucrative, when the officials do not have too much patron- 

-27-

age. If salaries are large or appointive "plums" many, campus politics are bound to enter. A safe rule is that if there is eager campaigning for the leadership positions, they offer too much, and that the welfare of the organization might be better served if inducements were reduced to a point where "the office seeks the man, and not the man the office."

A Salute 

Good luck, not just because you are on the trail of something that will mean savings to you and other students in your university, but because you have enough foresight to be in on the birth of a worldshaking revolution. Co-ops have revolutionized life in Sweden, Denmark and Norway, and they have left an indelible mark on England, Scotland, Ireland, France, Spain, Germany, Italy, and Russia. Yesterday and today they began to move throughout the United States, and their movement will far transcend any small or large cooperative you may be able to erect. This is the age of the consumer; from this point forward, the consumer counts. And when the con- 

-28-

sumer counts, a great many things are going to be changed.

It is easy to become excited about cooperatives, to vision them as near-panaceas. 

They affect the racial problem, for one of the Rochdale principles is that no one shall be refused admission to a co-op on grounds of race, color, or creed. Negroes at the

University of Illinois could buy their meals at only one restaurant. When that restaurant closed recently, and Negroes had no place in which to eat, Jews and gentiles combined to start a cooperative restaurant at which all races were welcomed. The largest Negro store in America was built in the last four years by the cooperative technique in Gary, Indiana.

They improve labor conditions, for one of their primary foundation pieces is the fair treatment of labor in the matter of hours, conditions, and wages. But more, in labor's basic role of consumer, they give him breaks never dreamed of in our present set-up. 

You're Going Somewhere

The whole cooperative movement is radical, not in its means, but in its approach to the problems that beset the world.

What it does, in effect, is reduce the world, not to an equality, but to a common denominator. Refusing to consider one man as capitalist and another as laborer and another, crushed between, as consumer, the cooperatives move toward a more sane 

-29-

method of living. That is one great reason for the failure, or partial failure, of producers' cooperatives in this country. They have improved the condition of the producer, but it is the producer against the consumer, and injustices follow. Consumers cooperatives are coming ever closer to proving that good sense and common justice also total to good economics. 

And so, because you are part of a great world movement for the restoration of balance to a world off balance, because you are training yourself and your fellow students for a more logical living in this world, because you are doing something immediate and practical, our hat is off to you, and our hand is out to you. If there is anything that we can do, let us know.
