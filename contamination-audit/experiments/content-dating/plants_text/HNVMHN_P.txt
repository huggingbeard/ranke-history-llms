The "Odyssey of a Flockmaster" A Story of The 1934 Drought 

A number of years ago a famed American humorist was said to have facetiously remarked that the weather was a prime subject of conversation but no one ever undertook to do anything about it. 

However, that was before science and inventive genius had wrought its wonders, and before a megalomaniacal government stepped right out and told you how it proposed to change nature and solve all the evils that man is heir to. 

The work of science referred to is the control, in a small way, of the extremes of heat and cold. 

For a long time in well-built houses we have been able to provide sufficient heat to make us comfortable, no matter how cold outside. And now with modern refrigeration and air-conditioning it is possible to provide in our homes and offices a healthy and comfortable atmosphere even in the hottest of climes. The efforts of the government to do the highly sensational thing in changing climate and affecting rainfall by building a nation-wide windbreak is our reference to the megalomaniacal government now in power. But to change nature or rainfall on an extensive scale is, and will continue to be, impossible. In parts of the globe there is ample precipitation always, and favorable climate. 

In others there is lack of one or both. 

Climate-that is, temperature, air velocity and precipitation-has profound influence on human activity and well being. As a result we find the large populations where climate is favorable to life. It takes heat, soil and water to produce plant and animal growth. Plant growth is not subject to much flexibility as to climate, but animal growth has a wide latitude. Thus, we find people and animals inhabiting areas which are only fairly well adapted to their uses. As populations increase and become congested in the more favorable locations, 3

they drift out into the less favorable spots and attempt to adapt themselves to the natural conditions existing there. In those places there may be a lack of moisture, a dearth of heat, or barrenness of soil. In New Mexico and the other states of the 

Great American Desert the chief natural deficiency is rainfall. There is a lot of good soil and for most plant life there is sufficient heat. But the water is always a question mark outside of the irrigated districts. The Weather Bureau has records of many years now, showing temperature, rainfall and wind velocity in all parts of the country. The agricultural colleges and those interested in farming now know about how much rainfall is actually needed to produce a crop. They say in this latitude that from 16 inches to 20 inches of rain will assure raising of various agricultural products. But the records show that that amount of rainfall comes only rarely. Therefore agriculture in this far west is a hazardous occupation and life itself is hazardous. Those engaged in agriculture outside of the irrigated or mountainous areas, and those engaged in livestock raising outside of certain favorable localities are running extreme risks, with the chances all against them. A bad year like last year, or several fairly lean years will break the best. Other occupations in this state are less hazardous as the risks are partially controllable and can be spread over a variety of economic activities. But agriculture and livestock raising are extremely risky.

The writer came to New Mexico 33 years ago with the idea of going into the livestock business.

He had heard of the romance of the west; of those vast ranches of hundreds of thousands of acres; of the enchanted life of the noble cowboy and the romantic existence of the lonely sheep herder. I was in ill health, sick of civilization, and in a very receptive mood for this song of the range. I chose the life of the sheep herder and worked a year at it for my board. Then I borrowed some money and, in the fall of the year, bought an interest in an outfit. In the early summer there had been copious rains and the grass was good and the sheep fat. I did not know then that I was starting into the worst year there had been here in 50.

From June of 1903 to August of 1904-14 months 

-there was not a half inch of moisture in the Estancia Valley. Practically no lambs were raised and many of the sheep died. At that time, however, there was free government range and we could drift anywhere we wished. So, when it began to rain over in the Pecos Valley country we drifted over there. In August of 1904 it started to rain quite generally and in September of that year one day six inches of rain fell at Moriarty. 

1905 was an exceptionally good year and 1906 was fair. 1907 was bad again and I decided then to quit the livestock business for all time as being too hazardous. Soon I went into the mercantile business and have been in it ever since. The firm 

I am now connected with has had livestock on the side for the past 50 years. For the last 15 years 

I have had general supervision of their livestock interests, though devoting most of my time and energy to the mercantile business. 

The jar I got in the awful drought of 1903-04 and again in 1907-08 when I decided to quit, and the other and frequent bad years when the success of my mercantile business depended so much on whether it rained or not on my customers, has made this question of weather and rainfall more than of academic interest. It has become a life or death matter to a large part of the population of the state.

I believe that last year, 1934, was the worst year in history for the livestock and agricultural business of the west. It was the worst for livestock because it was so widespread. From the Rio Grande 

River on the west to a point near Wichita, Kansas, on the east, there was a belt extending from the Canadian border to Mexico which was practically absolutely arid throughout the summer of 1934. 

Ranges were already stocked to capacity and when 

June passed without rain and July came, livestock men became alarmed. But when July gave no rain-July, the wet month-there was consternation and the Federal Government was called on for aid. 

It embarked on a program of supplying feed and of buying the least desirable classes of cattle and old sheep. But for many that didn't solve the

problem and it solved none for my company. The story of our problem and the experiences the writer had in connection therewith may be of interest, and with your permission I shall recite a portion of them.

As I stated above, when July passed and there was no rain, there was consternation. We were not so greatly concerned over the sheep we had under our care, as we were over those of our customers and especially over those which we had out on shares. We try not to overstock, ever, and as a result were fairly well provided for. But our customers who owed us heavy accounts were not so well fixed. Likewise most of them who had sheep on shares had nothing to fall back on. So when the 10th of August came with no rain, we knew what we were up against. We had sheep on shares with about 40 different persons. Many notified us that they desired to deliver them to us as they had no grass. And most of the rest of them were requested to turn theirs back to us so we could care for them. But what to do with them, where to go, was the problem. The range country, even before the drought, had plenty of livestock and the big farm country west of the Missouri 

River was burned up. On the 15th of August I started out and was gone six weeks on my first trip in search of range. I went west-to western 

Arizona, California and along the Mexican border.

I went into Mexico at various points but always a prospect turned out to be an impossibility. Arizona offered hope of placing a few herds and after we had arranged for about 10,000 head the Sheep 

Sanitary Board over there, without warning, passed a regulation practically prohibiting the importation of sheep. Only a threat of taking the matter into a Federal Court and the assistance of some good friends down there caused the Board to lift the ban. 

When I returned home after locating places for about 10,000 head in Arizona, I found that we had 46,000 more to locate. Winter would soon be here and to feed them here was impossible. Likewise feed of every kind everywhere was high. For instance, in Nebraska, where we sometimes feed,

alfalfa had jumpd from $4.00 per ton in 1933 to $16.00 a ton in 1934. Corn had advanced from 15 cents a bushel in 1933 to $1.00 a bushel in 1934. 

In the Panhandle of Texas where we often pasture on green wheat there was no wheat. So whichever way one turned he was up against a stone wall. We had not had a hard winter for a number of years and one was about due. I had visions of snow banks as I had seen them in the past and I almost despaired. In this condition I again turned towards Mexico. There was little time left and we had to hurry. I sent a couple of men to certain places and I went to others. But always something was wrong. The country would be inaccessable or shy of water. All the time we were trying also to bust the red tape of Mexican official regulations. We had been told of the difficulties down there and the necessity of taking our own herders in. But they wouldn't agree to let our men in, no workmen-only the owner of the stock. 

One might go in as a tourist but not as a laborer. 

So we abandoned the idea of taking our men in. 

After failing to find any suitable range in the border states of Chihuahua and Sonora, I decided to investigate the interior, although I was warned against getting so far from the line. Revolutionary talk was constant down there and bandits regularly made their pillaging forays. But the thought of winter coming on up here with its possible ice and snow made us decide to risk an uncertain government rather than a capricious nature. An acquaintance of mine in El Paso, who knows Mexico well, told me of some range we could secure in the State of Durango, about 550 miles below the border. I flew down in an aeroplane, met the man at Torreon, in the State of Coahulia, and together we drove to the range in Central Durango, a distance of 110 miles from Torreon. On that trip I got my first taste of Mexican roads. With a good Buick car and a good driver we made the distance in seven hours. I had already had intestinal trouble from eating certain Mexican foods and when we reached the end of that 110 mile trip I was done and had to go to bed. At that ranch there was a deaf and dumb woman who cooked and kept the house of the master. Noting my distress she motioned that

she would fix me, and soon brought me a cup of some kind of herb which did not taste badly and was real efficacious, for the next morning I was all right. I went to that ranch many times later and the little, old, afflicted Mexican woman treated me royally and seemed highly grateful for the thanks I motioned out. 

The day following our arrival at this ranch, named La Zarca, we drove around and inspected the place. It is 20 miles wide and about 60 miles long, containing 300,000 hektares of land, or about 750,000 acres. The grass was fine and there was an abundance of water. At one time, before the revolutions, it had supported 60,000 sheep and 10,- 000 cattle. All of this stock was stolen and driven off and only now, 20 years later, are the owners getting back on their feet. Now they have 10,000 cattle and about the same number of sheep. The owners of the ranch are named Veyan Bros., being half French and half Mexican. They turned out to be highly respectable men and it was a pleasure to do business with them. I quickly made a deal with them, drove back over the terrible road to Torreon, flew to El Paso, and hurried north to start the first trainload of sheep. We shipped them to the El Paso Stock Yards where they were unloaded for rest and feed, and for inspection for exportation. The inspection and other regulations were very rigid and I did not know what to expect in the way of railroad service down there on the 

Mexican National Railways. When we finally got across the international bridge at Juarez and the comparatively small engine started south with the string of double-deck Santa Fe cars and I realized the serious consequences that would follow if that engine failed or the track gave way, as well as all of the uncertainty and danger of the undertaking anyway, I questioned my judgment as to whether, after all, it might not have been better to have risked a snowbank with no grass in New

Mexico to warmth and rich pasture in Old Mexico. 

But it was too late to turn back so naturally I went on. But the rocky roadbed, the hard benches of the caboose (no cushions at all), the desolate country we crossed and the bleating of the poor sheep which seemed to cry out against being de-

ported, added to the misery of the situation I found myself in. However, the engine held together, the rails of the track stood up and we pounded along southward. We reached Chihuahua about nine o'clock at night. There I was able to get some Mexican food and some information. They said we would reach the next division point, Jimenez, at 4:00 A. M. and our destination, Ceballos, about 9:00 in the morning. I was pretty tired when I climbed back into the new caboose and feeling assured that we would make it I lay down on the hard bench and was rattled to sleep. Near noon the next morning we had reached Ceballos without accident, had unloaded the sheep and counted them out to Veyan Bros. They had their men and their burros and as they started those several herds out into the brush on a ninety mile trip through a rough country I had another case of the jitters and again held my breath until it was over. It was impossible to follow them by auto; even almost impossible to go through with a wagon. I went down the railroad with Rodolfo

Veyan to Torreon and after waiting about three days we drove over the rough road again to the ranch to go out and meet the sheep from the upper side. La Zarca Ranch is on a high plateau while the railroad where we unloaded is in the lowest part and poorest part of the country. The chaparral brush of the lower country is hard to penetrate. So we didn't find the sheep where expected. 

We finally reached them and learned of the extreme difficulty they were having getting through. 

I told Veyan Bros. I would risk no more sheep on that route and they then said by taking a branch line of the railroad out of Jimenez we could ship to Rosario which would bring us into the high country and thus avoid the trouble we were having. 

So after helping with the sheep for half a day we drove to Rosario to look over the route from that point. Then we drove back, expecting to meet the sheep at a certain watering place or farther on. 

But they had not arrived there and after we drove several miles we came to the front herd. They were stalled in the thick brush, had been four days without water and the men were about done, with their clothing torn to shreds by the thick gatuna (cats claws) brush. Four herds were fairly close

together with the fifth one several miles in the rear. At this point the trail was narrow and rocky and the brush was fierce. We tried to drive the front herd but in that awful brush they would scarcely move. Since mid-August I had had many shocks and always the haunting fear of approaching winter, with its attendant dangers. But here stark disaster seemed imminent. A reputation of thirty years standing seemed at stake. We were stopped dead in our tracks. The Mexicans didn't know what to do and I didn't know what to do.

It looked like we would lose them all. Finally I recalled how we handle sheep in thickly settled farming countries where we have to take them down a lane. A man cuts off a small bunch of ten or twelve and drives them down the road, bleating a little as he goes. The sheep behind, hearing the call and seeing the small bunch leaving, will follow. The herder's job then is to keep those behind from joining those ahead, merely keeping them coming. The whole herd will follow. 

I tried that on that narrow, rocky trail, and it worked. I directed the Mexicans not to break the line once they got started. They went almost in single file, it was so rough and narrow. We were going up a long ascent and as I reached the top and turned over the leaders to a Mexican to drive along I got on a high point and looked down that trail. I had never seen anything like it before and never expect to again. For, from that vantage point, I saw a solid string of sheep about four miles in length winding in and out up that tortuous path. As they came over the crest and out into a more open country we had to bunch them before going to the water on the other side. As it was they piled up quite badly as they plunged into the little stream coming from three springs set down in the heart of the desert-like country. There they watered and rested and then grazed into the night, to return the next mid-day to water again before starting on the last lap, up one more ascent to the open plateau of the La Zarca ranch. This promised land was reached two days later and I breathed an audible sigh of relief as I saw the sheep fill up on good pasture and lie down and rest in contentment. 10

That was a tough trip for the sheep and the men. It gave me an opportunity to see how the 

Mexicans down there work. I was favorably impressed. The herders are on an entirely different basis than they are here in the United States. 

Where we have wagons and tents and all kinds of cooking utensils and food, down there they have no wagons, no tents, merely a couple of earthenware vessels for their food and coffee, and a small one-gallon jug in which to carry water. They work for one peso a day, amounting to twenty-eight cents in American money, or about eight and onehalf dollars a month. They furnish their own food, consisting of beans, corn meal tortillas, which åre made at their homes and carried out to the herders in a sack, and coffee, and some meat if they have it at home. The sheep are counted almost daily and the herder is required to pay for any loss in the sheep unless he procures the body to show that the animal has died. The labor laws down there, however, are rather unfavorable to the employer, as he cannot discharge a man for incompetency and is required to pay an established minimum wage irrespective of the man's ability. 

However, most of the herders are able and I saw some of the best herding done that ever came to my attention.

After getting this first shipment located I wired to our men here to get ready another trainload as it was now late November. I went back to Torreon with Sr. Veyan to go north. There I called on a Mr. Garcia, Superintendent of the National 

Railways, to talk with him about the next shipment which was to go out on the branch line from Jimenez to Rosario. It was a stiff grade and the steel is lighter, so Mr. Garcia said he could not put their heavy power on that line, and, that the power used might not be sufficient to take a trainload up that long steep grade. This was very disconcerting for it might mean a lot of delay which we could not afford. Livestock must be moved expeditiously, especially down there where there are no feeding facilities along the line. I pointed this out to Mr. Garcia and suggested he might use two engines, etc. He was extremely courteous but would promise nothing except they would do the 11

best they could. What that meant I learned on the next shipment down. 

On that trip to Torreon our contract with Veyan & Company was prepared. Reference to it will be made later. It was quite long and it was barely finished in time to catch the plane to El Paso. 

We phoned to the airport and learned that the plane was a trifle late. They promised to wait, but it was quite a distance, and driving as fast as the taxi could we reached the edge of the landing field as the plane took off. Foolishly and franticly I tried to wave to them to come back. It reached 

El Paso in about six hours. On the passenger train 

I reached there thirty hours later. 

I secured my car in El Paso and hurried north to Corona. Ran into heavy snow drifts near Corona. Thus, at this early date-about the 25th of 

November-my fears of heavy snows seemed realized. Two days later we had assembled the second shipment from three different points and I met it at El Paso. When we were ready to cross the line I wired Superintendent Garcia at Torreon to help me get through in twenty-four hours, if possible, so we could get the sheep unloaded at Rosario and out on feed. In the United States, usually a full train crew consists of two men on the engine, two brakemen and the conductor, or five in all.

In Mexico, on the National Railways, they have three brakemen in addition to the conductor and engine crew. Also usually a boy or young man gets on a peon-who prepares the food and does chores on the caboose. In the work of getting the sheep loaded and across I forgot the date until one of the men in the caboose pointed to a full page picture in an El Paso paper I was reading, which was a picture of a bountiful Thanksgiving dinner. 

I explained it to them, this "el dia gracia," or 

Thanksgiving day in the tSates. It seemed like a cheerless Thanksgiving day to me, until in the middle of the afternoon I was invited back to the other end of the caboose where I was treated to a meal that tasted as good as any Thanksgiving dinner I have ever eaten. By now I had become accustomed to Mexican dishes and relished many of them, and in my real hunger at the moment I 12

enjoyed that plain, highly-seasoned meal to the utmost.

That afternoon and night and until noon the next day, I had an exhibition of railroading which I have told to railroad officials in this country, at which narration they were amazed. We had the full crew of six mentioned above, and the cook, as far as Chihuahua, which is 227 miles south of Juarez. After changing crews at that division point I noticed an extra man in the caboose. I enquired and learned that he was a traveling engineer, an expert that all railroads have who now and then travels along with the regular engineers to observe how they handle the trains and possibly to instruct them if they seem to need it. He told me he had orders to go through with our train on account of a new engine they would put on at the next division point, Jimenez, to pull the train up that grade to our destination at Rosario. I thought it was very fine service that my friend, Garcia, the Superintendent, was giving us. I learned from this engineer that they had sent a reconditioned engine all the way from Auguas Calientes, a distance of about 750 miles, especially for the haul from Jimenez to Rosario. That was more service and was 

Garcia's answer to my suggestion that he put on two engines. He had furnished an engine which could handle the job alone. When we reached Jimenez in the wee small hours and they were switching around and fixing one of our cars out of which they had pulled a draw-head, I noticed a rather officious gentleman who seemed to be taking charge and who gave orders with an army-like snappiness. When we finally pulled out I found him on the caboose. I enquired in Spanish what his job was and in good English he told me he was the trainmaster of the road. His name is Pescador, which being translated into English is Fisherman. He said that Superintendent Garcia requested that he go with the train to Rosario to see that everything was kept on the move. Now with the extra engineer and the trainmaster we had a crew of eight, which was what I call service. But when daylight came I noticed a well painted car ahead of the caboose and when we stopped I read on the side the words "Division Engineer in Charge 13

of Construction and Maintenance." The door opened and a fine looking high-type Mexican in civil engineering clothes invited me up. He had a fully equipped car with a crew of men. After exchanging pleasantries in Spanish he swung over into English and answering my query stated that he was going through with us to fix us up if anything happened. His crew brought our complement up to twelve. So we were going strong up that grade. If the train paused for a moment the trainmaster would run up ahead and raise "Ned." He cursed both in Spanish and English-in Spanish for the benefit of the train crew and in English for my edification, and to show me, perhaps, how they railroad in Mexico. Our train was not too long and in order to avoid the necessity of stopping for water they hooked a tank car on back of the engine. And in order to avoid stopping to take on coal they hooked a coal gondola on back of the water car. And, to get the coal from the gondola to the engine they put four or five peons on the coal car with boxes which they filled and carried on their backs up along the water car and dumped the coal into the tender of the engine, the while the fireman shoveled it into the firebox and the engineer blew it out through the smoke stack. And as we raced along up the mountain slopes fire flew from the engine and ignited the dry grasses and leaves and we left a cloud of heavy blue smoke behind us. But the final grade was too steep for even that special engine and after making a couple of runs at it they cut the train in two. I got on the front section and they ran into 

Rosario, a distance of about fifteen miles. While we were unloading that portion of the train the engine went back and got the balance of the cars. 

Thus we arrived at Rosario about 25 hours after leaving Juarez, in time to unload the sheep and get them out before dark. My friend Garcia had furnished a good engine and a crew of sixteen men and had made good to the utmost his promise of doing the best they could. A little later when I returned to the States my disgust knew no bounds when I learned that the Santa Fe Railroad in transporting some other of our sheep a slightly lesser distance had made us unload twice, although we had signed a 36-hour release. In other 14

words, to make practically the same distance they required about 72 hours as compared with 25 on the Mexican National. A few days after reaching 

Rosario I again went to Torreon and expressed to 

Superintendent Garcia, in person, the appreciation we had of the splendid service given us. 

The moving of the sheep from Rosario to the 

La Zarca Ranch, a distance of from 75 to 100 miles, was comparatively easy, as the country was smooth, well grassed and well watered. We went through several municipios, including the town and ranch of Canutillo, which was Francisco Villa's place until his death. Once on the ranch the sheep did well and we looked forward to a successful winter. I made several trips down there, all of which were plenty hard and some quite eventful. 

However, as spring approached we saw that the dry weather had extended into Mexico and stayed with us. As the months went by and it continued dry, we were compelled to move back into a still higher country which also finally became dry except there was plenty of water in the rivers. In 

May when conditions had greatly improved in New Mexico, while continuing worse down there, we began to prepare to return. I checked into the regulations at the border and found them worse than were the Mexican regulations going in. I refer mainly to the Sanitary regulations. These required that sheep coming out must come from a clean area—that is, one where there was no scabies-which area must have been clean for at least six months according to a certificate from the Mexican authorities; that they must pass very close inspection at the border by United States inspectors and be dipped once under government supervision. I knew there was no area clean where we had been running our sheep and I doubted if we could get a certificate. I asked the United States inspector at El Paso what he would do if they did find scabies. Would they let us in under quarantine to dip twice and kill the infection? He said, "No. If there is any scabies you can't bring them in under any circumstances." And that was that, and caused me many sleepless nights. I knew about these regulations when we went in but did not know that scabies was prevalent down there. 15

Later we found it on the La Zarca Ranch. And on an adjoining ranch which we crossed twice with our herds the sheep there were alive with it. How to get around all this was our problem. Once I planned to move back about half way, into the State of Chihuahua, where there were no sheep and therefore no scabies. But the range was very poor and little to be had. So we finally decided to run the gauntlet when we were ready to come out. 

I did not have time to stay down there constant. ly, so in April one of our men, named Brown, went down with us to stay until we shipped back. 

He reported to me almost every mail. The weather continued dry and it got hotter. The feed was poor but we moved them where there was an abundance of sweet river water and they held up pretty well, although the lambing was poor. All we now thought of us was getting out, if we were permitted, in the best condition possible. Brown is a good man at mixing with people and we secured a stand-in with those in authority. We were not sure that mail was not opened and read, so Brown and I had a code for some words and expressions. Finally in code I received the dreaded news that the scabies infection was found in our herds. These were promptly killed but we did not know when new spots would appear. The least exposed herds were shipped on the first load out and anything that looked suspicious was cut out and left behind. And on the last lap before reaching Juarez, Brown and I went through all the cars to take a last look. We found one infected. I carried her to the end of the car while the train was running full speed, shoved her through the small end door, and, swinging her back and forth by the hind leg, succeeded in throwing the poor animal clear of the track and the wheels of the train. 

That noon we crossed into El Paso to the Union Stock Yards. We expected to have an opportunity to look them over ourselves again in the corrals, but two United States Inspectors were right at the unloading chutes, one on each side, with their eagle eyes fastened on each sheep as it came down the gangplank. They saw nothing and we thought we were in the clear, only to be advised that they wanted to look them over still more down in the 16

corrals. Got by that in some way, and then they said they would inspect them again the next day as they were run through the dipping vat. But they passed and the next day were shipped back to their former ranges. We were out with our first trainload but Brown wanted to wager that the other would not come through. With that fear in mind I turned back to try.

At this point I desire to change the style of this narrative by introducing a letter I began writing to one of my brothers while on my way to Rosario for the last trainload of sheep out, which letter was completed in spare moments on the caboose and subsequent train travelling. The letter is as follows: 

"Enroute to Rosario, Durango, Mexico, May 23, 1935. 

Dear Brother Vern:

About a week ago I received a letter from Gertrude, forwarded to me down here, in which she wrote of seeing you and your wife in Washington and of course the pleasure it gave her. She stated that you said you wanted to hear from me. I have been negligent in not answering your last letter.

Quite likely what you had in mind was the question of the proposed trip to Mexico City. I do not think we can consider it. However, yesterday I made inquiry at Torreon regarding the new highway to Mexico City and was told that it is very doubtful if same will be completed before late in the year. They have 10,000 men working, but the road goes through some very rough country. Cars can go through but it must be rather difficult still. 

A week ago today we shipped a trainload of our sheep from Rosario to El Paso, Texas. One of our men, Roy Brown, was down here with us. We rode them through and lost only one sheep. We were required to pass close, rigid inspection at El Paso and had to dip the sheep once anyway. But we got through and sent them on their way to their former homes. I turned back from El Paso to bring out the balance, less about 2,000 head we sold down there. Have a week of strenuous work making delivery, fixing up papers (we sold them 17

on time) and now getting back to Rosario from 

Torreon to ship the balance tomorrow. Expect to reach Juarez Saturday afternoon and cross into the United States late in the day.

We have been in the heart of the worst part of Mexico-the State of Durango. And for about a month we have had the sheep on the range that belonged to Francisco Villa, the outlaw chief of some years ago. They are tough there and killings occur frequently. This hacienda is named 

Canutillo. It is a large one with several hundred people living there. It must have been quite magnificent at one time. The old headquarter house of 

Villa is in a bad state. Apparently at his death there was a complete letdown in morale. There are plenty of people there, carrying out the ideas of a socialistic state. The results typify the falsity or inadequacy of that theory. The present government of Mexico is purely bolshevik, although bolshevism is not fully carried out except in certain places.

I was told, when we decided to come down here with the sheep, that we could come in all right, but they would never let us get out. But unless something unforeseen happens today or tomorrow, we are going out all right and with only a normal loss. It is called a Government of Graft-that the palms must be greased, etc. I think our man

Brown used $7.00 for that purpose which is the extent to date. But the uncertainty and all has been unbearable at times and I shall be glad to get out. Brown and I were about exhausted when we reached El Paso with the other shipment. He had been down here a month; there was sickness in his family; his wife was worried about the bandits, so I sent him home. He had been in here illegally anyway. Could not get him his business passport. He had only a tourist passport, which does not allow business transactions of any kind. 

Had he been caught at it, there would have been imprisonment, fines and finally deportation. So I decided that it was my job to finish the thing. 

And, as I wrote Brown yesterday, I hope when we reach the International Boundary Line they will 18

permit me to ride on top of the cars as we cross into the United States so I can stand up there, look back this way and bid farewell to Mexico by placing a thumb to my nose and tweedling the fingers of both hands. A rotten disposition we might say, but somehow that is my reaction to the toil and rough jolts and uncertainty and discouragement that this country has wrought on me. 

On this trip I went to Torreon to see the man to whom we were selling the sheep. I had to check his financial responsibility. It is good. We then drove to his ranch-about 175 miles-which took 

13 hours to make. Upon our arrival, the ranch foreman advised that two days previous a band of six bandits had driven up where he was working, tied him, took him to the house where they kept him bound for four hours while they ransacked the place, carrying off his firearms, money, merchandise and anything of value. Shots in the freshlypainted walls made realistic their work. The men were masked, but they were thought to be from Canutillo, where we had our sheep. On our way to the sheep the next day we met a truck load of soldiers who jumped out and cracked down on us until they saw who it was. They were on their way to investigate the banditry. But those things are hard to trace. However, I know that these soldiers do not require much evidence before they start shooting. Every small town has its detachment. What these bandits want, mostly, is money. 

So it is rather dangerous to pack much money. We feel that there is less danger of us Americans being shot for money, for the reason that we do not know many here and could not easily aid in finding them. We might be held up, but not much danger of being killed. After that ranch hold-up 

I decided to hide part of my money, so for a couple of days while around there I had most of it in my shoes.

After delivering the 2,000 sheep to these men and starting the balance to Rosario where we ship, we went back to Torreon to fix up the papers. 

That trip was torture, for the worst part of it had to be made late in the day when it was hottest. 

Torreon is in a low country and the roads for fifty miles out are unbelievable. I was dead upon our 19

arrival. 

That night neither thoughts of bandits nor business worries, nor homesickness, nor heartache, disturbed the serenity of a long night of recuperation of a frayed mind and exhausted body. 

At 8:00 the next morning I was able to walk to the restaurant where I eat, and back, without feeling that it was up-hill both ways. That was yesterday and after a very full day with only a half hour rest in the afternoon, I had supper at 8:30 with my buyer and our attorney, and at 10:30 I took the train. The sleeping car was so full I had to sit up, to Jimenez, which we reached at 4:00 a.m. At Jimenez there was a branch line out to 

Rosario. I went to bed at Jimenez for two hours.

97 miles from Jimenez. However, this is a mixed train-stops at every little spot and detours at one point to Santa Barbara, which is one of the very richest mining districts in the world. 

In spite of the sordid conditions and extreme fatigue and threatened dangers, and uncertainty of getting the sheep out, I have had some interesting experiences and have formed a few acquaintances which I prize highly. I have been in and out several times. Twice I came in on stock trains and rode the boards. Twice I came in on a 

Pullman, the last one being air-conditioned. Once

I came in by auto, having traveled straight south from Albuquerque for a distance of exactly 900 miles. And about four times I came in by plane. 

I have gone out once by freight, twice by passenger train and the other times by plane. Once, on our way out on a plane, we ran into a storm cloud near El Paso and had to turn around and go back to Chihuahua, 225 miles south of El Paso, to land there after night on an unlighted field. Max Baer and his manager were on that time. On my next trip out, with the same pilot (Olson), El Paso was reported to be shrouded in dust, with visability zero-one of those Texas and Kansas dust storms drifted back. 5,000 feet thick. So he shot up to 12,000 feet and above it. We could not see the earth. He turned at right angles and we came out into the clear again. Then he jazzed along the edge of it. It was drifting slowly but it seemed to be closing in in the direction of Chihuahua. Olson got orders from El Paso by radio to go back to Chihuahua.

Pulling out of it we were over the railroad and highway south of Juarez. He circled around and around as we gradually lowered. Finally Olson sent a note back to me asking if I thought the terrain was solid underneath. I said it was probably plenty solid but almost too rough to land. I said, however, that an old lake bed he was circling over was smooth, but might be a little soft. He chose the rough, circled around a couple of times more, had the steward buckle us in, and finally successfully grounded the Ford Tri-Motor. He taxied over near the highway and radioed for some cars from El Paso. I looked on a railroad map and found we were exactly 50 miles south of El 

Paso and Juarez. A young Mexican came along in a car a little later. I hit him for a ride and hitch-hiked to Juarez. We arrived there at 8:30; balance of the passengers got in around 11:00.

Right now we are Parral, center of a great mining country. Here Villa met his death and a week ago today in Rosario I met and visited with the man who stopped Pancho Villa's mad course.

In my diary I have the man's name, written by himself-Mileton Lasoya. I was in a store in Rosario when a man came in whose manner and bearing were quite striking. He wore blue overalls, had a coat on and a good hat. I observed that he carried a gun. During a lull the merchant came around and whispered that this was the man who killed Villa. I told Brown and said that here was something worth while, that we must get acquainted with him. We got the merchant to introduce us. We soon observed that he had three body guards with him. They were drinking some beer. I quietly told Brown that we would buy them a case of beer. So Brown, who is a naturalborn diplomat, came up while I was talking with 

Lasoya and said: "Patron (Lord or Master), I am 21

going to buy these men a case of beer.” Lasoya beamed and it was done, and soon we were convivial spirits. At least, the body guards were convivial, although Lasoya was temperate as we were. 

We then invited him back to a room we used and there he talked quite freely. We were warned by the merchant not to ask him about Villa, nor talk English to each other. We complied. Finally Lasoya mentioned Villa and told of an interview he had with him. He said it lasted only twenty minutes but it seemed like twenty years. In that interview he said Villa showed all sides of his volatile personality. For a moment he would be fierce (his predominating characteristic); then he would laugh and joke; in the next instant he was highly dramatic; then he would burst into tears and weep. Thus he ran the full gamut of human emotions. Lasoya didn't say "gamut," but that was what he meant. He said he feared for his life, for many men had been called into such conferences only to be shot before they left Villa's presence. I do not know whether this meeting took place before Villa killed Lasoya's father or not.

But the killing of the father caused Lasoya to make the vow that he would kill Villa. And he did it at Parral several years later. Typical of the steadfastness and determination of Lasoya, he and some confederates waited in a certain room at Parral about three months for Villa to show up. Finally Villa came in a car with some of his men. Lasoya and his men walked out of the house and, like a bunch of Chicago machine-gunners, they killed Villa and most of his men. Villa was driving, and as he slumped at the wheel the car swerved and ran into a tree, cutting a deep gash in it. Brown has seen the tree and the gash. Lasoya and his bunch fled to the hills where they stayed several days and then negotiated for their protection, claiming that they had freed the country of its worst enemy. A few days in jail was 

Lasoya's punishment.

We did not get this information from Lasoya. 

He is very modest. And probably afraid to talk about it. Because, Villa has a son at Canutillo, now 18 years old, who vows he will get the man who killed his father. Hence Lasoya's gun and his body guard. 22

Brown and I had just secured a camera from the store man, as we wanted some pictures of the stock yards which are made out of railroad rails; also some other views. So we asked Lasoya his permission for us to "shoot" him. He inquired if we wanted pictures of the "notables"-notables. 

It was the only reference by himself of himself in connection with his notoriety. So we had two or three shots made. Unfortunately, the exposures were too bright, as I just learned at Parral, where the young merchant boarded the train to see me.

He said he would bring me the pictures to Rosario this afternoon, but they are not good.

Then we had Lasoya to dinner with us at a little restaurant run by an old Mexican woman. At supper time that night she asked me if I knew whom I dined with at noon. She said, "That was the man who killed Villa." After lunch, arm in arm, Lasoya and I leisurely walked up the dusty street of the dirty little village and back to the store where a little later he got into his old Dodge roadster, with the usual big bunch of riders, and started for home-some twenty miles up the river west of Rosario. I promised to send our car out to pick him up and told him I knew his old wreck would not carry so many so far. I don't know how they made out, but the next morning when I was in the chute at the stock yards, working like h-1 to get the sheep loaded before noon, a man peeked through the cracks of the chute and spoke to me. I turned and saw it was one of Lasoya's body guards, and I began to wonder if the pictures and his autograph and other things were not worrying larger some and perhaps induced him to send back to destroy the evidence. But I was plenty busy and could not stop from my self-appointed task of closely scrutinizing each and every sheep of those 5,400 head, to see that none with evidence of the dreaded scabies got by. When I had finished, the train immediately pulled out, so I don't know whether my friend (?) was gunning for me or not. Anyway, Brown and I enjoyed very much this interlude in an otherwise fairly drab experience down here.

In spite of the squalor and poverty and disease 23

and moral delinquency down here there are many people worth while. In the cities there are many of charm and culture whose courtesy is exemplary. But Mexico City, nor Torreon, nor other cities is all there is to Mexico. Out in the haciendas -the big ranches-where economic conditions are poor and life is hard, do we find what makes or breaks Mexico. These haciendas are on water courses, streams or rivers, which provide water for some irrigation of beans and corn. Sometimes, however, they are located at only a spring which supplies water for domestic purposes and the livestock they raise. The hacienda is owned by the 

Patron (Lord or Master) and around him cluster a group of peons with their families, numbering from ten to fifteen families up to several hundred persons. They have land to till for themselves and also they work intermittently for wages, for the patron. They have their local government, a subdivision of a larger unit, known as a municipio.

The buildings of the hacienda are similar in design-being flat-topped, high ceiling adobes. The home of the patron is usually quite imposing, consisting of a large number of rooms with an inner court. The chapel, containing altar and all the trappings of the Catholic church, occupies, or rather occupied, an important place in that group. Since the revolutions and the ascendency of the Calles order most churches have been closed and the chapels were destroyed. In Canutillo, mentioned heretofore, the Capia (church) was quite grand. 

The last one was built, according to the carved date, about 1907. It was magnificent with high towers and a Carrillon from Europe was placed therein. But now it is defaced and mutilated and when I was there the Sunday afternoon before I wrote this, kids of different ages were climbing upon the walls and roof and even up in the towers where still hung the bells which they struck now and then in a sort of mockery of the previous era. 

The houses of the peons are thick adobe with blackened walls within as there are no stoves and cooking is done over an open fire. Now practically all of the haciendas, from the home of the patron down to the adobe of the lowliest peon, is in a bad state of repair. The revolutions, beginning with 24

Madero and continuing down to and through Villa, devastated all haciendas and small towns. Only by paying tribute did the larger places escape. All livestock that could be sold was driven off and the country was ruined. All of this demoralization, together with the practical abolition of the Church and the enactment of the agrarian laws and the teaching of bolstevism, has broken the morale of the people and the evolution from the depths necessarily is slow. And until the rural life gets on a much higher plane the government and social life of Mexico will be on a comparatively low level despite what is seen in some of the cities.

The people, generally, even the peons, are openly courteous, and treat one graciously. But by observing the high walled enclosures at the haciendas, the grated windows and heavily padlocked doors of the buildings in the cities, and the boarding up of all show windows of stores from 1:00 p. m. to 3:00 p. m. every afternoon siesta, it is apparent that they do not yet fully trust each other and feel the necessiy of guarding against robbery and pilferage. It is the heritage of the revolutions. 

But we know that there are many individuals whom we can fully trust. As instances, one night in Chihuahua a Mexican boy had carried my heavy bag in and out among the tracks and cars of the railroad yards, taking me, at midnight, to a little eating place and then took me back to another caboose. When I finished my meal I paid with a $5.00 bill. The restaurant man did not have the change and had to send over town to a saloon to get it. I had very little time left to catch the train. 

The Mexican boy said he would wait and bring me the money to my caboose. I decided to trust him.

The train started, but before it got under much headway, here came the boy hot-footing it to deliver me my change. Later on two or three other trips through Chihuahua late in the night I saw the same boy and we became great pals. Another instance of trust reposed was our delivery to Veyan 

Bros. of about 12,000 sheep for their sole care, without bond or guarantee of any kind except their good word. They agreed to care for the sheep and turn them back and make good any unnatur- 25

al loss. And they are doing it. A customer of ours, a Spanish speaking Basque, took his sheep down there. He and his son looked after them until they came back. Their loss was as heavy as ours, but whereas we had the pelts from most of those that we lost, they had no pelts. So our trust in the Mexicans was justified. Another Mexican whom I have come to admire and trust is a young attorney of Gomez Palacio, a city just across the state line from the city of Torreon. When I asked that a contract be drawn up, Veyan suggested this young attorney, named Noberto Valdez. He was 

Veyan's attorney. I was in a foreign country with an imperfect knowledge of their language and no knowledge at all of their laws. I had been told repeatedly that the Mexicane would get me some way or another, and here was a perfect set-up. But after we conferred with Valdez for a time and I noted what he said must be included to make the contract legal, I decided that Veyan's attorney was good enough for us and told him to go ahead. I had already prepared a letter which Veyan signed, which was the understanding we had. 

It was short, covering only one sheet. It was the basis of the contract. Valdez complimented me when I had translated it. But he said the Mexican form was longer and when he finished it and sent me a copy later, I found that it was almost a volume. 

It was couched in Spanish that was extremely difficult for me to translate, but I observed that the essence embodied the facts of my letter and I signed. Down there the original instrument is the instrument written in the books of record and the principals sign therein. The copies may or may not be signed. When I received my copy a month or so later, I sent it to our Attorney, Mr. Ilfeld. 

Familiar with the Spanish language, he pronounced the "escritura" (contract) a masterpiece. Likewise Valdez' letter of transmittal was a beauty. 

On subsequent trips down there I tried to see Valdez, but greatly to my sorrow he was away.

Valdez is a sort of police judge with an office on one side of a large court yard, on the other side of which are the prisoners, the guard, etc. 

In there I saw many desperate looking characters and bought some gadgets made by some of the prisoners. In this seat of the law my friend, Val- 26 26

dez, ruled supreme. I asked him a few days ago if he had the power of life and death. He said he could do almost anything.

We sold a herd of sheep-about 2,000 headon time. The buyer offered security and we returned to Torreon to fix the papers. I told the buyer, a Mr. Arozarena, "Rice Flour," I wanted 

Valdez to do the work. I had not seen Valdez for about six months and drove over to see him. As the taxi came opposite the door into the court yard I saw Valdez in there in the midst of a group of men. When I stepped out of the taxi and he saw me, he came forward and called out in real good 

English, "Hello, Earl Lake Moulton of Albuquerque, New Mexico. I am glad to see you." In a hard, foreign country of a strange tongue, where you feel that a majority do not like your native land, such a greeting by such a man was music indeed. Arm in arm we walked inside and after a few minutes of felicitations I told him what I wanted and what security I preferred. He immediately suggested how this could be brought about and the next day the "escritura" was drawn up and was signed by Mr. Arozarena and myself. As security, in addition to the sheep themselves, we have about 550 acres of cotton land adjoining the city of Torreon-a town of 65,000 people. That night we had supper together-we three-at the Spanish Club. Out there on a wide out-door veranda, overlooking the beautiful plaza of the town, underneath the languid stars of that southern clime, we ate a wonderful supper, the piece de resistance of which was a most delectable fish, with all the side dishes of the real cooks of that country, the while we sipped their wine and I listened to the beautiful Spanish as spoken by this educated young Mexican and the highly intelligent Spaniard to whom I had sold the sheep. Our supper appointment was at 8:00 p. m. (they do not sup earlier) and it lasted until 10:00, when they took me to my train and I bade goodbye to a couple of gallant gentlemen, the remembrance of whom gives me a thrill as I think of the day and evening we spent together. 

I have said that I shall be glad to get out of 27

Mexico (and as I finish this the sheep and I are out). The reason is because of the uncertainty of it all, the extreme fatigue caused by my trips in and out of there and the danger involved. But 

Superintendent Garcia and others of the National 

Railway, the Veyan Brothers, Attorney Valdez and

Mr. Arozarena and even some of the peons whom I finally became quite well acquainted with, are the bright spots of the whole experience. Brown and 

I have made some real friends down there and we have done our best to show the Mexicans whom we contacted that we respect their country and their people and that the United States is a friendly nation. We found that in spite of their nationalism, they privately admit that their northern neighbor is some country and that they like the people of California, Arizona and New Mexico. We have always known of the ill feeling between the Texans and the Mexicans. I have come to the conclusion that practically all of the antipathy that Americans have for the Mexicans is due to the Texas influence and propaganda. Now, knowing quite well both the Texans and the Mexicans, I can truthfully say that the Mexicans on the average are more courteous, more intelligent and more dependable than the average Texan of southwest 

Texas. The Texans love to keep alive the memory of the Alamo and nightly down there in Mexico the Mexicans can hear the broadcast of the Texans' hymn of hate. The Texans love to pour it on, and they like to say over and over again that the Mexicans believe that if it were not for Texas they could whip the United States. To that I reply, after spending about six months down there and knowing the Mexican psychology, that if it were not for the Texans the Mexicans would not want to whip the United States.

The above are some of my reflections and some of my experiences. I was in the midst of the description at Parral. I went on to Rosario and the next morning loaded out the last of the sheep. I started at 4:30 to 5:00 and finished at 10:00 a. m. 

Then the train crew courteously waited until 1 could get my things together and eat a muchneeded breakfast. They playfully blew the whistle while I ate. At the last moment a Mexican boy 28

offered to sell me a little lobo (wolf) puppy. I bought him; we put him in a box. I took him on the train and now he occupies a cage in the Albuquerque Zoo with his name, “Panchita Villa," printed on a plaque on the outside. He had come from the old Villa ranch at Canutillo and I named him "Panchito" in memory of the old Pancho, laird of Canutillo. Panchito was a pretty good little puppy, except the first night he howled a lot and kept it up until I slid off the hard bench where 

I tried to sleep and went over and petted his head and gave him some water, after which he was a very docile wolf. Come daylight I went up ahead and climbing from car to car of the moving train I pulled the small doors open and went inside and unpiled the sheep that were down, threw out a few that had died in the night and laboriously worked my way through all of the cars. In all also I found eight Mexican tramps, inside the cars and locked in by some friendly allies in the hope, I presume, of getting across the border into the United States. (They had read down there how our great and good President up here feeds 'em all, and that "no on shall go hungry.") But I roued them all out,and the trainman kicked them off at the first stop-and a God-forsaken spot it was.

Ten minutes earlier than the despatcher had promised he would have me in Juarez, we were there. The Mexican National engine shoved us to the middle of the bridge over the Rio Grande and the Santa Fe engine from El Paso came out to the center and hooked on. Against International rules 

I got up on the stock cars and as we pulled across the line I saluted Mexico. But I did not do it as

I had threatened to, for the memory of the friends I had made down there, the sight of the skyscrapers in El Paso and the glow of getting back to our own land softened my spirits; so my salute was, after all, a most friendly gesture.

We moved on into the El Paso Union Stock Yards, unloaded the sheep in the heat, ran the gauntlet of the keen-eyed inspectors, complied2with the thousand and one regulations of coming across the border, snatched a little lunch at 2:00 p. m., 29

(no breakfast that morning) and at last, worn to a frazzle, I took the elevator to my room in a good American hotel. It was 7:00 p. m. I took off my dirty clothes and lay down on the bed in my underwear, deciding to rest a bit then bathe and go to supper. I don't recall getting onto the bed but it was 3:30 a. m. when I came to. It had turned cold.

Monday we dipped them, and on Tuesday I took them north to Corona. I went later to our ranch; then home for a couple of days, then out to western Arizona to deliver some other sheep we had sold; then an inspection at Flagstaff, then a day at

Gallup and now I am on my way home, fifteen days from the time I started this letter. Have written only while on a train. 

Now you write one, and send this to Myrtle if you think she would like to read it. Yours, Earl." 

I started that letter five months ago today. It was fully three months before I fully recovered from the physical exhaustion that I felt when we got these and other sheep back home. I think of those experiences in Mexico often and each time there comes back the old fear and dread and uncertainty of it all. A few days ago I met Albert 

Mitchell, manager of the big Bell Ranch of Conchas Dam fame, who, last fall, took some 6,000 cattle a little distance south of the border into the state of Sonora. He was on his way with some men to bring the cattle out. I asked him how he had made out. He told me briefly of his experiences and his reaction to it all. They were prac. tically identical to my own.

This spring and summer nature smiled on most of New Mexico. The snows came and melted, the rains fell, the sun brought its warmth, weeds sprang up where the grass had died last year, and the desert blossomed as the rose. The sheep are fat, the cattle chew their "cuds" in luscious contentment, and the ranchman whose memory is short 30

is lulled into peaceful forgetfulness, dreaming that he lives in a land of plenty. But the realist realizes it is but a "breathing spell"; that he must be prepared to again see and feel the dry cold winds and a parched earth. For this, gentlemen, is a part of the Great American Desert. 31
