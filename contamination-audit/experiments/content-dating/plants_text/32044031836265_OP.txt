1. Authority to appoint receiver. In Illinois, a court of equity has no jurisdiction, in the absence of express statutory power, to appoint a liquidating receiver for an insolvent bank.¹

2. Constitutionality of Section 11. The Supreme Court has not directly passed upon the constitutionality of Section 11 authorizing the appointment of a receiver for an insolvent bank by the Auditor. In Nelson v. Toluca State Bank, 334 Ill. 83, the court refused to consider the constitutional question for the reason that there was no reviewable order or decree. People v. 

Shurtleff, 353 Ill. 248, was a mandamus proceeding to compel the trial court to expunge an order appointing a receiver for a bank other than the person appointed by the Auditor. The 10

judgment awarding the writ was based on the lack of jurisdiction in a court of equity to appoint a receiver for a corporation in the absence of a statute conferring such power. In a special concurring opinion, Mr. Justice Heard expressed the belief that 

Section 11 is constitutional. The constitutionality of Section 11 

was upheld in Webb v. Marozas, 268 Ill. App. 338. The appeal in that case was from an interlocutory order and the court held that it had the right to consider the constitutionality of the Act. In People v. Niehaus, 356 Ill. 104, a similar act pertaining to liquidation of insolvent insurance companies through a receiver appointed by the Director of Insurance was definitely upheld. The Supreme Court expressly repudiated the rule declared by way of dictum in the case of People v. Marquette 

Fire Insurance Co., 351 Ill. 516 and held in the Niehaus case that a liquidating receiver is not a judicial officer but that the court is bound to recognize and accept him. A similar federal statute (12 USCA Sec. 191, 192) empowering the Comptroller of the Currency to appoint a receiver for a national bank has been held not to vest judicial power in the Comptroller in violation of the constitution.¹

(B) Title, Rights and Duties of Receiver. 

14. Receiver represents all parties powers defined. 

While the receiver of an insolvent corporation is regarded as the representative of the corporation, asserting its rights, taking its title, and being subject to its liabilities, he not only represents the corporation but also the creditors, and when in any proceeding he occupies exclusively the latter status, he may, and under some circumstances must do many things which the corporation itself, or its representative, could not have done. He is trustee of the funds of the insolvent corporation for all its creditors, and he may intervene to see that no injustice is done to anyone.¹ This does not apply to a receiver appointed in an action to enforce enforce stockholders' liabilities.2 

15. Receiver is administrative officer. A receiver is not merely the representative of the owners of the property submitted to his control by the Auditor but is the instrument of the law. His powers are derived from the statute and in respect to such rights he is not circumscribed or limited by the rights which were vested in and available to the owners. The assets of the bank coming into his hands are in his custody as an administrative rather than a judicial officer. The court has only such supervision over him as is prescribed by section 

11 of the General Banking Act.* 

16. Rights of receiver-title to negotiable instruments. In case of negotiable instruments the receiver generally occupies a position similar to that of the bank, and defenses which might have been properly urged against the bank are available against the receiver. This is not true, however, in suits instituted by a receiver to enforce payment upon a mortgage or note given to the bank for the purpose of enlarging its assets so as to deceive the bank examiner. In such cases the courts have held that the makers were estopped to allege want of consideration as against the receiver acting as the representative of creditors."

II. BANK DEPOSITS (A) General and Special Deposits. 

¶19. General deposits-printed rules-acceptance of passbook-joint deposits. Deposits in a bank are either general or special. A general deposit is one made to the credit of the depositor, to be drawn upon by him in the usual course of banking business. The relation between the bank and its general depositor is that of debtor and creditor, and, in the event of the insolvency of the bank, all general creditors share in the assets of the bank, after the payment of prior claims, in proportion to the amount of their respective claims. A deposit in a bank is presumed to be a general deposit in the absence of an agreement to the contrary. The printed rules of the bank contained in a pass-book issued to a depositor are generally binding upon him.5 The acceptance of a pass-book evidencing a deposit is a strong indication that it was a general deposit rather than a special. Under Chapter 76, section 2, Cahill's Ill. Rev. Stat. 1933, a joint bank account, in the absence of a specific agreement, is deemed to be an account in joint tenancy and the funds on deposit pass to the surviving depositor."

IX. CONSTITUTIONAL AND STATUTORY PROVISIONS 188. Origin of liability. A liability upon stockholders of 

Illinois banks was first imposed by Article X of the Constitution of 1848, but said liability applied only to banks authorized to issue bank notes to circulate as money.¹ While that 

Constitution was in effect, about 175 charters were issued to banks with varying provisions relative to stockholders' liabilities. Many of these banks failed during the 1857 panic and the Civil War and a number of suits for the enforcement of stockholders' liabilities reached our Supreme Court and resulted in a series of decisions governing that liability. The rules laid down in those decisions, however, are not always now applicable, because in 1887, after the adoption of the Constitution of 1870, the Illinois legislature enacted the first General Banking Act which made all charters uniform and each stockholder of banks of every kind was made liable only for liabilities which accrued while he remained a stockholder. 

189 Constitutional. Section 6 of Article 11 of the Constitu tion adopted in 1870 provides as follows: "Every stockholder in a banking corporation or institution shall be individually responsible and liable to its creditors, over and above the amount of stock by him or her held, to an amount equal to his or her respective shares so held, for all its liabilities accruing while he or she remains such stockholder."

X. NATURE AND EXTENT OF LIABILITY 

¶93. Liability defined.

Both the provisions of the Consti tution of 1870 and Section 6 of the General Banking Act as amended are fully discussed in the case of Golden v. Cervenka, 278 Ill. 409, decided April 19, 1917. The Supreme Court held in that case that under the constitution, a stockholder is primarily liable only for debts accruing during his ownership of the stock and that under Section 6 of the General Banking Act, no transfer of the stock operates as a release of that liability. A stockholder is responsible to the amount of his stock for all the liabilities of the bank which are incurred during his ownership of that stock, and no more, and such responsibility continues until such liabilities are paid or otherwise discharged. He remains liable after the transfer of the stock

to the same extent as before but not for any debt subsequently accruing, or for any indebtedness thereafter discharged. The transferee becomes liable only for such debts as accrue and are unpaid during his ownership of the stock and so it is with each successive holder.¹ 2 ¶94. Obligation contractual-primary-not penal. While the liability is called statutory because the statute or the constitution attaches the obligation to the ownership of stock, the liability is in fact contractual and springs from an implied promise. By becoming a stockholder, one assumes a primary liability to creditors of the bank to an amount equal to his stock and he is contractually bound to such creditors. The liabilities created by law prior to the organization of the bank are also assumed by the stockholder and he is estopped from denying his liability by becoming a stockholder of a bank authorized to operate under the banking law. The liability of a stockholder is not penal." ¶95. Liability similar to that of partner. Bank stockholders are not mere sureties; they are in reality principals and are individually and personally liable, as partners, to all creditors of the bank and are answerable as original and principal debtors. Our Supreme Court, in referring to a stockholder's liability as that of a partner, did not intend to limit that liability or prescribe a method for the payment or discharge thereof, but merely to refer to a legal theory similar in character. Neither the constitution nor the statute limits the liability of a stockholder to that of a partner and when the legislature said "No transfer of stock shall operate as a release of liability provided in this section," it could not have intended to characterize the liability as that of a partner in all respects.

XIV. INSOLVENCY OF BANK 

115. Proof of insolvency unnecessary. It is not necessary to prove that the bank is insolvent to enforce the liability of a stockholder. The liability of a stockholder to a creditor is primary and is regarded as that of a partner, occupying the same relation to the creditor as the bank itself and owing the same debt to the depositor as the bank owes.2 Notwithstanding the cases supporting the foregoing, plaintiffs in stockholders' liability suits frequently establish the insolvent condition of the bank at the time it was closed, merely as an added ground of equity jurisdiction. 

116. Insolvency defined. 

While some courts have held that a bank is insolvent when it is unable to meet its liabilities as they become due in the ordinary course of business or when it cannot pay its depositors on demand, the greater weight of authority holds that an impairment of capital does not mean insolvency and that a bank is insolvent when the cash value of its assets allowing a reasonable time for liquidation by its owners is not equal to its liabilities, exclusive of its stock liabilities. The former rule is generally applied to civil proceedings while the latter cases arise out of criminal prosecutions under a statute. The liability of the bank to its stockholders must be disregarded in determining its solvency, for if it has property of sufficient value and so readily convertible as to enable it to pay its creditors within a reasonable time, it is not insolvent.“

123. Liability of successive holders-bankruptcy—reorganization-payment twice-waiver. XX. DISTRIBUTION OF THE FUND 135. In general. Paragraph 11 of the General Banking 

Act of Illinois, as amended in 1929, specifically provides that the fund collected from stockholders on account of their ownership of the stock of the bank, after the payment of the costs and expenses of collection, including solicitors' fees, shall be distributed according to law among the creditors of said bank in such manner "as the court shall direct." It naturally follows that in the event of a surplus remaining in the hands of the receiver after discharging the liabilities of a particular class of creditors of the bank, such surplus should be distributed among the stockholders of the bank who contributed to that class in proportion to the amount collected from each of them. This process of distribution often presents a complicated and highly involved mathematical problem due to the numerousness of creditors of the insolvent bank and their assimilation into groups and classes conforming to accruals of the liabilities due them respectively. Since the act authorizes distribution in such manner as the court shall direct, it might prove more practical and economical to distribute the fund pro rata among the creditors in proportion to their claims against the estate. Also, in the event of a surplus, to distribute such surplus among the stockholders in proportion to the amount paid in by them. This may be attained by con struing Section 6 of Article 11 of the Illinois Constitution of 

1870 as creating a liability running to the creditors of the bank as a class rather than to each of the creditors individually.
