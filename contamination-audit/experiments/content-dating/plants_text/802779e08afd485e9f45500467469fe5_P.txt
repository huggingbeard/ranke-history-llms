- 5 - During the harvest season the abundant supply of fruits and vegetables in the area exceeds the demand of local consumers. Substantial quantities are shipped in fresh state to other markets by rail and by truck. Some are placed in the many storage warehouses in the 9-county area and are thus available during the winter months for local consumption and for shipment to markets outside the area. Large quantities are sold by farmers to the 279 processing plants in the area.
There has been rapid expansion in processing by the quick freeze method.
In 1950, 42 of the area's plants had facilities for quick freezing as compared with 24 in 1948. It has been estimated that about 42 percent of all commercial fruit and vegetable production in 1950 went to processing plants.
The 9-county area produces poultry and eggs in quantities large enough to meet local demand at most seasons of the year. At the height Eggs of seasonal production some eggs and some live and dressed poultry are shipped by truck to markets outside the area. No appreciable quantities of locally produced eggs and poultry are placed in storage during seasons of surplus production. For this reason, local demand at times must be met by supplies brought in from other commercial producing areas.

Dressed poultry comes from the Delmarva section, from the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, from north Georgia, and from the Middle West.
Principal inbound shipments are by truck, but some dressed poultry and eggs from the Middle West areas are received by rail.
Many animals for slaughter in packing plants in the Rochester area come from local farms. Packing plants in the city also receive live animals by rail and truck from Kentucky, Ohio, and other States in the Middle West. A small volume of livestock of all classes is shipped from farms in the 9-county area surrounding Rochester to outside destinations.
Fruits and vegetables are most important of all 9-county farm products moved through the facilities under discussion. The marketing of this large volume of fruits and vegetables, in both fresh and proc essed form, constitutes a problem of prime importance. It involves the operation of many facilities designed for all types of use. Before any attempt is made to develop a comprehensive plan for improved facilities, attention should be given to the existing facilities, their location in the area in relation to important producing sections, and to the service they now perform in getting these important crops to market.

- 11 - WHOLESALE FOOD DISTRIBUTORS IN ROCHESTER Information on the variety and volume of products handled at wholesale in Rochester, methods of transportation by which these products arrive, the trade groups that handle them, and the manner in which they are distributed was obtained from wholesale dealers, farmers, truckers, buyers, the market master of the Rochester Public Market, and railroads, and from observations of the actual handling of the products through the market.
Fruit and Vegetable Dealers Eleven independent wholesale dealers, two chain store warehouses, and numerous farmers and truckers supply fruits and vegetables to the Rochester area in wholesale quantities. Three other food chains supply their retail store units with fruits and vegetables from warehouses in other cities or purchase supplies from Rochester wholesalers.
Estimated direct receipts of fruits and vegetables in Rochester in 1950 are shown in table 4. Local production accounted for nearly onethird of the total receipts of 8,220 carlots. Not included in the table was a large but unestimated quantity of fresh fruits and vegetables delivered direct to retail stores and other consuming outlets by farmers and truckers. Of the total 8,220 carlot equivalents shown in table 4, 2,535, or about 30 percent, arrived by rail and 5,685, or approximately 70 percent, came into the city by truck.
The 11 independent wholesale dealers estimated that in 1950 about 95 percent of all fruits and vegetables handled by them was for use in the city, and 5 percent went to towns and cities in the Rochester trading area, A high percentage of the business of these independent wholesale dealers comprises the delivery of orders in their own trucks along established routes. In 1950 about 73 percent of their total volume was handled in this way. The other 27 percent was picked up at wholesale stores by buyers and hauled away in their trucks. Wholesale delivery routes are confined mostly to the city of Rochester. Only a few deliveries are made outside the city and none outside of Monroe County. Among buyers on the market local retail storekeepers predominate, but numerous truckers and smaller wholesalers from nearby towns also purchase supplies there. The major part of the produce sold by wholesale dealers consists of fruits and vegetables coming from distant areas. Wholesale dealers handle only a small quantity of native farm produce. Ninety-five percent of all fruits and vegetables handled by these wholesalers moves through their store facilities, only 5 percent being delivered from rail cars on team track. Six of the independent dealers handle a complete assortment of fruits and vegetables; three handle bananas only; one sells potatoes, onions, and watermelons only; and one combines a dry grocery business with the sale of bananas and potatoes. Bananas are freely traded between the wholesalers; otherwise there is little interchange of produce among dealers.

Truckers' supplies consist mainly of fruits and vegetables brought into the city from other producing areas or those purchased on markets in other cities. Included in offerings by truckers are fruits obtained at auctions in New York and Daltimore.
The major part of products sold on the Rochester Public Market by farmers and truckers is picked up by buyers. Sellers occasionally deliver large lots to premises of buyers within the city. Wholesale purchases on this market are made by the same buyer group that patronizes wholesale fruit and vegetable stores. The group buying at retail is made up of consumers from all walks of life and from all parts of the city and its suburbs. These people come to the market in large numbers, particularly on Saturdays, on foot, in automobiles, and by public transportation.
Poultry and Egg Dealers The poultry and egg business in Rochester lacks organization. There are no wholesale dealers who handle these products exclusively. Three packer branch houses handle sizable quantities of eggs and dressed poultry. The distribution of these products is in conjunction with the distribution of meat and meat products and allied packing house products.
Several independent wholesale grocers distribute eggs along with dry groceries and grocery specialties. Chain store warehouses supply their retail units with poultry and eggs obtained in various ways.
Some eggs are brought to chain store warehouses by truckers who collect them along routes in the nearby area. These eggs are then candled and cartoned at the warehouse before being sent to the retail stores. However, the chain stores usually arrange with packer branch houses for the direct delivery of dressed poultry to retail stores.
Numerous small independent poultry and egg dealers in stores scattered throughout the city sell mostly at retail, but they handle a small volume of wholesale business. In the main, their supplies of both eggs and live poultry are delivered to the stores by truckers who have assembled them along routes in nearby farm areas. Some eggs are candled and cartoned by these dealers, but many are sold without this type of processing. Most poultry is killed and dressed on the store premises, but some sales of live poultry are made direct to consumers who visit - - 14the stores. The wholesale business of several of these small poultry and egg dealers consists in delivery of orders to retail stores, restaurants, clubs, hotels, and institutions.
Substantial quantities of eggs and live poultry are sold on the Rochester Public Market by farmers and truckers. Many consumers go to this market to purchase these products. Several truckers who collect eggs along routes in the nearby area use market stalls to sell them in case lots to retail storekeepers and in smaller quantities direct to consumers.
Many farmers in the immediate vicinity of Rochester sell eggs direct to consumers along established house-to-house routes or through contacts in factories, offices, and other business establishments. No reliable estimate of the size of this business is available, but informed sources point to the importance of this method of sale in Rochester as compared with other cities.
The loosely organized nature of the poultry and egg business in Rochester precludes a reliable estimate of the volume of these products handled at wholesale. In respect to distribution practices reliable sources estimate that in 1950 over 95 percent of poultry and eggs handled by Rochester dealers in stores and on the Rochester Public Market was distributed within the city.
Meats and Meat Products Meats and meat products are handled at the wholesale level in Rochester by numerous trade groups. No distinct pattern of distribution is evident, and there are no well-defined limits to the area served by certain wholesalers who find outlets for their products in the northeastern United States.
Three national packers maintain branch houses in the city. Two other national packers operate through the medium of car route salesmen.
Incoming supplies are received by both rail and truck from plants in distant cities. All these national packers distribute carcass meat, meat products, and allied packing house lines to retail stores, hotels, restaurants, institutions, and other outlets in the city and along established truck routes reaching out from the city, a distance of about 40 miles. All three packer branch houses prepare meat products in their Rochester establishments under inspection by the Health Bureau of the city.

Wholesale Dealers in Groceries and Grocery Specialties Six wholesale grocers have warehouses and distribute nearly complete lines of dry groceries in Rochester and the surrounding area. The large and varied demand for special foods coming from various groups in Rochester's cosmopolitan population is met by numerous smaller wholesale-retail dealers in these specialties.

- 17 - DESCRIPTION OF WHOLESALE PRODUCE MARKET FACILITIES The only recognized wholesale produce district in Rochester is in and around the Rochester Public Market. (See fig. 2.) The greater part of the wholesale business in fruits and vegetables is transacted on this city-owned market and in nearby stores of independent dealers. Three wholesale grocers have warehouses on a street bordering the market.

Rochester Public Market The Rochester Public Market occupies approximately 10 acres of land in the central part of the city. (See fig. 2.) Surrounding the market is a heavily populated residential area into which there has been some infiltration of manufacturing enterprises. The market property is bounded on the east by First Street, on the north by the line of properties fronting on Pennsylvania Avenue, on the west by a short section of North Union Street, and on the south by rails of the New York Central Railroad.
The Rochester Public Market was opened for business in June 1905.
Since that date some improvements have been made but, in general, the layout has remained unchanged. The facility is owned by the city of Rochester, and since 1938 it has been supervised by the city Department of Commerce.
The property is triangular in shape. Along each side there are three open sheds (figs. 5 and 6). A fourth shed, which is L-shaped, is inside the area bounded by the other three sheds and parallel to two of them. Beginning with the shed on the northern border and proceeding counterclockwise, the three outer sheds are designated A, B, and C. The L-shaped shed is designated D. Space between the sides of shed D is occupied by two brick buildings. One of these buildings has two stories and contains offices for the market superintendent and his staff and space for the supervision staff of the Department of Weights and Measures. The other building is one-story high and contains public rest rooms. Between these two buildings is a large platform scale for public weighing.
All market sheds are of the same style. Over center concrete platforms, 18 feet wide and of curb height, double-pitched roofs are supported by steel columns at platform edges, 16 feet from center to center. On each side, the roofs extend 14 feet beyond the platform edges. Roofs are 18 feet high at peaks, and the eaves are mostly 12 feet above street level, but in several spots there is only a 10-foot clearance. Space between posts on each side of the platforms is marked off into stalls, 8 feet wide and 5 feet deep, and buyers' walks 8 feet wide run through the center of the platforms.
There are 450 stalls in, the four sheds and 475 additional open stalls are available along curbs. Major Defects in Market Facilities Certain defects in facilities used and methods followed in the wholesale handling of foods in the market have been noted in the preceding description. Since any program for improvement should be designed to correct these inefficiencies, it is appropriate that they be examined in greater detail.
Existing facilities in and near the Rochester Public Market through which fresh fruits, vegetables, and other farm products are handled both at wholesale and retail levels have come into being without benefit of any coordinated plan.
For many years the city-owned Rochester Public Market has proved to be a reasonably acceptable market place for farmers and truckers. Continued patronage by consumers indicates that the market operates to the benefit of certain buyer groups. Conduct of business in wholesale stores near the Rochester Public Market, as observed by the authors and compared with other markets, is not a deplorably wasteful operation. However, the limited area in this downtown location is inadequate for handling the total business of the market.
The outstanding defect in the facilities being used is the lack of rail connections to the stores of fruit and vegetable dealers. Only about one-third of their rail receipts are unloaded at stores which have rail connections, or are delivered direct from cars on team tracks.
In 1950 the contents of about 1,250 cars were trucked from team tracks to wholesale stores at an average estimated cost of $30 per car. This trucking is costly not only in terms of cash paid for the operation, but also with respect to substantial losses sustained through the extra 26handling of produce and its exposure to the weather. If all fruit and vegetable stores had direct rail connections, nearly all this trucking could be eliminated and much loss could be avoided.
Another major defect of fruit and vegetable stores is that much unloading and loading of trucks takes place over sidewalks and involves a great deal of manual labor. Although two stores have platforms at truckbed height, these platforms do not have sufficient space for the amount of produce moving over them.
At the eight locations where buildings designed for other purposes have been converted to fruit and vegetable stores, there is much loss of space. Obstructions, such as old partitions and posts, have interfered with the convenient location of refrigerated and ripening rooms.
There is much loss of time, and additional labor is required to move produce into and out of refrigerated or ripening rooms in remote parts of the stores. Many of the rooms used for ripening bananas are of old construction, their use resulting in uneven ripening of the fruit.
Seven of the 12 stores have no outside space, even on sidewalks, where merchandise may be displayed.
In the Rochester Public Market all sales platforms are of curb height only. For this reason the transfer of merchandise takes place by manually lowering merchandise from sellers' trucks and lifting it to the tail gates of buyers' trucks. At these platforms all space is assigned to sellers. No parking spaces are provided for buyers. Buyers usually find it necessary to park their trucks in market streets, often at some distance from the stalls where purchases are made. Produce is carried by hand or on two-wheel hand trucks from sellers' stalls to buyers' trucks. Frequently this journey is over open streets where there is no protection from sun, rain, or snow. A consequence of delivery by this method is deterioration of the produce and loss of time. The intermingling of vehicles of numerous retail customers with trucks of wholesale dealers and buyers on the market results in congestion and slows down all transactions on the market.
Under market regulations there are five groups of sellers on the Rochester Public Market. This classification appears cumbersome and has been criticized by many who use the market.

- INTEREST IN PLANNED MARKET IMPROVEMENT In recent years several plans for the improvement of wholesale market facilities in Rochester have been proposed by various groups who use the market and by the city. However, up to the present time diversified needs of the various groups that have interests in market improvement have prevented concerted approval of any of those plans.
The Genesee Valley Regional Market Authority was established to develop better facilities. The purpose of this authority as set forth in the 1951 law "Shall be to acquire, construct, reconstruct, improve, equip, operate, and maintain adequate regional market facilities within the district for the buying and selling of agricultural products and to conduct such other business activities as reasonably are incidental to and in furtherance of the convenient, efficient, profitable, and success - ful operation of such regional market facilities." The authority's scope of operation includes the entire Genesee Valley Regional Market District.
A logical first approach to facility improvements is to explore the feasibility of establishing a modern consolidated market in or near Rochester to serve the city and its trade area and to provide a wholesale market place for producers in the area. Sellers, buyers, and the community can best be served and maximum operating efficiency can be realized only if wholesale facilities are consolidated in a single market area. Any move leading to continued diffusion of facilities should be avoided. The extent of interest in a new market by all groups and the willingness of individual operators to use new facilities should be determined before any development is undertaken. A careful analysis of this situation was made.
All 11 independent wholesale fruit and vegetable dealers agreed that the market needs improvement. However, only 2 showed active interest in a new market and said they would use space in it. Two other dealers indicated that they would move to a new market if they could dispose of their present stores and if financial advantages could be shown. The other 7 dealers stated that they are definitely opposed to the development of a new market and said that they would remain in their present locations regardless of action taken by other dealers. If this divided interest on the part of dealers continues, the building of a market in a new location would bring about the undesirable effect of a "split market" unless positive steps were taken to discontinue business in the present market area.
During the survey, numerous farmers in the Genesee Valley Regional Market District were contacted by representatives of the New York Department of Agriculture and Markets. One group of the farmers interviewed live within a radius of approximately 25 miles of Rochester and are regular sellers on the Rochester public market. While all of this group said that the market needs improvement, they were divided in their opinion - · 28 - Another concerning the feasibility of building a new market. Many of them were definitely opposed to moving the market from its present location.
group of producers living in more remote parts of the Genesee Valley Regional Market District seldom or never sell their products on the Rochester market since they have other long established outlets for their produce. There was no well defined opinion among this group as to the feasibility of and chances for success of a new market. A few in this group said they would patronize a new wholesale market by offering part of their produce for sale thereon. Others indicated that they would not sell on the new market, but would continue the practice of selling through other channels.
None of the truckers who sell fruits and vegetables on the Rochester Public Market was interested in a new market. Two truckers who sell eggs were interested and said they would consider leasing space on a new market. One wholesale dealer in poultry and eggs now located about 50 miles from Rochester said he would support a new market and rent a store in it.
The manager of one wholesale grocery said his board of directors would follow the development of a new market with interest, but doubted that they would want space. Three other wholesale grocery houses objected strenuously to any plan for moving the market from its present location.
No other groups or individual operators were definitely interested in a new market at this time. The extent of interest of packer branch houses could not be determined because decisions of this nature are not made at the local level. Local slaughterers and processors of meats operate a type of business not adapted to nor desired on a produce market.
This lack of interest by dealers, farmers, and other market groups will make the establishment of a new market difficult. The only way to establish a successful consolidated market in Rochester will be to enlist the interest of the groups who would be tenants in the market or to take positive action to close the existing market area, which would force the relocation of the business.

- - 29 - A POSSIBLE PLAN FOR A NEW WHOLESALE PRODUCE MARKET Although at this time principal groups that might become tenants of a new market have little interest in a new and modern market in or near Rochester, it appears prudent to present a plan for such a market which may be studied by all groups concerned with the problem of developing improved facilities. In developing the plan it was necessary to make assumptions as to the number of operators who might occupy new facilities at the time a market might be built and those who might be included at a later date. The amount of facilities that might actually be built would undoubtedly vary from those presented in this plan, depending on the sentiment at the time construction is proposed. However, unless enough interest develops to warrant building substantially as many facilities as are included in the plan for initial construction, the promoters of the market should proceed with extreme caution, as a wholesale market would not be likely to suceed unless a major number of the existing operators transferred their business to it. Also, the continued existence of the Rochester Public Market and of nearby stores, where a large part of the wholesale food business is now transacted, poses an immediate problem to the success of a market elsewhere. Plans for a consolidated market depend on a decision as to whether satisfactory improvements can be made in the present market area or whether a new market should be developed at another location to which a majority of present handlers would move their business.
In the event that only part of the dealers elect to move to a new location and others remain in their present stores the success of the proposed venture would be doubtful. In any event, construction of a market should be preceded by obtaining from financially responsible parties commitments for leases for reasonably long-term occupancy of facilities.

Space for Expansion After a new market begins to operate, it is likely that additional wholesalers of fruits, vegetables, poultry, and eggs will want to move into the area. Dealers in farm implements and supplies, baskets, bags, crates, and other containers probably will want stores in the market.
Furthermore, experience in the development of other markets of this kind has been that other types of food handlers such as wholesalers of dry groceries and dairy products tend to gravitate into the market area. If the initial construction of the market is successful, its drawing power will be considerable so that adequate expansion space for these needs is a necessity. In addition, there often develops a demand for space in a wholesale market for such other services as a service station, public garage, and warehouses of various kinds. Many facilities of the kind suggested may be built and operated by private enterprise. However, since they would be of value to the wholesale market, it is desirable that the agency building the market acquire sufficient land to meet these demands when and if they develop. Acquisition of a large expansion area involves little risk since property in the immediate vicinity of a successful wholesale market tends to increase in value.

Factors to be Considered in the Selection of a Market Site The people most directly concerned with the location of a new market may be divided into four groups: (1) Buyers, both local and out-of-town, who go to the market for their supplies; (2) sellers, both nearby and distant producers and shippers, who either bring or ship produce to the market; (3) dealers, warehousemen, and processors who would use facilities on the market; and (4) transportation agencies. Indirectly, many other groups are concerned in the selection of a market site. Consumers in the distribution area have an interest in the selection, since a proper site is essential to economical distribution. The city of Rochester through ownership of the Rochester Public Market has an interest in market improvement. Regardless of the course which market improvement may take, the city also will be interested because of the effect these plans may have on zoning ordinances, traffic control, street and highway planning, and other services rendered by the city. In reaching a conclusion as to the most suitable location for the market, six principal factors should be considered.
1. A location most advantageous for local distribution. The principal service performed by wholesale handlers in Rochester is the distribution of food within the city. In 1950 an estimated 95 percent of all fruits, vegetables, eggs, and poultry handled by Rochester wholesale dealers was distributed to retail food outlets within the metropolitan area. The ideal location for a wholesale market to serve these local outlets would be a site at the shortest average distance from these establishments, assuming, of course, that such a site is at a point which can be easily reached over existing or proposed streets. Buyers for retail outlets who regularly go to the market for supplies would share with wholesalers who maintain delivery service the economies incident to a convenient location. Because of their regular dependence for supplies on the wholesale market, retail grocery stores were selected in establishing a convenient point to local outlets. The locations of all these stores in Rochester were spotted on a map. From these locations a center was determined representing the nearest point to all stores, that is, as many stores are east of this point as there are west of it, and as many north of the point as south of it. No consideration was given to the size of the stores. This center is at the corner of Joseph Avenue and Nassau Street. Availability of land at reasonable cost. The cost of land on which the proposed market is to be developed (including the cost of placing the land in condition for construction and the cost of buildings that may be on the site and their removal) will have a direct influence on the cost of the project and on the amount of rental income necessary to amortize the investment. It has already been shown that a large acreage would be required for a wholesale food market. Therefore, land may represent a substantial part of the total market development cost.
This makes it essential that the land be obtained at a reasonable cost if the maximum benefits of the new market are to be realized.

Site No. 6 Big Ridge Road. Site No. 6 in the town of Greece, comprises about 50 acres at the southeast corner of the intersection of Big Ridge Road and Lee Road. It is bounded on the west by Lee Road and on the north by Big Ridge Road. Since there is frontage of about 1,500 feet on each of these roads and the tract contains about 50 acres, which is more than enough land for the development of a market, no definite east and south boundaries have been established.
Local buyers could reach this site by way of Ridgeway Avenue or Mount Read Boulevard. Out-of-town buyers would approach the site from Lee Road after coming into the area from any one of a number of east-west highways.
Rail service could be extended to the site from the western terminal of the Subway or from the New York Central or Baltimore and Ohio railroads.
A public water supply and electric power are available from lines on Big Ridge Road. Storm and sanitary sewers would have to be provided by those building the market. The site is practically level. There are three small one-story structures in a poor state of repair.
In 1951 the assessed value of this property was approximately $65 per acre, and the tax rate in the town of Greece that year was $18.96 per $1,000 of assessed value.

Approximately 10 acres are contained in the city-owned market property. Since a minimum of 20 acres would be required for present and future needs, it would be necessary to acquire private property adjoining the market on two sides. Expansion to the south is prevented by rail lines. It would not be feasible to extend the market to the west across Union Street. About 10 acres could be added by the acquisition of properties between the market and Pennsylvania Avenue from First Street to Union Street, and by adding a strip of land averaging about 200 feet in depth on the north side of Pennsylvania Avenue and the east side of First Street. Parts of Pennsylvania Avenue, First Street, and Niagara Street would need to be taken into this expansion area.
No assessed value has been placed on the city-owned Rochester Public Market. The adjoining properties suggested to make up the required acreage had a total assessment in 1951 of $519,760 (land, $114,570; buildings, $405,190). At comparable rates the assessed value of the market would be about $225,000. Thus, the total assessed value of the site would be approximately $750,000. The 1951 tax rate in Rochester was $45.39 per $1,000 of assessed value.
In addition to its location in an area of high land values, the site has several other features that make it unsuitable for market development. Numerous business enterprises are housed in substantial buildings on the surrounding 10-acre tract. These buildings would have no value for market purposes and would have to be razed. Present occupants would have to find new locations. Many householders also would be dispossessed and have to move. to new quarters. Multiple ownership of private properties would make acquisition difficult and costly. It would be necessary to close to general traffic those sections of Pennsylvania Avenue and First Street which would come within the market. Trucking incident to market business would continue to contribute to the traffic load on streets in the central part of the city. Operation of the market during the period of construction would also constitute a problem.
Comparison of Suggested Market Sites In selecting a site best suited to the development of a new market, extreme care should be exercised to make sure that the site meets the approval of the major groups that will use the market. Otherwise the business might be split into two or more areas, thus losing many of the economies which would result from the consolidation of the market in one area.

Sites Nos. 3, 4, 5, and 6 are all within about 4 miles of the geographical center of retail food outlets. However, sites Nos. 3 and 4 are more accessible to the city because they are easily reached by way of Chili Avenue and its intersecting streets. Sites Nos. 1 and 2 are about mile farther from this center of retail outlets than are sites Nos. 3, 4, and 6. However, it should be pointed out that sites Nos. 1 and 2 are easily accessible by way of several routes. Since these routes are not heavily burdened with traffic, distance is not a serious handicap to sites Nos. 1 and 2.
With respect to location best suited to handle out-of-town business, whether incoming or outgoing, sites Nos. 1 and 2 are in a most favorable position because of their closer proximity to the proposed Through Way and the existence of several good highways to communities east of Rochester. Sites Nos. 3, 4, 5, and 6 are not quite as well situated for the handling of out-of-town business. Roundabout highway service to and from the east is a handicap to these sites. Site No. 7 is less favorably situated than any other site with respect to convenient approach by out-oftown highways.
Sites Nos. 1, 2, 3, and 7 immediately adjoin rail tracks from which spurs could easily be extended into the market area. Longer rail connections would be necessary to reach sites Nos. 4 and 6. Main line passenger and freight service on rail tracks adjoining site No. 5 presents a problem in extending rails to it. Railroad officials may object to tapping these tracks for a spur.
All sites except site No. 7 could easily be fenced off to keep out nonmarket traffic. Only in the case of site No. 7 would it be necessary to close existing city streets to accomplish this. Sites Nos. 1, 2, 5, and 6 are the most favorably situated with respect to nonmarket traffic on nearby streets or highways. Nonmarket traffic is heavier on streets near sites Nos. 3, 4, and 7.

Amortization of Investment The length of the amortization period for an investment of this type depends on the length of the useful life of the facilities. It is possible to extend the financing of such a project over a long period of time for several reasons. Prior to undertaking the development of a market, some estimates of possible benefits, if any, should be made. In addition to the tangible savings discussed herein, a number of other savings that cannot be accurately measured would result from operations in a new market. For instance, the same volume of business could be handled with fewer man-hours through well-designed wholesale stores and farmers' sheds because of the possibility of doing business in a shorter period of time with fewer employees. New types of handling equipment might well increase time savings in a new market.

Each seller interviewed was asked to furnish data on these costs. Farmers and Truckers Farmers and truckers doing business in a new market will benefit primarily in the saving of time. Interviews with this group disclosed that on each load sold on the Rochester Public Market an average one-half hour is lost because of congestion.
If all 200 stalls in a new market were used at a rate of 1 load per stall each day for 200 days in a season, 40,000 loads would be handled.
If one-half the time lost in selling an equal number of truckloads on the present market could be saved, the annual saving would amount to 10,000 hours. If the time of a man and his truck is valued at $2.50 per hour the annual savings on 40,000 truckloads would amount to $25,000.
Possible Savings to Buyers It is not possible to estimate accurately the number of buyers who may be expected to visit a new wholesale market daily. However, based on opinions of dealers covering present operations in a market where most of the business is handled by the service wholesale method, and on the anticipated volume of business, an average of at least 50 wholesale buyers may be expected to visit the new market on each business day. In a market operating 6 days each week with 50 buyers per day there would be 15,600 trips to the market each year by buyers. Assuming that on each trip traffic congestion causes loss of buyers' time equal to that of time lost by farmers and truckers, the yearly loss of time would amount to 7,800 hours. If half of this time could be saved in a new market and the time of a man and truck is worth $2.50 per hour, the annual savings to buyers would amount to $9,750.
Other Benefits In addition to direct benefits to dealers, farmers, buyers, and others who will use the market, other benefits may be visualized.
Railroads might improve their competitive position through direct delivery of produce cars to the market. Public team tracks now used for the delivery of produce could be diverted to other uses. Railroad employees now engaged in supervising the delivery of produce could be relieved for other services.
The city would benefit through the relief of traffic congestion in the downtown area. Sanitary and health regulations could be more easily enforced. Consumers in the area could expect to benefit through receiving produce in better condition and possibly at lower cost.
The building of such a market would materially aid those engaged in over-all planning because when the market is once built and in operation - 59 - with adequate expansion area, it may be expected to establish a wholesale food district for the area.
As it has been reported that the city has been reluctant to consider closing the Rochester Public Market, it is proper to examine more in detail the way in which new market development might affect that market.
For many years the Rochester Public Market has operated in a manner generally accepted as in the public interest. Wholesale and retail business has been conducted in a reasonably efficient way. There have not been flagrant violations of regulations relating to sanitation and public health. With the exception of congestion on the market proper which prevents the entrance of large trucks no acute traffic problems have been created by the market. The location of the market does not conflict with any proposed highway improvements or zoning projects.
This market was built in 1905 at a cost of $190,000. In the intervening years money spent on improvements has brought the total investment to approximately $275,000. A nominal assessment of the market property has been estimated as $225,000. Annual interest on this amount of money.
at 2 percent is $5,625. Also, potential tax income, calculated on its estimated assessed value and at 1951 tax rates, amounts to approximately $10,210. Together, loss of interest on the investment and loss of potential tax income amounts to $15,835. In a previous chapter it was pointed out that in recent years expenses of operating the market have increased, and that in the last 2 years for which figures are available (1949 and 1950) income from market operation has exceeded expenses by less than the $15,835 represented by potential income from taxes plus loss of interest.
With minor changes in the physical plant, with some revision of fees, and in regulations this "break even" position can probably be maintained, possibly improved.
If, on the other hand, a new market were built in or near the city, it is assumed that the Rochester Public Market would, by consent of the city, be abandoned and the property converted to other use.
The 10 acres of land contained in the market with adjoining rail service offers possibilities for development as an industrial site. The city of Rochester would be a beneficiary in such a transaction by reason of the potential gain in tax income from a new enterprise.
Major deterrences in the path of present day development of a consolidated market in or near Rochester are: 1. Lack of interest on the part of those who would do business there. The majority of wholesale dealers are now doing business at long-established locations in stores where they have substantial investments in real estate and equipment. The high rentals necessary to support a new market will make them hesitant in signing leases for new facilities.

- 60 - 2. The adverse attitude of the city in respect to participation in any plan that will result in the closing of the Rochester Public Market.
3. The failure of estimates to show that operations in a new market will result in substantial dollar savings. Measurable net savings to fruit and vegetable dealers are estimated at $58,200. Savings to farmers, truckers, and buyers, based on time saved, are estimated at $34,750.
Although a new market development in Rochester can be economically sound, those responsible for its development must recognize that the success of the undertaking would require a changed attitude on the part of the several interested groups.
Neither the city nor those who use the market can be forced to change their attitudes. Support of a new market by all of these groups can probably be secured only through persuasion, accompanied by substantial proof that economies to all concerned will result from its construction.
Economic justification for a new market will be measured only by its capacity to operate as a self-liquidating enterprise and supplant existing market outlets by rendering more useful or economical service to producers and dealers and by bringing greater benefits to the community at large than are possible through existing facilities.
It has been estimated that a new market facility for the Rochester area could be constructed for $716,000, exclusive of land cost. The necessary annual revenue to support such a facility on a self-liquidating basis has been estimated at just over $80,000. This revenue must be secured from the rental of stalls to farmers and truckers and of stores to wholesale dealers and others. Before any bank or other financing agency will consider such a project some assurance will be required that the estimated revenue will be forthcoming.
The success of those responsible for the development of a new market in securing the necessary assurances of market income from the growers and dealers will in large part determine whether a new market can be justified.
