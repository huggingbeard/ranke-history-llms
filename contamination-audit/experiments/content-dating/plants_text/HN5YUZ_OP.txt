HAZEL HURST 

HAZEL HURST was born twenty-five years ago of humble parentage in a small town in New Jersey. Three days after her birth an accident shut the light from her eyes but it could not close the windows of her soul. A gymnasium accident when she was twelve injured her spine and paralyzed her body so she could not walk, but the driving power of a courageous will was not crippled. Aided by the small local Rotary Club, she went through high school in a wheel chair; to Columbia University on crutches while periodically undergoing a series of operations to restore locomotion. Fifteen of these have been performed, the last one this year which took three doctors three and one-half hours, but the battle was won. Hazel now walks and alone.

TRAFALGAR 

LAST week was the anniversary of the battle of Trafalgar. That battle sealed the possibilities of an invasion of 

England by the French at that time. When we think of 

Trafalgar, we think of Nelson's last signal to his fleet. We picture a small man with only one arm, literally wading into death. With twenty-seven sail of the line and five frigates, he attacked the enemy with thirty-three sail of the line and seven frigates and he won. 

Southey says: "Nelson's strong sense of duty to his king and country gave him the keenest incentive to exertion, and a never-failing stay and support amid the vicissitudes of fortune. He was free from all petty jealousies.

His victories were due to his thorough knowledge of his profession in all its details, to his quick discernment of the thing necessary to be done, and to his devotion of all his powers to the accomplishment of his aim."

No man who knows his profession, seizes opportunity and devotes all his energy toward success can ever be defeated regardless of the odds.

Nelson's last signal to his fleet was the famous: "England expects every man to do his duty." Then when he heard the shouts of his fleet coming over the water in response to the signal, he said: "Now, I can do no more. 

We must trust to the great Disposer of all events, and the justice of our cause. I thank God for this great opportunity of doing my duty." 

That was Nelson's last battle and his greatest. Twenty minutes before the enemy ship "Redoubtable” struck, a sharpshooter from her mizzen-top shot Nelson through. 

Nelson had refused to go below or to take off his admiral's uniform or his stars. His last words: "Thank God I have done my duty."

We salesmen do not really sell for financial returns. At least such returns do not get the extra effort. We do go to town on pride of accomplishment. Ever think about closing your book on salesmanship? Will the record cause you to stick out your chest? Be a Nelson in every battle and it will.

RED PANTS 

GOLF is an ancient game and through the years various implements with which to play it have been devised. 

Intricate courses dotted with varying hazards have been constructed all over the land. In fact there seems to have been a race between some men working with nature to make the game tough and others using their science to produce skillful men and clubs to trim the natural and created obstacles.

The 1938 British open disclosed, however, the true secret for success in a golf match or any other contest. A youngster from Atlanta, Georgia, won this tournament.

Although he was little known prior to this match, he nevertheless shot a steady and consistent game in sun-

shine or driving rain. The pace of the championship drive saw every American contestant eliminated save this blond youth. Finally, he reached the finals and won against 

England's best. 

What caused this upset? A pair of red flannel shorts that Bobbie Jones had given him when he left Atlanta. 

Bobbie told him to wear them and he would win. The kid went to town on that belief. When he came in soaked by the rain, match after match, he personally dried those flannel shorts and wore them away from the course. He believed in their power because the greatest golfer the world has ever seen gave them to him and told him he could not lose if he wore them. The unknown in red flannel pants shot over his head and knocked over every obstacle in his path.

All a salesman ever needs is a Bobbie Jones pants idea. 

Splendid merchandise, great campaigns, markets glutted with money-these do not make men sell. We have seen good men starve to death in the middle of all these. Likewise, we have watched men thrive on much thinner sales soil. Men sell when they get a firm grip on the idea that they can sell. It is your job to give them that belief. Do it and they will sell the pants off of a wooden Indian. Fail to do it and they will lose their pants and yours along with them.

ON NEY 

N OCTOBER 18, 1812, Napoleon gave orders for the retreat from Russia. Of the 600,000 that had inaugurated the invasion, 108,000 heard the bugler sound "Retreat.”

A mere handful of these were ever to see French soil again and only one of them refused to accept defeat. He was assigned the task of covering the rear of the disorganized mass fleeing for their lives. 

By November 6, the thermometer had fallen to thirty below. By December 7, the last of the horses had perished.

Even crows and ravens (omens of ill) dropped like stones from the stiff branches of the trees. Napoleon had fled. 

The army had completely disintegrated and the last remnant of discipline had vanished save only in the handful of the rear guard under the command of a man who was never known to quit. 

On November 17, the Cossacks cut him off, surrounded him with overwhelming numbers and fired salvos in honor of his impending capture. At nightfall, he marched right back toward Russia, lit campfires, then wheeled

about, reached the frozen Dnieper River, abandoned horses and baggage, and crossed on the ice to rejoin the wildly retreating army and cover its rear on foot. For forty-two days he kept up these tactics, fighting on the ground with musket in hand to keep the Cossacks from the helpless stragglers.

The last stand was made on December 7. The Russian attack was so severe that even this rear guard was routed -with the exception of the leader and four privates. 

These fought on, picking up and firing the loaded muskets which had been thrown away by the fugitives. By eight o'clock that night the last straggling soldier of the once Grand Army crossed the Niemen and left the soil of Russia. An emperor, two kings, a prince, eight marshals and 600,000 men had been defeated. Only one, the son of a barrel-cooper of Saarlouis, Marshal Ney, had won. He backed across the line on foot, smeared with blood, ragged, a smoking musket in his hand, but with his face turned defiantly toward the enemy and still fighting. 

When hope seems gone, shove out your chin and fight. 

Everyone else may lose. You will not.

OLIVER 

A CAREER 

liver Wendell HOLMES was first of all a philosopher.

Because of that fact, he was next a great jurist. He looked through the hulls and saw the kernels of life's problems and expressed his findings tersely and understandably. 

He chose law as a career because he thought it was worthy of his best efforts. He once said:

Every calling is great when greatly pursued. 

That fits our calling as salesmen. There are few avocations where great effort receives such rich rewards and so speedily. Likewise, there is no profession that so quickly makes tramps of the half-hearted. 

Mr. Holmes' philosophy on the present and the future is equally pointed and convincing. 

The man of action has the present, but the thinker controls the future.

There is no law against a man vigorously applying himself today while planning an equally active campaign for the future. The man who spends all the day dreaming and hoping for tomorrow is just as far out of pocket as the man who is rashly jumping about today with no idea of where he will land tomorrow. Act and think. You can do both.

Selling individually or collectively is a matter of development. That calls for a combination of immediate promotion with a future projection. Today's sale must provide a future satisfaction and recognition for purchaser, salesman and company. Such contracts must be increased daily. When today's means to that end are exhausted, others must be supplied. The thinker is always ready with new ways to accomplish his goal.

To get the maximum out of your capabilities today while planning for greater maxima in the future is progress toward a great career. Boiled down it means reaching this month's quota while laying stronger foundations for more sales next month. Act and plan. Win today while planning for tomorrow.

HOBBIES 

HEARD a man say the other day: "Bob Stranahan thinks of nothing but Champion Spark Plugs." This remark was made by a golfer who was discussing the merit of Mr. Stranahan as a golfer. He went on to say that when Mr. Stranahan was on the golf course he was thinking about Champion Spark Plugs at every stroke. He said: "That's his life-his very being all that he eats, sleeps and dreams. Get him down to Florida, away from his office and he will shoot with the best professionals, but at home it is all spark plugs.".

Perhaps that is the reason that he is the success he is. 

Elbert Hubbard once said: "Happy is the man who gets paid for doing what he likes to do." We might dare to

suggest that no man gets paid for doing what he does not like to do. The more you see of your job-the more you study it, the better you will like it. There are many hidden paths that you have never explored in it. It has many possibilities that you have never seen.

More so today than ever before, it is necessary to eat, drink, sleep, think and dream about your job. It takes a lot of smoke to make the grade. Cannot get smoke without fuel. Suppose early in this new year we resolve all over again to make a hobby of our jobs. Forgetting all else, let's stick to them until the day's problem is met and mastered. It is a good thing to have your town say of you: "He thinks of nothing but his job."

A WORKMAN 

His hair was meticulously cut and combed. His white jacket and apron were spotlessly clean. The counter at which he worked resembled a drawing room table. The utensils which he used glistened. The knife he wielded was particularly keen. There was a glint in his eye and he moved with the grace of a greyhound. Not a motion was lost and no idle conversation escaped his lips. 

Ten or fifteen waiters screamed orders at him. "Raisin toast down. Make it two . . . one rye down; hold the butter... three minute eggs ... hot muffin with ... etc.”

With a quiet enthusiasm he turned them out. In went the toast. Out it came, was buttered, sliced and passed on. 

That rapier-like knife was handled with the precision of a surgeon. Into the water went the eggs. Out they jumped.

They were opened and passed on. No spilling. Nothing sloppy. A workman was at work.

Pyrrhus, Plutarch says:

Pyrrhus was declared by Hannibal to be the greatest general of his time. He was a brilliant and dashing soldier in battle, but did not know how to make use of victory.

With all his marching and fighting he really accomplished nothing.

In a few more weeks, you will write "Finis" to this year. 

How far have you come? What really worth-while things have you accomplished? How have you directed your efforts? How much fighting have you done that has carried you toward the point for which you headed in January. Let's be definite about it. That is the only way to ever attain what you desire.

Take a sheet of paper today-right now, and write on one side of it your accomplishments. Regardless of the kind, write them down. Perhaps you have improved your health; mind; method of working; handling of people, etc. 

Write them all down. On the other side, write down the battles in which you have lost ground. Be honest. Put them down. Then see how many of those battles you can win before December 31. KEEP DIGGING 

FOR over seventy years Philip Francis, of Knoxville,

Tennessee, has been digging under the ground. As a mere lad, not even in his teens, he started by picking slate out of the coal in the mines of Pennsylvania. 

He can tell you about days of hunger—almost starvation-when as a boy he ate raw potatoes to keep alive. 

Sunday after Sunday, a stepfather pitted him in fist fights against other boys. The prize was a bucket of beer to the father of the winner. Phil usually won.

Panics are not new to Philip Francis. He vividly remembers those of the last century. During one of them he practically walked from Pennsylvania to the Rocky Mountains in search of work. He found it and again he was digging under the ground; this time for silver and other ore.

He was one of the first developers of the coal fields of Kentucky and Tennessee. He went there when the coal sections were ruled by the fist and the gun and as a mine

superintendent he handled those rough frontier men in their own way. He engaged in twenty-one bare knuckle fights during those days, and the other fellow went to the hospital each time.

Today, at eighty-six, Philip Francis is a successful coal operator. He is still vigorous and can outlast most men who are supposedly in their prime. These last years haven't worried him. He has seen too many tough spots. His pet piece of advice is: "Keep serene and keep digging."

Figuratively, Philip Francis has never dropped the pick and shovel. Almost from infancy he has been in love with his work. His faith in himself and his job has never wavered. It has been mentally easy for him to pay the price of success. We commend his philosophy to you today. It will make each successive week a success.

CANAWL 

THE "Big Ditch" linked the lakes to the ocean. The opening of this Erie "Canawl" was America's greatest commercial achievement of the first half of the nineteenth century. From Albany to Buffalo the cannons boomed within hearing distance of each other on the day that water crept into one end of the "ditch" and started crawling toward the other end. Within a few months the canal was a seething water highway jammed with horse and mule drawn vessels. Dotting its banks were inns, stores and factories. Life on the canal took on a romantic thrill. 

As the years rolled on, the canal became a part of the lives of all who touched it. It was powerful. For more than half a century it had surpassed all other agencies in feeding our growing nation with men and supplies from the Atlantic seaboard. To them it was a giant that would never die.

In the early "fifties," when railroad right-of-ways began to be cleared along the canal and steel bands were spiked onto ties that ribbed those paths, the champions of the "Big Ditch" became indignant. Nothing could ever compete with it. Even when the first steam locomotives began to whistle up and down the run, these hardy canal champions would not give in. They would go down to the station for the definite purpose of standing there and

"cussin'" the trains. Some even stood between the rails before the starting engines and refused to move. Occasionally they were bumped aside and bruised, but they were proud of their wounds. They called it "standin' up to them trains.”

Today the Erie Canal is only a watery, disjointed ruin, clearly indicative of how foolish it is to become so attached to old ways that you want to "cuss" the implements of progress. No man of vision looks backward. Because they were good enough for your father is no reason for you to suppose that fifty-year-old machines and methods will win for you today. You can sit in a rocking chair on a canal boat and lazily drift toward your goal. You may enjoy it. You may believe it is a fine way to do business. 

You may even sneer at the trucks, trains and airplanes that whizz by you. But they go on just the same and the people aboard them are carried to new achievements. 

They point to you and say: "Poor fellow, he has no vision. He is perishing."

If you are traveling in a rut, get out of it, regardless of how large it seems. Adopt new implements and methods. 

Ride them to new records. Start this week.

THE FIRST MILLIONAIRE 

BENJAMIN FRANKLIN is revered by Americans for almost everything but some may fail to recall that he was our first big moneyed man. From a roll of bread in his hand walking down Philadelphia's streets with empty pockets he went a long way. Here are his twelve rules that he says accounted for his success: 

1. TEMPERANCE-Eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation. 

2. SILENCE-Speak not but what may benefit others or yourself; avoid trifling conversations. 

3. ORDER-Let all your things have their places, let each part of your business have its time.

4. RESOLUTION-Resolve to perform what you ought; perform without fail what you resolve. 

5. FRUGALITY-Make no expense but to do good to others or yourself; i.e., waste nothing. 

6. INDUSTRY-Lose no time; be always employed in something useful; cut off all unnecessary action. 

7. SINCERITY-Use no hurtful deceit; think innocently and justly, and if you speak, speak accordingly. 

8. JUSTICE-Wrong none by doing injuries, or omitting the benefits that are your duty. 

9. MODERATION-Avoid extremes; forbear resenting injuries so much as you think they deserve. 

10. CLEANLINESS-Tolerate no uncleanliness in body, clothes or habitation. 

11. TRANQUILITY-Be not disturbed at trifles, or at accidents common or uncommon or unavoidable. 

12. HUMILITY-Imitate Jesus and Socrates. 

Mr. Franklin added: "Any person who follows these simple rules faithfully will increase his wealth, his happiness and his peace of mind." 

Well, he ought to know. He had wisdom, wealth and position. Perhaps we can well follow these rules. Which ones of the above rules are you short on? Pick them out and do something about them. If they were good for 

Mr. Franklin, they will serve you. They are simple and easily attainable.

PUSH OUT 

THE founder of the Vanderbilt fortune started his career with a rowboat. As a stripling he borrowed one hundred dollars from his mother, bought a rowboat and started paddling people from Jersey to Manhattan. 

That flat bottomed skiff grew into a large ferry business. 

Then the pioneer Vanderbilt began seizing other opportunities. Water and steam transportation pushed beyond the frontiers under his direction. Within a generation the 

Vanderbilt fortune grew from a borrowed one hundred dollars to over two hundred million dollars. The strength of this grizzly pioneer is best illustrated by an incident of his closing days. His doctor prescribed champagne as a stimulant for his fading system. The old gent replied: "My goodness! I cannot afford champagne." The trail from the rowboat to millions had not softened the pioneer frugality nor the driving spirit of Mr. Vanderbilt. 

There are some fine streams before you. Opportunity beckons from the banks on the other side. Many men will put in the hours of close application that will enable them to paddle over to the other side. That spirit that makes the most of its opportunity will go on to build "steel and water" empires in this business. We are on the brink of a new era.
