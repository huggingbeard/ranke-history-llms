Aside from the fact that the Indians were all

copper-colored, there is little else to indicate that all tribes came immediately from a common stock. 

They differed widely in physical characteristics as well as tribal cultures. In these respects the 

Five Civilized Tribes of the South were unlike the nomadic Comanches and Cheyennes of the Great Plains, or even the highly developed Pueblo tribes of New Mexico. Furthermore, within a given area one tribe was often dissimilar to another. On the Southern Plains, the Comanches were wiry, athletic and resourceful, while their allies, the Kiowas, were large in size, indolent and given to obesity. Cultural differences were sometimes equally marked. On the eve of their removal from their Southern ranges to new allotments in the West, the Cherokees, Creeks, Choctaws and Chickasaws had democratic assemblies, lived in substantial villages, and subsisted in part on corn, fruits and vegetables which they cultivated, while on the other hand the Great Plains nomads were primitive and opportunistic in all their ways. In fact, there was a wide range in Indian cultures, from the low level of the Western Coast Diggers, who subsisted on grasshoppers, snakes and rats, to the semi-civilized Pueblos, their neighbors on the east, or to the Cherokees. 

2. INDIAN VIllages 

Indian villages also exemplified the same variety. The Hopis and Zunis of the Pueblo family

Rev. Isaac McCoy, a Baptist missionary on the

Indiana frontier, wrote in 1840 that the northwestern Indians "erect their huts in their villages [usually lodges made of skin or bark] without regard to the order which would produce streets. 

They are placed promiscuously, as leaves fall from the trees, and they never plant their corn or other vegetables in rows. All of which prove that our modern Indians are really the aboriginal race, and that they never had been more civilized than they were when we first became acquainted with them." He could have made the same comment as regards the Sioux, Cheyennes,

Arapahoes, Comanches and Kiowas of the Great Plains, except that they did not cultivate the soil.

They were entirely nomadic in their habits. A 

Comanche band might set up a village on the 

North Platte River in Nebraska in July and on the 

Brazos of Texas in September. The Great Plains tribes, too, pitched their lodges without order. 

The American Indian was a naturalist in all his ways. Since he shared the dangers, hardships, privations and successes of his tribe, his interests were communal. For example, the red man could not conceive of private ownership of land. To him, land was like water and air-free to all. 

Indeed, he had few personal possessions-a horse, riding equipment, weapons, trinkets. Other things he shared with his fellows, like game, food, and even sometimes, blankets. Still, a tribe could claim a given tract of land as its range, beyond the bounds of which it could move occasionally but not to establish a village without the consent of the neighboring tribe claiming the range.

Often, however, a strong tribe drove a weaker one from a coveted hunting ground. 

3. GOVERNMENT AND SOCIAL LIFE 

Every band of a tribe was under the control of a chief, sometimes inheriting his right but more often selected by the warriors because of his prowess on the hunting and war trails. Assisting the chief in all important matters were subchiefs, head men, and warriors, who in council

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians generally decided on when and where to conduct the next hunt, to found a new village, or to wage war on an offending neighbor. Seldom did a chief dare to initiate an important move without consulting the warriors under him. Then within the tribe was usually a chief who had authority over all chiefs of bands.

The warriors spent much time in council where the opinions of one was generally reckoned as equal to those of another. Heated arguments were common over trivial matters in which all shared. 

Squaws often sat as listeners, and sometimes gave advice on important matters, but they never voted. 

Boys could express opinions if their elders called on them to do so. In such deliberations the opinions of the chief were worth little more than those of warriors.

The popular conception of the early day Indian is that he was taciturn and reserved, expressing himself in monosyllables or grunts. This was far from the truth. In village relations, or while sitting about the campfire with his fellows, he gave vocal expression to his every emotion. He was unreserved, and when in the mood would give vent to whoops, shouts of laughter, or tirades of wrath. A typical Indian village was a bedlam of noise screams, loud laughter, shouts, angry commands, animated conversation in high pitched voices, the barking of dogs, and the neighing of horses. At night, a carnival spirit prevailed. War-

riors would group in games of chance or to regale each other by highly exaggerated narratives of exploits in war or on the hunt. Modesty was unknown in such matters and often regarded as a weakness! Women and children also joined in night revels, in dancing, in games, and in lively conversation. Time meant little. If the villagers spent the night in revelry and dancing, the following day was devoted to rest and sleep.

Family life almost merged into community life. 

The warrior's lodge was open at any and all times to friends and neighbors, and his food was their food, if they chose to partake. If a hunter had been successful on the hunt he might be the hero of one hour and forgotten the next. All could gather about his fleshpot without invitation and help themselves, although invitations were usually given. He was always the generous host, and he in turn expected to be a welcome guest when another warrior had been similarly successful. A warrior was expected to accept every invitation to dine, even though he might receive ten or fifteen invitations during the day! And be it said to his credit, he uniformly did ample justice to every meal, sometimes to the extent of amazing gluttony! 

Early records reveal few cases of indigestion in such instances! But they do show that starvation was almost as common a visitor in the Indian village as was bounty. In such instances, the warrior and his family subsisted on anything that

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians would furnish nourishment, such as roots, terrapins or snakes, if nothing else could be found. Even then starvation decimated Indian ranks almost as much as plague, and often these two scourges were closely associated.

Every warrior was a law unto himself in personal matters. If in anger he killed a fellow warrior, the tribe looked to the family of the deceased to avenge the deed, which often could be satisfied by a gift from the murderer of a horse or other things. In rare cases this would not suffice and a warrior of the offended family would stalk down and slay the murderer. If a warrior's wife were unfaithful, and ran off with another man, the offended husband would seek satisfaction in much the same way. In rare instances, the offender was slain and the squaw horribly disfigured by the cutting off of her nose and ears.

Theft was one of the few offenses against the band or tribe. If a member of a band stole from another member, he was often severely beaten and expelled from the village. Since every

Indian was free to enter every lodge at will, such severity against theft was deemed necessary. But pilfering from a warrior of another band or tribe was not only permitted but was regarded as a manly virtue. Only in tribal offenses did the chief and the warriors in council seek to punish the guilty party.

4. FAMILY LIFE 

The warrior and his spouse devoted much time to the rearing of a son. He was to be the huntsman, the warrior, or the sage of the future, and no effort was spared to give him proper training. 

The boy was taught to endure suffering and pain and was subjected to trying ordeals. If he were the son of a Great Plains warrior, he must learn to ride a horse expertly. In learning to ride, he might be thrown to the ground again and again, but he must remount after each fall until he had mastered the technique of riding. If he were the son of a warrior living in the forest, he must study woodcraft, the art of stealing unobserved and silently along dim trails or from tree to tree, for only as a successful woodsman could he win over his enemies. As a mere boy he was given a bow and blunt arrows, and was painstakingly taught how to use them well, even though occasionally his target was a dog, a horse, or a squaw. Likewise with only a shield he must learn to stand before archers and ward off their darts. As a result of long years of this sort of rigorous training and in self-denial, in excruciating ordeals, in thirst and hunger, and in physical exertions, the boy often budded into a resourceful, Spartan-like warrior.

Not so much attention was shown the daughter. 

She was to be the drudge, although her mother

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians taught her primitive domestic arts such as the making of moccasins, attending lodge duties, and preparing food. But a father was often interested in his daughter's marriage. Since a daughter must marry, and since great value was put on bridles, saddles, lariats and buffalo robes and horses, he was disposed to barter with his prospective son-in-law.

The matrimonial ambitions of the young warrior were expressed in an interesting manner. If he chanced to meet his prospective bride in or about the lodge of her father, he awkwardly and bashfully gave evidence of his interest by a sly remark or look. If he felt that the maiden was also interested, he now was free to approach her parents.

Sometimes his mother was an emissary to point out to the girl's parents the many admirable traits of her off-spring; or, where the maiden's mother felt that the young warrior was a fine "catch," she would call his attention to the excellent qualities of her daughter. After an introduction, the youth watched carefully, in so far as he was able to do so, every move made by the maiden-at ball play, at other kinds of games, in conversation, and at work about the lodge. 

Thomas C. Battey, a Quaker missionary among the wild Kiowas in the early 1870's, thus described such an instance: "The young men are silent witnesses of the contest [ball play], which ends abruptly on the

setting of the sun. Their ears were also open to the remarks of the old women. If the heart of any one of them is touched with the tender passion, he seeks acquaintance and cultivates a friendship with a brother or other near relative of the maiden, tells him of his love, and by the gift of a pony, or some valued present, procures his good offices as a friend, to intercede in his behalf, not only with the object of his affection, but with her parents. He magnifies the bravery, strength, courage, success in the chase, and other good qualities of his friend to his parents, conveys to his sister some present from him to herself, with information of his love, and the number of ponies he possesses, and finally gives him an invitation to the lodge. If a favorable impression has been made, he is met at the entrance of the lodge by the object of his love, who takes his horse, unsaddles it and lariats it out, while he is invited into the lodge by the father or brother. If she is not duly impressed with a sense of his worth and tender affection, she is not seen. Should the course of love run smooth, eventually, perhaps not for weeks, a contract is made; her value is extolled by her mother, while her father, anxious to drive as good a bargain as possible, fixes her price in ponies, blankets, or other articles of value. Plural marriages were sanctioned, but not generally practiced, for a warrior who had more than one wife was forced to put forth all the more energy to provide for the wants of his family. 

It was not uncommon, however, for important chiefs to have two or more wives. On one occasion

5. RELIGIOUS CONCEPTS, DANCES AND RITES 

Tradition and superstition were strong determinants in the many feasts, rites and dances of the Indians. Among the sedentary tribes of the 

South and the Old Northwest there were seasons of feasts, dances and ceremonies in the spring, mid-summer and fall. When the green corn was ripe the Indians celebrated by feasting and dancing. 

Among the Plains tribes the medicine dance was generally observed. A concentric space of twenty feet in diameter was roped off for the dancers, outside of which were stationed guards

between the dancers and the spectators. The medicine man of the tribe then announced his selection of warriors who were to make the dance, which no warrior dared to oppose on pain of death.

When all was in readiness the dancers entered the ring prepared for them. Richard Irving Dodge, a plainsman of long experience in the Indian country, thus described the dance: "The medicine chief arranges the dancers in a circle facing to the center, whilst he himself, having got out of the way, gives the signal to commence. At once every dancer fixes his eye on the suspended image, blows shrilly and continuously on his whistle, and begins the monotonous and graceless Indian dance, the whole line of dancers moving slowly round the circle. Some of the young ones, carried away by religious enthusiasm, bound vigorously into the air; but the older and more experienced expend only a bare sufficiency of force, for this is a dance of endurance. The will of the gods is to be known by the effect of the dance on the dancers, and, until the high priest shall announce himself satisfied, the dancers must continue their weary round, without sleep, food, drink, or obedience to any demand of nature. "For the first eight or ten hours the dance is uninteresting enough, but by that time fatigue, the slow rotary motion, the constant keeping the eyes on one spot, and the expenditure of breath

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians in unceasing whistling, begin to tell. By this time every foot of space inside the lodge is crowded with eager and intensely interested spectators. Relatives and friends watch every movement of the dancers, rouse up the flagging by yells and shouts, by words of encouragement or terms of endearment. The lodge is a frightful babble of sounds, which culminate in shrieks and a rush of women, as some dancer totters, reels, and falls to the ground. The rush is sternly met, and the body dragged by the guard out of the circle of the dancers, and into that of the guards. 

There it is laid on its back, and the high priest proceeds to paint symbols and hieroglyphics on the face and person, with 'medicine paint' of varied colors. If consciousness is not restored by this treatment, the body is taken into the open air and buckets of water thrown over it. This, as a rule, soon revives the inanimate form, at sight of which the women set up yells of delight, and surround the priest with prayers and entreaties that this dancer may be spared further effort." 

But the word of the medicine man was law. 

If the proper number of ponies were offered him, one, two or three, he generally acceded to the entreaties of the squaws and the offer of the tired dancer. If the dancer made no such offer, however, the medicine man often sent him back into the ring to resume the dance. Throughout the dance, the onlookers kept up a hubbub of drum-

ming, singing, dancing, tumultuous shrieking and smoking.

There were many kinds of dances practiced by the Indians, the most fantastic of which was the war dance, which varied almost as much as there were tribes to engage in it. In January, 1872, Thomas C. Battey, a Quaker missionary and schoolteacher among the wild Kiowas, stated that he was awakened one night by a fearful combination of noises drumming, howling of dogs, yelling of men, and the laughter of women and childrenand that soon thereafter he heard the voice of 

Trotting Wolf, a friendly Indian, shouting his name and calling upon him to "be quick, come and see!" After he had hastily dressed and came out of his lodge, Battey was led to a large assemblage of several hundred Indians.

A large fire was burning, the light of which illuminated objects for some distance around. Several hundred Indians of all ages and sizes were seated in groups forming an irregular circle, while near a large fire were the warriors who beat upon drums continuously and who sang in a wierd kind of wild harmony.

Two almost naked braves, painted and with feathers in their hair and holding long lances ornamented with feathers of various colors, left the others and advanced toward the fire in a series of fantastic jumpings, jerking up of one foot and turning about in a variety of indescribable gestures

The seated

Indians encouraged them by joining lustily in singing the wild chant. Ever and anon a dark figure flitted across the arena, which in the dim uncertain light had more the appearance, said Battey, "of a demon than a human being." Thus the dance continued well into the morning hours. 

The Indian's dances, as well as his feasts and rites, stemmed from his superstitions. His word "medicine" had no relation to drugs or curatives; it embraced magic, mystery and supernatural influences. Not infrequently a warrior would claim that he had "good medicine," that is, he could forestall evil influences, disaster, and could attract luck, good fortune and success. To qualify for the power of making "good medicine," he usually withdrew himself from his fellows to a lonely hill or an out-of-the-way place to fast for two or three days and to meditate and listen to the spirits' voices. But he had to demonstrate his newly acquired powers before his fellows, on the warpath, on the chase, or in village relations. By mixing the ashes of certain herbs, of colored clay or other elements, all the while using certain incantations and movements of the hands, he could

make successful medicine, wherein he was given the powers before mentioned.

Indians believed that good and evil influences could be encouraged or discouraged in many ways. 

A Great Plains warrior dug a hole in front of his lodge, and on the fresh dirt drawn from it he placed a buffalo head facing the opening of a lodge in which lay a sick friend. In doing so he believed that healing powers would be transmitted to the sick man. Tatum stated that on one occasion a band of warriors were fleeing before pursuing troops, and a squaw sought to save them by building a "bog medicine" in which the pursuers' horses would mire down in a river's quicksand. 

It was seldom, however, that a warrior could claim the powers of "good medicine" to the extent of the medicine man, who held a rank in the tribe hardly below that of the chief. He was pampered and petted by the women and children and honored by the warriors because of his powers of combatting evil influences, of immunizing the warriors against harm while on the chase or the war trail, and of attracting the good. Sometimes, too, he sought to heal the sick. If a patient were suffering from an acute attack of cholera morbus, or dying of tuberculosis, his treatment was much the same -"howls, only howls, most doleful and lugubrious." As the patient grew worse under such treatment often squaws were called in to assist the medicine man by lifting up their voices to howl

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians in chorus. In addition, the medicine man would mutter incantations, perform mysterious hand movements and seek to drive the evil spirit from the lodge by the fanning of a wand or feathers. 

If the patient grew still worse, the medicine man would call to his assistance a lusty young warrior who would beat a tom-tom over the head of the patient. As a rule this generally finished the matter one way or another!

In sickness, particularly of the contagious kind, the sweat bath was popular. It was something similar to our modern Turkish bath. A small airtight lodge was set up over a rectangular fireplace, near a running stream. Heated stones were placed in the fireplace, near which the naked patient was required to sit, equipped with a bucket of water and leafy bough. He would then dip the bough into the water again and again and sprinkle the hot stones until the lodge was filled with a hot steamy vapor. Finally, sweat would come from the patient's body, until almost overcome, he would rush from the lodge and leap into the water of the nearby stream, the colder the better, according to the ideas of the medicine man. But before he again returned to his own lodge, the medicine man would precede him to drive away the evil spirits by elaborate rites and ceremonies. Glisan stated that this "treatment was adopted in epidemics of whooping-cough, scarlet fever, measles and small pox, and generally with the most appalling results."

Glisan also states, however, that this practice was not observed in the country of the Five 

Civilized Tribes. "Occasionally," he said, "a broken-down white educated physician would immigrate to their country, marry a squaw, and settle down to the practice of his profession; but, as a general rule, these people had to depend in sickness, except when near a military post, on the so-called doctors among themselves, who know little or nothing of the true principles of medicine, and that little being the cast-off garments of the old-time practice of the regular profession, when blood-letting was considered the sine qua non in almost all diseases. . . . This practice, too, they would adopt in even the lowest types of the disease, such as typhoid, typhus and winter fevers. No wonder that the mortality from these diseases was often fearfully great."

Religious conceptions among the American Indians varied as much as did the degrees of advancement in their cultures. The more primitive the culture the more nearly was the tribe given over to a rule of superstition, sorcery and witchcraft. Major R. S. Neighbors, who was the federal agent for the nomadic bands of Texas prior to the Civil War, asserted that all the bands under his supervision believed in one supreme being, as seemed to have been true elsewhere. And in addition, many of the tribes believed in an Evil Spirit. 

The Good Spirit aided a worthy warrior in all

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians his undertakings, delivered his enemies into his hands, protected him from danger, privation, and pain, and provided him with all good and perfect gifts. The Evil Spirit was his nemesis and brought him misfortune. It was responsible for every mortification, disaster, drought, disease and discomfort, and was the author of unhappiness, sorrow, and defeat.

An American colonial writer stated that the Indians were "never degraded by the worship of images, nor poisoned by the numerous defiling associations of eastern idolatry." They seemed to have been, more than ancient pagans, responsive to the impulses which the Creator had placed in every human heart. When they were first encountered by the white man, many of the tribes had traditions of the creation, the fall of man, the expulsion from paradise, and the deluge. But most of them practiced sorcery and secret, superstitious rites, so that allied tribes were often mutually ignorant of the particular ceremonies used. The great object in all of their rites was to ward off the malice of the Evil Spirit, who, they believed, sent the tempest, the earthquake, famine and unsuccessful war. But at the same time, all believed in one Great Spirit, a benevolent god, who gave them sunshine and showers, corn and game.

The Indian believed that these spirits were his rulers only in life; therefore, he seldom sought

direct communion with them. Still there were exceptions. Charlevois, a Jesuit priest of Canada during the colonial period, stated: "Shenandoah, a venerable chief of the Oneidas, who died at the advanced age of 113, thus expressed before his departure the deep feeling of his loneliness, 'I am an aged hemlock. The wind of an hundred years have swept over its branches. It is dead at the top. Those who began life with me, have run away from me. Why I am suffered to remain, God only knows."" Not inferior in pathos was the request of Scanando, an aged chieftan of the same tribe, who had embraced Christianity: "Lay me, in death, by the side of my minister, and my friend, that I may go up with him at the great resurrection."

There is little doubt but that relations with the white border settlers and traders brought degeneration among the Indians. Uniformly the Indians were proud, honorable and resourceful when first they met the whites. But when they were browbeaten by greedy land-seekers, cheated in barter by the traders, and degraded by the whiskey sellers, they became ambitionless, indolent, and given to debauchery. Once powerful tribes became mere bands of begging dependents, hopeless and servile. 

An 1840 authority remembered that "the Indians, in the days of our fore-fathers, were an athletic people. The rigors to which their infants were subjected, destroyed the feeble ones, and those that survived, being accustomed to privation and the hardships of war, to the use of the bow and

the pursuit of game, became vigorous and swiftfooted, and many of them were models of manly elegance. When Sir Benjamin West, in his youth, first beheld the statue of Apollo Belvidere, he exclaimed, 'It is a Mohawk Indian!'" This opinion was shared by other observers who first encountered Southern and Western tribes. 

The Cherokees, Choctaws, Creeks, Chickasaws and Seminoles retained many of these early virtues after they met the white man. Heckewelder says that from two or three months among them, under various circumstances of exposure and dependence, he always met with prompt and warm-hearted kindness, and never once lost the most trifling article.

But war, suppression and exploitation imposed by the whites, left the Indians embittered, suspicious and resentful—a state of mind which constituted a mighty handicap for the missionaries who brought the Gospel of Christ. More often than not, relations between the two people had run in a vicious circle. First border settlers would encroach on Indian land and establish their cabins here and there. These would be attacked by the outraged Indians; and, in turn, the settlers would retaliate. Then an Indian war would follow in which the whites would be successful and force the red men to relinquish the squatter-occupied area. Settlers would now rush into the ceded district and the process would be repeated.

Baptists have a right to be pleased with the position taken on Indian rights by Roger Williams, one of their brilliant colonial leaders. Williams was a Welshman, trained at Cambridge, and early converted to Baptist principles. Indeed, Professor 

William W. Sweet says that American Baptists did not come historically from the English Baptists; they date from Roger Williams. While a minister of the Congregational church at Salem, Williams became a convert to Baptist views and immediately began to preach those doctrines which led to his expulsion from the colony of Massachusetts, in 

January, 1636. In addition to his declarations in favor of religious liberty, he stated that the English king could not grant a patent to American land, since it belonged to the Indians; and he refused to serve the church at Boston because it would not sever its relations with the Church of England. The General Court declared him to be schismatic and voted by a small majority to banish him. With a few followers, he went to the country of the Narragansett Indians, now Rhode Island, bought land from the Indians, entered into an agreement of friendship with them, and established a new colony on the principle of religious freedom. 

In dealing with the Indians, he was invariably humane and just, and, in return, the Indians treated him kindly. Although he had been expelled from Massachusetts, he literally turned the other

cheek by using his influence with the Narragansetts to prevent them from attacking its settlements. 

Peter Minuit, the Dutch colonizer of Manhattan, and William Penn, the Quaker proprietor of

Pennsylvania, employed the same Indian policy and met with equal success. But these examples were not generally followed by other colonists, nor by federal officials after the revolution. Generally, the more opportunistic policy of force was used. Again and again the land-hungry settlers occupied the Indian lands and their government forced the red men to move out. In these early relations, the typical settler point of view was, “a good Indian is a dead one," and he hunted the native in the forest as he did a wild beast. In time, this led border people to doubt that an Indian had a soul! This point of view greatly handicapped the work of the first missionaries, who sought to create public sentiment in favor of their work. Mission boards were slow in furnishing funds when churches looked with askance on proposals to Christianize the Indians.

PART II BAPTIST MISSIONARIES AMONG THE INDIANS 

As early as the colonial period of American history the Baptist churches were missionary in spirit. In 1796 a Baptist missionary society was formed in New York, and six years later a similar movement was launched in Massachusetts, obedient to Christ's injunction to "Go ye therefore and teach all nations" (Matt. 28:19). Colonial Baptists soon recognized that religion is a singular commodity-the more it is exported, the more we have it at home! When once the movement had gained momentum in these areas funds for missions were available. From Serampore, Bengal, Dr. William Carey wrote that God had wonderfully stirred up the whole religious world to give to missions. Even the Baptist women of the eastern seaboard states supplemented regular church offerings through the efforts of their Female Mite and 

Cent Societies.

Foreign missions were first given large support by American Baptists in the organization of the 

General Missionary Convention for Foreign Mis sions, at Philadelphia, in May, 1814, a movement headed by Dr. Richard Furman of Charleston, South Carolina, with Rev. Thomas Baldwin of

Here was a representative of a race that had been the Indian's nemesis. 4. A PROPOSED INDIAN CANAAN 

The more McCoy encountered the destructive influence of whiskey and those who peddled it, the more he was convinced of the futility of missionary enterprises among these Northwestern tribes. General and habitual drunkenness prevailed to such an extent that rapine, murder and crime were of daily occurrence. At times he was even afraid of leaving his wife and children unprotected, for several murders were committed near the mission. On one occasion a drunken 

Indian almost murdered his little daughter but fortunately an Indian student came to her rescue. 

The faithful missionary finally concluded that the Indians' only hope was in removal to new homes in the West, beyond the reach of the traders. He made more than one trip to Detroit to confer with the territorial governor, Lewis Cass, and then to 

Washington to advocate removal to Secretary of

War John C. Calhoun, and to members of Congress. To each of these he told of Indian wrongs, poverty and plight. Unless the Federal Government came to their relief immediately and removed them to the West, the tribes would soon be extin-

guished. All gave him an attentive and sympathetic hearing, although some members of Congress argued that the cost of removal was prohibitive. 

McCoy's plan was not new. In organizing the 

Louisiana Territory, Congress had enacted that "The President of the United States is hereby authorized to stipulate with any Indian tribes owning land on the east side of the Mississippi and residing thereon, for an exchange of lands the property of the United States on the west and settle thereon." Then after the explorers, Zebulon

Pike (1806-7) and Stephen H. Long (1820), had said that the Great Plains was a desert and only suitable for Indian occupancy, the Federal Government had immediately launched the removal of all Eastern tribes and bands thereto. 

Indeed, as early as 1808, federal commissioners had persuaded the Osages to cede practically all of the territory now embraced in Missouri, and that of Arkansas north of the Arkansas River. 

And in 1818 and 1824 other treaties with the same tribe, and with the Quapaws, made available what is now Oklahoma and much of western Arkansas for reservations upon which eastern Indians could be settled.

When once the way was cleared in the West, the actual removal of the eastern tribes came quickly. The Shawnees, Weas, and Pienkeshaws were assigned a reservation in northeastern Kansas;

Missouri and Kansas rivers, their reservation extending twenty-three miles along the Missouri; the Ottawas were assigned a seven-mile square site south of the Shawnees; and the Pottawatomies were located temporarily near Fort Leavenworth. (Most of these tribes were later removed to Indian 

Territory.) Missionaries Lykens, Ira D. Blanchard, 

Meeker and Simerwell accompanied the Northwestern Indians to their new homes and worked faithfully among them. Lykens and Meeker were particularly helpful in teaching them to walk in the "new road." They translated into the Indian languages, by the "new method," religious tracts, parts of the Scriptures and schoolbooks; and the 

Indians responded by studying them zealously. 

Lykens also edited the Shawanoe Sun, said to have been the first newspaper ever published entirely in an Indian language.

The lives of the McCoys were to become inspiring examples for the many Indian missionaries who followed them. After their removal to the 

West, McCoy became the secretary to the American Indian Mission Association (Baptist supported), for which he labored as zealously as in the missionary field. He died on June 21, 1846, from an illness brought on by exposure in severe weather, and his devoted wife followed him to the grave five years later. They had literally forgotten those things that were behind and had

ever pressed on toward the mark of the high calling. No two souls could better qualify for the honor of America's foremost missionaries.

The most conspicuous monument to the memory of the McCoys was the marked advance in civilization and religion of those tribes whom they served. 

They had found the Indiana and Illinois Indians hedged in on every side by land-hungry whites and designing whiskey peddlers. Indian villages reeked in filth and squalor, and their inhabitants were despondent, debauched, and starving without hope of a better day. McCoy and his wife first taught them that Christ was the light of the world; then they showed them how to spin, weave, sew, clear away fields and plant corn, and the many other things necessary to advance them on the road to civilization. Obviously, there was much back-sliding, much uncertainty, as the Indians moved forward. Sometimes the missionaries were so discouraged as to feel the utter hopelessness of success, but their Christian zeal, born of hope and faith, revived them and drove them on to final success.

PART THREE BAPTIST WORK AMONG THE SOUTHERN TRIBES 1. EARLY CHEROKEE MISSIONS 

Soon after the dawn of the nineteenth century Baptist missionaries appeared in the Cherokee country, among whom was Rev. Thomas Johnson of Georgia. But it was not until 1817, as mentioned in Part Two, that the Mission Board formally confirmed the appointment of Rev. Alexander Posey, thus initiating a denominational program. In the previous year, Rev. Luther Rice had met Posey, while traveling through the South, and had held a conference with him relative to the starting of Baptist work among these Indians. 

Posey convinced him that it was not only desirable but that it would pay large dividends. The result was that Secretary Staughton gave Posey a "goahead" approval of the Board.

The Cherokees had the advantage of a more advanced culture over those tribes among whom McCoy had worked. Some of their leaders were not only literate but demonstrated a high quality of leadership, and urged their people to learn the ways of the white man's civilization so that they could thus prove their right of national existence. Already they had advanced far in their civilization. They lived in well built log cabins, 60

had fields of corn and vegetables, raised horses and cattle, carried on certain industrial pursuits, and enjoyed a tribal government not greatly inferior to that of neighboring whites. They composed one of the so-called Five Civilized Tribes, and were, indeed, the most progressive one.

The Cherokee country was a beautiful land of fine mountains, rippling streams, extensive forests and fertile valleys, lying within northern Georgia, southeastern Tennessee and western North Carolina. It had the advantage of sufficient farm lands and abundant wild game. Here the Indian could satisfy his propensity to hunt and fish and at the same time enjoy the unfolding life of the village. 

In council meetings, in social relations, or while stalking the deer or fishing along the stream, he found contentment. Yet always before him like a threatening cloud was the ever swelling tide of white frontiersmen slowly advancing from the east.

Again and again he had ceded land to the intruders until now there was no more to cede, unless he accepted the white man's edict to move to a new home west of the Mississippi River, and this he was reluctant to do. It is not surprising, therefore, that the Cherokee was suspicious of every move made by his white foe, and this was a natural barrier which the missionaries must break down before effective work could be instituted. 

Posey was not dismayed by this lack of enthusiasm on the part of Cherokee leaders for the

The Creeks and Choctaws were others of the 

Five Civilized Tribes among whom early Baptist missionaries worked. Already Moravian missionaries had labored among the Cherokees, Creeks and Choctaws, thus paving the way for Compere's mission. The Creeks, according to a contemporary, were yet in Egyptian darkness, in spite of this preliminary work. "Their firm attachment to the customs of their fathers, their extreme mental apathy and physical indolence, their mutual quarrels and bitter animosities: all combined to present a formidable barrier to the introduction of Christianity." And added to this missionary handicap was the baleful influence of whiskey peddlers, much as had been the case among the Northwestern tribes.

But Compere was not daunted. In 1822 he established the Withington Mission at Tuchee-

Records of the Board for the first three or four years carry little information about its success. 

They reveal only that Compere was encouraged in his work. Twelve students were studying arithmetic, grammar and geography. John Davis, an "intelligent and sober-minded" Creek youth, was serving as interpreter. 

One should not conclude that the Federal Government had been entirely neglectful of its obligations to the Creeks. In 1827 Colonel McKenney, Creek agent, negotiated an understanding with the Indians whereby they would be furnished $1,000 annually to expend on education; and he visited the Withington mission, expressed his approval of Compere's work and of his and Mrs. Compere's fitness for dealing with the Indians. 

Compere was greatly discouraged, however, because of Creek indolence. Proud warriors argued that it was degrading for their children to do the kind of work required of them by the missionaries. 

They said that this would reduce them to slavery. 

But the faithful missionary continued his efforts and in 1827 had the satisfaction of witnessing the conversion of Interpreter Davis, a Creek girl, and a colored woman.

3. THE TRAIL OF TEARS 

The problem of removing the Five Civilized Tribes to new homes in what is now Oklahoma was as complicated as that relating to the Northwestern tribes. The Choctaws were the first of the Five Civilized Tribes to agree to removal, although the Cherokees of the Hills, about 5,000 strong, had entered into such an agreement as early as 1817. Pushmataha and other Choctaw chiefs met Generals Andrew Jackson and Thomas

Hinds at Doak's Stand, on the Natchez Trace, in 1820 and agreed upon general removal terms, which were made more explicit twelve years later. 

At this later date, Colonel Le Flore exacted from reluctant Federal commissioners a removal sum of $50,000 and $20,000 to be paid annually for twenty years. Moreover, each of four chiefs were to receive as a present, $500, and four others

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians $250. But by treaties of 1832, 1837 and 1855 the Choctaws surrendered a part of their princely holdings south of the Canadian River to their relatives, the Chickasaws.

In 1823 Governor George M. Troup of Georgia had asked President John Quincy Adams to remove the Creeks and other Indians holding state lands to western lands in keeping with a Federal promise of 1802. This Adams agreed to do, and promptly the Federal commissioners, Duncan G.

Campbell and James Meriwether, were sent southward to negotiate with the Creeks. But before their arrival, the Creeks met in council and voted not to cede any land and in addition threatened to take the life of any chief violating their agreement. Nevertheless, Campbell and Meriwether persuaded William McIntosh and a few lesser chiefs to sign such an agreement, at Indian 

Springs, on March 7, 1825. The treaty was repudiated by the Creek Council and the sentence of death was imposed on the offending chiefs as previously threatened. Then in the first part of the next year a new agreement was signed with the Secretary of War whereby for a consideration of $217,000 and an annuity of $20,000 they agreed to exchange their eastern land for a new home in what is now Oklahoma, south of the Cherokee holdings and between the two Canadians. 

Meanwhile the Cherokees had considered exchanging their eastern land for a new country

in the west as early as 1808 when a delegation of their chiefs made such a proposal to President 

Thomas Jefferson. The president was favorably inclined to the move, but other problems soon arose to divert his attention from the Indians' need. But in 1817 a band of 5,000 Indians, known as the Cherokees of the Hills, signed a removal treaty and moved to a choice range in the northwestern part of present Arkansas, between the Arkansas and White rivers. Encroaching white settlers, however, caused them to move again a few years later to northeastern Oklahoma and they were given an outlet to the buffalo plains farther west.

We know now that those Cherokees who yet lived in the east could have been saved the untold suffering and hundreds of deaths had they chosen to accept this Oklahoma assignment as this emigrant band did. John Ross, a Cherokee fullblood and conservative, fought removal to the end. He was principal chief of the tribe and commanded a majority following. Boudinot, Major and John

Ridge, Stand Watie and other liberal chiefs were finally committed to removal, and this brought on a bitter feud that was to have its murderous repercussions long after their removal to Oklahoma, in 1838 taking the lives of Boudinot and the two Ridges. Under terms of the treaty of New 

Echota, on December 29, 1835, the Cherokees were to surrender all their eastern holdings and

But still they clung to their old homes. 

In 1838, when they had refused to leave their old country, General Winfield Scott and a large force of Federal troops moved into their midst. 

Like cattle, the unfortunate Cherokees were gathered up from their villages and farms and herded into concentration camps. The fall of 1838 had come and few of the Indians had made preparations to withstand the rigors of winter that was. now upon them. Women and children were scantily attired and without blankets upon which to sleep at night, and many were without shoes. The soldiers dealt with the discomfited Indians so harshly that Ross appealed to General Scott to permit the 

Cherokee leaders to direct their own removal and his request was reluctantly granted. What then followed was the terrible journey over the "Trail of Tears." "Man's extremity is God's opportunity." For three years after the treaty of New Echota, Chief Ross and his people had bitterly opposed removal. "It is doubtful if white people," writes Grant 

Foreman, "with their readier adaptability, can understand the sense of grief and desolation that overwhelmed the Indians when they were compelled to leave all these [homes, fields, burial grounds and villages] behind forever; and begin the long journey toward the setting sun which they call the Trail of Tears."

As soon as Scott arrived at New Echota, in May, 1838, he issued a proclamation to the Cherokees, telling them that he must use force if they resisted removal. "I am an old warrior," he warned, "and have been present at many a scene of slaughter, but spare me, I beseech you, the horror of witnessing the destruction of the Cherokees." There can be little doubt that Scott was sincere in this appeal. Yet he tempered sternness with mercy, for he issued an order to his troops that "every possible kindness, compatible with the necessity of removal, must be shown by the troops. 99 Evan Jones was yet the faithful Baptist missionary among these distressed people, and during all these days of stress and conflict he had fearlessly preached the doctrine of Christ. Nor had the missionaries' efforts been unavailing. Stalwart 

Christian natives supplemented their efforts and called upon their people to look to God for help. 

Among these were Jesse Bushyhead, Stephen Foreman, John Wicliffe and Oganaya. Bushyhead was perhaps the outstanding convert. He had united with the Baptist church after reading the Bible independently. Then he had organized a church among his own people in 1830 or 1831. He was the product of the Candy Creek Mission School, and in 1835 helped to organize the Amohee Church on the Ocoee River where he worked with Rev.

PART FOUR REKINDLING RELIGIOUS FIRES 

The Federal government's harsh removal program had been a trial by fire for the Five Civilized Tribes, many members of whom looked with resentment and suspicion on the efforts of the missionaries. What good can come from the white man? they bitterly inquired. Let's turn away from his God! He has brought us only harm! 

But others had found Christ a tower of strength, a rock in a time of storm. To these, Christianity had been a vital force for good, a ray of hope in their dark hour; and they looked with interest on rekindling religious fires in their new country. 

Their missionary friends had been true and steadfast, accepting whatever hardship and deprivation was necessary in order to continue their work. 

The Indians responded whole-heartedly to their appeal, their emotional outpourings constituting a fertile field for the seed of the Gospel, a field that is even yet "white unto the harvest." And this new opportunity was seized upon by the Southern Baptist Convention (organized in 1845) which set up a Board of Domestic Missions which later took over the work of the Indian Mission 

Association in this new field.

PART FIVE THE INDIAN MISSION FIELD OF TODAY 

The American Indians have traveled far on the road of civilization since they first met evangelical missionaries. Primitive ways are fast being supplanted by orderly, constructive habits, and Indian children by thousands are attending both their own and the white man's schools. Superstition, rites and ceremonies, too, are finding less and less use in Indian life. No people in so short a time have made more pronounced progress in the way of civilized nations. Those tribes and bands whose warriors frequented the war trails as late as the 1870's are now herding cattle and sheep, cultivating fields, and engaging in various industrial pursuits. Today, Indians ride to town in automobiles, dress much as do their white neighbors, attend rodeos, athletic contests, movies, revivals, and take a lively interest in public affairs. 

Christianity has been in large part responsible for this transformation. The missionary's Gospel of "peace on earth, good will toward men," and of the Golden Rule, has been effective. The love of Christ has been an inspiring urge in the heart of the red man, driving him toward a better life. 

Christian chiefs and leaders have worked as enthusiastically to propagate Christianity among their people as have the missionaries. 105

The so-called "blanket" Indian tribes are the

Rev. Worthington has recently written me about

The Indians gave $14,302.97 to missions! As to Indian sincerety in worship, he adds: "Indians are great believers in prayer. It has been most remarkable to me to witness the faith that was manifest in some Indians I have worked with. Well do I remember one night just before my service was to begin. Our baby was quite ill, and I hesitated going out to the church at all. Mrs. Worthington said, 'Go ahead. If I need you I will send some one for you.' Just as I had finished reading my

Scripture lesson, she sent for me. I explained to them why I was leaving and asked them to go ahead with the service. I got a physician out and the baby was presently resting. About mid-night 

I heard some one knocking and I went to the door to find one of my Christian Indians there. 

I asked him what he wanted, and he said: 'I want to know how the baby is?' I told him the baby was better, and then he said: 'I knew it was better.' I said, 'How did you know it was better?'

He replied, 'We have been over at the church praying for it, and I know Jesus answers prayer.' 

As I walked away I said in my heart, 'Lord increase my faith.""

3. MISSIONARY WORK IN NEW MEXICO AND ARIZONA 

999 New Mexico, "the Land of Enchantment," was first visited by the Spanish conquerer, Coronado, in 1540, although it was not settled until fiftyeight years later. First at San Juan, then at Santa 

Fe, the Spaniards planted their Catholic missions and sought to win the native Pueblos and wild Indians to their faith. But for many decades they made but little progress, and even today the Indians have fused their own ceremonials with Catholic practices. With the appearance of Bap-

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians tists and Protestants in this field, Catholic priests were active in their work against them. A government officials, writing on July 29, 1851, stated that a "Mr. Nicholson from the Pittsburg Conference [Methodist] was stationed at Santa Fe." "He is a glorious fellow," the writer said, "and if any individual could do any good to this population he would be the man. But with all his labors, he has not only not got a single convert, but he cannot get a dozen hearers out of the whole population, Mexican and Americans." Yet in the face of this discouragement, "he continues to preach, sometimes to ten hearers-sometimes to five. Reverend Mr. Kephart, whom you know, is my room-mate. He hitches team with Nicholson and I think has about abandoned the field in despair, although he continues to do all in his power.

There are besides these two, two Baptist preachers whose success is just equal to their co-laborers. We made an effort to raise a temperance meeting on Sunday night last, but 'nobody didn't come!"

But in spite of all these handicaps Baptist missionaries stayed on and as the Anglo-American population of New Mexico increased, there was a corresponding increase in the Baptist work. And at present, fine Baptist churches grace the cities and towns of this state. Nevertheless, Indian pueblos and reservations have stubbornly resisted all efforts of the missionaries until comparatively

recent times. But today, fields of opportunity are opening. Government Indian schools, hospitals and some of the pueblos have thrown open their doors to the missionaries.

4. THE NEW CHALLENGE 

In the foregoing pages we have read the story of the development of Baptist missions among the 

American Indians. One hundred and twenty-five years have elapsed since Rev. Isaac McCoy established his first mission among the Northwestern tribes and since Rev. Alexander Posey worked among the Cherokees. Southern Baptists have been in the field ninety-eight years. Many heroes of the Cross-McCoy, Posey, Compere, Evan Jones, Ficklin, O'Bryant, Buckner and Murrow-have been the Christian beacon lights all these dark years. And with them were many native mission-

Baptist Missions Among the American Indians aries-Gosa, Bushyhead, Oganaya, Jumper, Mc- Intosh and others. All of these were seed planted in the garden of life! What a harvest! Today, there are upward of 20,000 Indian Baptists out of a total population of 225,000 within the area served by Southern Baptists. We have in the field 

73 missionaries, 58 of whom are Indians, and the missionaries report for the past year 459 conversions, 246 baptisms and 288 received by letter! 

These heartening figures show that a good beginning has been made in Christianizing the Indians. But there is much that remains to be done. Let us remember that there are a total of more than 225,000 Indians in that part of the United States served by the Southern Baptists. 

Hundreds of new missionaries should be sent into the various Indian fields and two thousand new churches should be organized. These should be followed by schools, hospitals and other kinds of institutions that would go far toward reducing the Gospel of Christ to practical terms. 

The new challenge of missionary service carries with it self-sacrifice, heroic service, and sustained creative endeavor. He who would find his life in Indian service must lose it! American Indians by tens of thousands can be won for Christ. This has been proven by the devoted services of McCoy, Posey, Jones and others of the nineteenth century, as well as by many missionaries of our own century; but white Christians must prove to thousands

of yet doubting Indians that Christianity is a living force for good in the world. Until the red men can see realistically how the Christian religion is superior to their own cults, they will continue to seek the favors of their Great Spirit by rites and ceremonies. The task of Baptist missionaries, then, calls for the highest order of Christian living and service. The Baptist worker must offer his life and talents without reservation unto Christ! 

And the "field is white unto the harvest." Writing in 1941 Rev. Stumph stated that among the 

Indians of New Mexico a very large work is done by the Presbyterians, Methodists, Christian Reform and others. "In nearly every Navajo community we find representatives of one of these denomi nations. Because of their large resources, it is difficult for Baptists, with no equipment, to cope with them. The Alamo Navajos are in the central part of the state. It is here that we have our only 

Navajo work. This is an open field, untouched by other missionaries." Miss Wilma Bucy, a field worker, found in Arizona a similar situation. She said that there were but 3,123 Baptists in the whole state, and for 53,000 Indians there were but two missionaries! An Apache chief sent word to Rev. Frazier, "Come preach us Gospel. My people ready to hear it when the Baptist preach it."
