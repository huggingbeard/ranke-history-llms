The power to win the loyal attachment of thousands of followers is not the sole prerogative of the spiritual leader. Unrighteous but magnetic orators have often led a nation to its doom. The

Early Christianity was spread throughout the Graeco-Roman world largely by anonymous workers and travelers who had never seen Jesus. 

Someone would tell the "good news" to a friend who would, in turn, pass it on to others after discovering how transforming the message had been to his own drab and incomplete life. Who first brought the "good news" to Rome, Alexandria, and other great centers of antiquity? No one can answer that question today. Perhaps an apostle who knew the Christ, but more likely some sailor or merchant or perhaps even a slave. 

Joseph the Prophet started with his own family and convinced his father and mother, then his brothers and sisters that what he said was true and that it was good. From them it spread to their neighbors, to Ohio, to Canada, the Middle West and from there to Europe, South America, and the islands of the Pacific.

A Restorer Among the "Restorationists"

THE PROPHET'S FRIENDS as well as his en emies have been active for over a century in pointing out the sharp differences which separate his teachings from those of the major Christian denominations. However, a casual dispassionate examination of his theology reveals that most of his basic ideas, isolated and taken one by one, have been championed by some Christian leader in modern times, not to mention the New 

Testament writers and the early Christian fathers.

It is only after one examines the combination of his teachings and observes the ecclesiastical government through which they function that the marked line is seen which separates him from the reformers who preceded him and the "restorationists" who were his contemporaries. 

The Prophet taught that Jesus was the Messiah, that He rose from the dead, that He would return to earth again, and that all men must be baptized. So had thousands taught and millions believed since the first Christian century.

That a man must have priesthood to administer the ordinances of the Gospel was held by the Catholics, Anglicans, and others. His doctrine of preexistence was as old as Plato and as modern as the poet Wordsworth, who was the Prophet's contemporary.' Most Christians were ready to concede the point that Jesus had lived an ante-mortal life even though they denied the preexistence of all mortal men and women. Conrad Beissel (D. 1768) had baptized the living for the dead and the Irvingites (the Catholic Apostolic Church organized in 1830) had "apostles" and "prophets" and made claims to the "spiritual gifts" reported in the New Testament. George Fox (D. 1691) had established the Society of Friends on the foundation that all good Christians, men and women, could receive divine revelation. His doctrine of "the inner light" was also held by others. And shortly before the birth of Fox, John Robinson, the aged pastor of the Pilgrims, had given his followers this parting counsel as they set sail for the New World. "He charged us . . . .. to follow him no further than he followed Christ; and if God should reveal anything to us by any other instrument of his, to be as ready to receive it as ever we were

to receive any truth by his ministry; for he was very confident the Lord had more truth and light yet to break forth out of his holy Word.' 991A Had the Prophet so desired he could have laid claim to a fellowship of ideas with many of the great Christian leaders who had preceded him. Roger Williams (D. 1683), “the New 

England firebrand," reached the same conclusion from a study of the New Testament and Christian history that Joseph Smith arrived at two hundred years later with respect to the apostate condition of all Christendom. After moving from the 

Anglican to the Congregational, to the Baptist, to the Seekers, Williams said that the Christian churches of his day were so corrupt that there could "be no recovery out of that apostacy till 

Christ shall send forth new apostles to plant churches anew." Thomas Jefferson wrote to a friend in 1820, the year of the Prophet's first vision: "I hope that the genuine and simple religion of Jesus Christ may be restored, for it hath become so muffled up in mysteries that it is concealed from the vulgar eyes.' 99

Campbell believed that an essential blue print of the ancient apostolic church could be found in the New Testament, and proceded to restore" primitive Christianity with the Christian scriptures as his guide and authority. The church which he organized called itself "The Disciples of Christ," hoping to show by its name that it was not 'sectarian," but the true church of Christ within which all Christians might unite. 

The Disciples believed in baptism by immersion for the remission of sins and their favorite passage of scripture was from the Book of Revelations: "And I saw another angel fly in the midst of 66

heaven, having the everlasting gospel to preach unto them that dwell on the earth, and to every nation, and kindred, and tongue, and people, saying with a loud voice, fear God and give glory to him; for the hour of his judgment is come: and worship him that made heaven, and earth, and the sea, and the fountains of waters." They interpreted this scripture as referring to the restoration of the ancient gospel through their instrumentality. They believed that they had much to offer America and the world at large, they have become the largest Christian Church to originate in the New World. Many Americans and Europeans a century ago were eager to follow a leader who could give evidence that he was a "restorer."

Let us now examine some of the distinguishing features of the Church of Christ as it was established under the direction of the Prophet and later developed by his successors. And it should be recognized at the outset that many of the characteristics which are to be cited consist of a greater emphasis or a more detailed development of some item by the Prophet and his followers rather than an original idea or unique practice unknown to all other Christian groups.

Alexander Campbell and other "restorationists" opposed the Prophet and his movement on many grounds, but chiefly on his claims of divine authority which went beyond any New Testament call or that "authority" which all Christians associate with moral living. The Prophet laid claim to a Priesthood, a sacerdotal authority which had been bestowed upon him by resurrected apostles who once served in the primitive church. Strange as it appears to anyone acquainted with the spirit of the American frontier and the religious traditions of New England where Catholic papacy and Protestant episcopacy were usually thought of as the instrumentalities of the devil, the Prophet, with such a background, insisted that without priesthood all baptisms were inefficacious. He said that all Christendom was merely a conglomeration of ethical societies, that not one of the numerous denominations of his day was the Church of Christ because not one had the divine authority which Jesus possessed and which He bestowed upon His ancient disciples. Campbell and his associates attempted to restore the ancient church by using the New Testament as an infallible guide. Joseph the 

Prophet claimed that through him God had re-

stored that ancient authority which Roger Williams had sought in vain among the churches of his day.

Joseph and his brethren could lay no claim to diplomas from any theological seminary of their day nor priestly ordination from the Anglican or Catholic churches. But they laid claim with great vigor to a divine commission direct from Jesus Christ through Peter, James, and

John of olden times, and they spoke as men having authority.

These young apostles of the nineteenth century opposed Catholicism wherever they encountered it, declaring that it had "transgressed the laws, changed the ordinance, broken the everlasting covenant.' "And whenever they met

Many modern Christian writers have attempted to belittle religion in all of its organized forms, and have completely ignored the offices found in the literature of ancient Judaism and the early Christian Church. It is true that the Prophet 

Joseph was chiefly concerned with restoring the same power that existed in the primitive church, but he was nonetheless aware that "as a bad trellis can ruin a good vine, or a poor government bedevil civic community, so ecclesiastical institutions can either work havoc with religion or give it support and opportunity.' 18 Before April 6, 1830, the Prophet headed a vital organism; on 

June 27, 1844 he bequeathed to his followers a living dynamic Church which had been created for the salvation of the people and not the people for its institutional power and glory.

Perhaps it has been sufficiently emphasized that Joseph Smith took the stand that above all else he was a Prophet. However, it should be

clear that he did not limit the prophetic calling to himself and that he did not limit its function. to the popular conception of mere foretelling. 

Neither the Prophet nor his colleagues wished to be classed with Mother Shipton, Nostradamus, or the lesser lights who from time to time have become famous as foretellers. True, the Prophet and others were credited with cases of remarkable foretelling, but primarily they were forth-tellers. 

In Old Testament times the great prophets were chiefly concerned in revealing God's will to their particular generation. The written word was revered by the ancient Jew and the early Christians, but both groups looked to their living prophets for "the will of the Lord" to them. 

Campbell and most of the other "restorationists” either did not know what an important place was held by Prophets in the primitive church, or they were afraid to attempt the restoration of an activity which was so obviously dependent upon the Diety. Who were eligible for membership within the Church which the Prophet, his two brothers and their three friends established by law on 

April 6, 1830? Everyone who would comply with a few simple but profound principles and practices. First, there was to be evidence of repentance, and faith in the Lord Jesus Christ. This was to be followed by baptism by immersion for the remission of sins, regardless of the position the applicant may have held up to that time in some Christian communion, because "old covenants have I caused to be done away in this thing; and this is a new and everlasting covenant, even that which was from the beginning."'15 And this rite was then to be followed by the laying on of hands for the reception of the Holy Ghost, all in harmony with the ancient scriptural order and forms.

At an early period the Prophet began to urge another qualification for Church membership in addition to a new direction of the mind and will and a turning away from the evils listed in Hebrew and Christian scriptures; he began to insist that full membership entailed acceptance

of another ancient ideal also, namely, a physical body unharmed by the use of drugs, narcotics, and harmful diets. The Prophet may not have known that the word "salvation" can be traced to a Latin word meaning "health"; but one thing is certain, he was an advocate of a clean, healthy mind and spirit, housed in a clean, sturdy physical body. “It is the will of God," he said, “that man should repent and serve Him in health and in the strength and power of his mind, in order to secure His blessing. 'His revelation on health did not go in the direction of the food faddists of his day and their numerous successors. Its twenty-one verses point out several specific things to avoid and several definite foods to use, but in the main it sets forth "a principle" which is to be "adapted to the weakest of all saints." And the promise of "health" and "treasures of knowledge" is given to those who comply with the principles set forth.18 158 While strict on the "fundamentals" as essential for membership within the Church, the Prophet did not champion the doctrine of “salvation and sanctification" as popularly interpreted by many Christians. He did not claim to be a sinless man, and well knew that the rapidly grow-

IN 1815, the year that Napoleon was finally defeated at Waterloo, a mother of eight children with but two cents in her purse drove into the town of Palmyra in western New York. The oldest child, Alvin, was a youth of sixteen, the youngest was Catherine, aged three. A few months later the driver of the team was to become the mother of another son. This pioneer woman was Lucy Mack Smith. Her husband had preceded his family to Palmyra from Norwich, Vermont, hoping to make this, the seventh move since marriage, their final one.

Among the children in the wagon of Mother 

Smith was Joseph, ten years of age; he was her fourth born, and named after his father. At this time he was still lame from a painful operation on his leg. The physicians had been compelled to chisel away part of the bone in order to save his life after an attack of typhus fever. 

Never was Mother Smith to forget that gruesome operation performed in her home. There had been

no anesthetic; the boy had refused to be tied to the bed or to drink the alcoholic drug which was offered him, stating that he would “tough it out” if his father would hold him in his arms and his mother depart beyond the sound of his voice.

The Smiths were a part of that great stream of migration away from the thin soil of New England, and the commercial depression which followed the War of 1812. This family had known better days, but now it was one of a multitude of economically bankrupt families seeking new homes in western New York and the valley of the Ohio. 

Another American family, the Lincolns, were moving into Indiana at about this same time from the state of Kentucky. There were to be many similarities between the lives of Abraham Lincoln and Joseph Smith. In 1816, when the 

Smiths were clearing the trees from their new farm near Palmyra and building their first house, Abe Lincoln, as a boy of seven, was living in a rude shelter of poles, backed by a huge fallen log, with the southern face open to the weather.”¹

Neither Lincoln nor the Prophet was to receive a formal schooling, yet both were to become wellinformed men and leaders wherever they went.

Both were to settle in Southern Illinois in their manhood, one to build Nauvoo, the largest city of the state, and the other to vote in the state legislature for its liberal charter. Both were to have several intimate friends in common, including the powerful Stephen A. Douglas who will always be remembered in history because his life touched the lives of Lincoln and the Prophet.

Both were to detest human slavery, yet object to the violent methods of the "abolitionists". Both were to aspire to the Presidency. The Prophet had his friends put him forward for president in much the same spirit that Peter Cooper the great inventor and philanthropist placed his own name at the head of the National Greenback party thirty-two years later -to place his ideas effectively before the nation. Perhaps neither expected to be elected. Lincoln was to become 

America's greatest President, the world's greatest humanitarian; Joseph Smith was to become a great modern Prophet. One was to fall at the hands of an assassin, the other before the murderous fire of a mob with painted faces. Both were maligned in life, and well-nigh worshipped after death.

The plan further provides for perpetuating this equality. People are stewards only over their possessions which are to be known as stewardships, or inheritances, but each steward is to have a deed to his stewardship. Those who have surplus property, i.e., more than is required to provide a frugal living for the family, are to consecrate it by deed to the church, for the benefit of the poor. "Surplus production beyond family needs from each stewardship is to be turned over to the Bishop's Storehouse. Equal according to their wants and needs. Those who are either excommunicated from the church or themselves leave it, keep the inheritances deeded to them, but do not receive back the surplus that has been consecrated to the poor. In order for us to understand the chief contribution of the Prophet to his people, let us consider further the subject of current agnosticism: In three recent books on religion, written by leaders in the Protestant ministry, the hope of personal survival beyond the grave does not definitely appear. A discussion of the hope of a physical resurrection, as held by the Primitive Church, will break up a gathering of modern Christian teachers as quickly as it did the assembled Greeks who gathered on Mar's Hill to hear St. Paul. On the latter occasion "When they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked: and others said. We will hear thee again of this matter." Another recent book by a Christian leader does not mention the resurrection in its index and refers to immortality but briefly with this warning to the reader: "It is therefore important to maintain a decent measure of restraint in expressing the Christian hope.' 10

Since the great war in Europe began with its tragic daily harvest of countless lives, there has appeared a book on Christianity purporting to be an "inquiry into its nature and truth." This book was chosen by the judges as the best of the 214 manuscripts which were submitted from thirty-one states and nine foreign countries. The author received a cash prize of fifteen thousand dollars, and many honors for his scholarly work. 

Yet in the subject index of the book the words "death," "immortality," or "resurrection" do not appear.

Such is the silence and the agnosticism met by a suffering world as it mourns over its battle slain sons. More men are in arms today than walked the earth a few milleniums past; more blood has been spilled in warfare during the past few decades than flowed in the veins of all the Europeans who lived in the days of Luther. Yet great areas within Christendom are barren of any vital hope for the soldiers who face great danger or for the lonely parents who grieve at home. 

Out of the suffering of World War I came philosophies of despair and disillusionment which have embittered whole nations. Agnosticism and atheism, which were once the expressions of

ennui from the idle rich or the whimperings of the weak who could not rise from the blow of some personal loss, became the belief of millions. 

This was largely due to the fact that the Christian churches had ceased to speak of God and immortality with conviction. The middle-aged discarded "Thanatopsis," and the youth never learned its great lines:

Large divisions within Christianity have long since surrendered the great heritage of faith which was the origin of Christianity, itself. They have little to offer a suffering world which cannot be found in any naturalistic philosopher's handbook. The following accounts are typical of the experiences and reflections of many Christians who are engaged in warfare: Said a sailor: "From the ship's lights I have seen hundreds of men struggling in the waters of the Pacific, and we could not rescue them. As they threshed about in the swirling black water churned by the swift moving ships I though of a giant ant bed when stirred by a stick, and these questions came to my mind, 'Are men so different from ants? Is there really a God?'"'

Said a colored soldier whose duty it was to search out the slain of his nation and give them decent burial: "I have seen the bodies of men from six different nations litter the same battle field and I thought as I gathered the dead, 'Is the brotherhood of man only that of cattle which are butchered on the same day?'" "I have helped take the blasted bodies of infants, the aged, and tender youth from the same building after the bombers passed over," said a volunteer worker, "and I thought, 'does Jesus Christ, the lover of little ones, see this?'"

To the countless lonely hearts of the mothers, sisters and wives, of the fathers, brothers and sons who mourn the violent deaths of earth's fair youth who have been slain in recent years, the questions arise: "If a man die, shall he live again?" Is the life of man more than blood and brains? Or is "Life but a walking shadow, a poor player that struts and frets his hour upon the stage and then is heard no more . . .A tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing?"'¹¹

What once seemed like a virgin spring flowing from Mount Zion in Jerusalem, now appears to be but part of a great river whose chief branches can be traced to Mount Sinai and Mount Olympas, with a thousand small tributaries winding their tenuous ways into psychological and historical regions which I have no desire to explore." 

The Christian student says, "What shall I do with Osiris, Attis, and Mithra? of their tragic deaths followed by their happy triumphs over the grave? And so the vexing, faith-destroying questions arise before many a truth-loving, educated Christian. He is separated from the historic origins of his faith by pre-science creeds, an ancient psychology and by nineteen hundred years of time; 11

he is connected to it by fragmentary records, written in an ancient tongue. He finds that the old Christian stories, unsupported by modern revelation, carry little weight to his mind; the "acids of modernity” have etched composite and distorted pictures of the once plain New Testament scenes.

However, there is at least one growing community of Christians which lives with that joy, and dies with that peace which accompanies an unwavering faith in a personal immortality and a world to come full of never-ending possibilities for spiritual development. These are the followers of the Prophet Joseph. They believe that “if a man die" he will live again, that "as man is God once was, and as God is, man may become," that "Adam fell that men might be; and men are that they might have joy," and that "this is life eternal" to know the true God, and Jesus Christ who was sent by Him. The Latterday Saints believe that God said, “Behold this is my work and my glory, to bring to pass immortality and eternal life of man.' 99 the

The Latter-day Saints cherish the ancient scriptures; but God, the ultimate source of their inspiration, is not mediated to them alone through

the inadequate and ever-weakening channels of history and tradition. God is greater than history, or any definition of Him. The living voice of divine authority, the current scriptures which are concerned with the problems of today and tomorrow, these come first in their interests and in their abiding loyalties; and through them the past has meaning and the future a high promise.

A great English poet, a contemporary of the 

Prophet, wrote: "I hope to meet my Pilot face to face." This might well be called the high water mark of faith in immortality as expressed by any eminent modern writer. On the other hand, the Prophet, and those who accept him as a revelator of God's plan for this age, say, "T know that I shall meet my Pilot, and all of my departed friends face to face." God is as real to a devout Latter-day Saint as the mayor of his city; life everlasting is as certain to him as tomorrow's sun. "I have support for the ancient stories in modern scriptures," he says, and then he reads, "The veil was taken from our minds, and the eyes of our understanding were opened.

Nineteen hundred years ago a young man in

His early thirties sat in an upper room in old Jerusalem with twelve close friends. He washed their feet. He "took bread and when he had given thanks, he brake it, and said, “Take, eat: this is my body, which is broken for you: this do in remembrance of me.' After the same manner also he took the cup, when he had supped, say-

ing. "This cup is the new testament in my blood: this do ye, as oft as ye drink it, in remembrance of me. "19 He then sang a hymn with His friends, after which they departed to a garden where He prepared himself by prayer for the great ordeal of death by torture. In the garden He was betrayed, delivered to His enemies, and in spite of the great legal protections, devised through centuries by Roman and Hebrew jurists to save the innocent, He was condemned to die by crucifixion. 

And who were His relentless and merciless enemies? The chief leaders of an ancient and highly moral religion. And why did they resort to bribery, perjury, and threats of blackmail to force the legal machinery of that age to execute One, the latchet of whose shoes few men have been worthy to stoop down and unloose? In the main, because He declared new revelations of truth, claimed a direct authority for His teachings, and would not submit to the authority of the dead past, by which His enemies attempted to maintain their leadership, and invalidate His.

In Jesus they saw a potential danger to their institutional privileges, and perhaps a great danger to the institution itself: "Then gathered the chief priests and the Pharisees a council, and

said, What do we? for this man doeth many miracles. If we let him thus alone all men will believe on him: and the Romans shall come and take away both our place and nation. "'20 

And how did He die? With a calm courage, born of the knowledge that death could not hold him. He withstood the physical and mental torture of His enemies and passed into the next world without surrendering His manhood and without their breaking His will. And from that death which His enemies thought would be lost to history among the thousands crucified in that age, came a turning point in history. From Him, and that little cluster of disciples who stood by Him, came the hope of the ancient world. Even the slave, who caught the vision of the resurrected, immortal Christ, was afraid no more; men became possessed with a hope which was to prove stronger than armies. The Primitive Church was born when humble men and women began to tell their neighbors, "The Lord is risen indeed, and hath appeared to Simon;' "21 He has also appeared "to the twelve and has been seen of above five hundred brethren at once. People believed because One rose from the dead. Friends could now encourage one another in trials and 22

sorrow with the knowledge that their "Saviour

Jesus Christ had abolished death and had brought life and immortality." (11 Tim. 1:10.)

The death of Jesus and the spiritual experiences of Peter, Paul and others, focused the attention of the early Saints upon the resurrected glorified Christ while encouraging them to carry their share of the burdens of this world. The spiritual experiences and the heroic death of 

Joseph Smith focused the attention of the Latterday Saints on the same Person, and they too have become a Christ-centered people without turning their backs upon the task of building the Kingdom of God upon this earth. 

One hundred years ago a young man in his late thirties stood on the Iowa side of the Mississippi River. His older brother Hyrum and several close friends were with him. Horses and provisions were being gathered for a long trip to the West. The young man was Joseph the 

Prophet; the date was June 23, 1844. 

Across the river in Illinois stood Nauvoo the 

Beautiful, with its fifteen thousand industrious citizens, its great temple, its pleasant homes and fine farms and orchards where, less than five years before, had stood a miserable maleria-

infested frontier village. Now evil days had come upon Nauvoo. Every hour new rumors arrived that mobs were ready to attack and attempt to do what the mobs had done a few years earlier in Missouri-burn, steal, kill, and abuse. 

A few miles distant was little Carthage, the county seat, one of several neighboring villages which with jealous rage had seen the up-start Nauvoo outstrip them in economic and political power and fame. And in Hamilton's Tavern, at Carthage, sat the Governor listening to the Prophet's enemies, while outside on the village green lounged hundreds of state militia men and all the restless elements which go to make up a frontier mob. "The Prophet shall come to Carthage and stand trial or we will level the City of Nauvoo and fetch him; we will not accept the court decisions of Nauvoo," said the fuddleheaded Governor. And those who sought vengeance, riot, and plunder-and not justice-gave each other the sly wink, patted their guns and said, “And this time there will be no change of venue for the Prophet, no acquittal and no appeal." 

Why did the Prophet return to certain death?

He had escaped his enemies again, and there

before him lay the trail to the rich Oregon country with its golden harvest of forests, streams and farm lands. The Prophet's agents in Washington. recently had sent him all the geographical and political information necessary for him to go forward with confidence. The shrewd Stephen A.

Douglas had told them that if they moved to 

Oregon they might form a "noble state within five years," and if at that time the United States did not wish to receive them into the Union they would have a government of their own." 23 It required no seer to foretell that fame, fortune and political power awaited the man who could plant thousands of home-makers in Oregon. That man would settle the vexing problem of British vs. American rights in the great northwest. 

And what of Texas? Eventual annexation was as certain as tomorrow's sun. And at that very moment the "Southern gentleman," Dr. 

Southwick of Louisiana, with his Texas lands and schemes was sitting in the Nauvoo Mansion wondering why the Prophet, his host, did not return and learn more of the opportunities which awaited his people in that area. And what had

the Prophet discussed with the son of Calhoun who had called at Nauvoo a few days earlier?

A young robust body, a venturesome spirit, a social dream and a religious vision of guiding thousands of depressed people from the states and from Europe to western America and there build such a Zion as the world had never seenall these and more cried out to the Prophet, to mount with his friends and ride west. Why did he return? Why did Jesus set his face like flint and go up to Jerusalem, and certain death? 

Why did Socrates pick up the hemlock and quite readily and cheerfully drink off the poison, and ignore the importunities of his weeping friends that he use the means of escape which they had provided? Why in every hour of modern warfare do men in the skies, on land and sea die to save a comrade when duty does not demand it and a way of escape is open that they might live? These questions are unanswerable to all who believe that man is but brains, blood, and bones, plus some social experience. 64 99 The scenes of the Prophet's last days have been recorded with detailed accuracy for all to read; the high points are these: He discarded the plans for the western journey and returned to

To those ready to make the journey with him he said: "I am going like a lamb to the slaughter, but I am as calm as a summer's morning. I have a conscience void of offense toward 

God and toward all men. If they take life 

I shall die an innocent man and it shall be said of me, 'He was murdered in cold blood'!"'25 

Then farewells to his sisters, and to Mother

Smith, who was now a widow and who would soon lose three sons to mob violence. There also was the final good-bye to the children to the adopted daughter Julia, to Joseph, Frederick and Alexander. And there was the final parting with his wife Emma who was soon to give birth to another son whom she would call David. 

Hyrum, faithful unto death, Dr. Richards, and John Taylor were finally selected from the friends who acompanied him from Nauvoo to Carthage to play their respective parts in the last scene of that tragic drama which had begun fourteen years earlier when a young boy said

to his mother, "I have seen God and Jesus Christ; men are fashioned in the image of God and are co-eternal with Him. I shall be known for good or for evil throughout all the world."

Men who would attempt to understand the 

Prophet should remember that in his hour of

greatest peril he turned to the scriptures which most sharply separated him and his people from all the rest of Christendom, he turned to the writings which were the primary cause of all his troubles and for which he has been called epilep tic, imposter, and false prophet.

The reading from the Book of Mormon was followed by the rush of the mob, the withdrawal of the guards, and the stream of musket fire upon the prisoners.

In a few minutes it was all over. Dr. Richards escaped unharmed in a hail of lead, John Taylor was wounded in four places, but lived to serve the Church of Christ for almost half a century. 

And what of Hyrum and the Prophet? Hyrum, to be placed with all noble men who are first in loyalty, was the first to fall. He was followed by Joseph, whose spirit went crashing into eternity with the words, "O Lord, my God!"

Thus Joseph the Prophet, who began his prophetic career with a vision of God before his eyes, closed his earthly career with his Maker's name upon his lips. 

On June 27, 1844, died Lt. General Joseph 

Smith, Commander of the Nauvoo Legion, Mayor of the City, candidate for the Presidency

of the United States. He died on that day in the same sense that Jesus, the Carpenter of 

Nazareth died eighteen hundred years earlier. 

But Joseph The Prophet was not killed by his enemies in that miserable frontier jail, any more than Jesus The Christ was killed by His enemies between two thieves at Golgotha. A few thousand wept at the news of the Prophet's death; today he lives in the hearts of a million who sing: "Praise to the man who communed with Jehovah... 

Men shall extoll him and nations revere." 

Like all great prophets, Joseph the Prophet.. "being dead, yet speaketh."
