[2] INTRODUCTION Educating the people through the use of institutions of learning is a principal agency evolved by society to assure social progress. When education is thought of in this light it has two distinct aspects: (1) giving to the largest number of people a basis for effective membership in the social group-general education, and (2) giving to the members of society the training whereby they may render most effective service in the several vocations-vocational and professional training.* The function of a college is to take up these two aspects where the high schools leave them, and to train young men and women for social leadership and give to them a thorough preparation in those vocations which depend very largely for their successful operation upon the completion of at least a college education.** As a part of our educational scheme, general education is to guarantee reactions to ordinary social situations in line with the best interest of society. Since general education of a college grade is attained by only a relatively small fraction of our citizenry, that small fraction is bound to wield a disproportionately large influence in setting educational, ethical, scientific, social and technical standards for all.
The Alcorn Agicultural and Mechanical College, Alcorn, Mississippi, has established its place as one of the important Negro Land-Grant Colleges to help fulfill the above-described purposes. Because of its historical background and its present range of services, it is committed to the task of providing a first class collegiate education for the Negroes in its section of the country.

[5] History and Development of Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College The history and development of Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College may be divided into four periods. The first period was the Reconstruction Era which closed about 1871; the second was the founding of Alcorn University; the third, from 1871-1899 witnessed the early development of the college; the fourth period dates from 1899 to the present, and is called by some the period which characterizes the making of the "Greater Alcorn".
Some analyses of Negro education in Mississippi prior to 1871 will serve to throw light on conditions which led to the establishment of Alcorn University.
Negro Education In Mississippi Prior To 1871 Prior to the civil war, almost all of the free schools in the state were those maintained out of the proceeds coming from the sale or lease of the sixteenth section lands granted to the state by congress during the early part of the century. These schools were open to white children only.
The work of teaching the Negroes began with the occupation of the state by the Federal armies, and the first schools established for them were at Corinth, shortly after the occupation of that town by the Union troops in 1862. Other schools were were established for Negroes about Vicksburg before the close of the war by such organizations as the American Missionary Association, the Freedman's Aid Society, and the Society of Friends. With the organizing of the Freedman's Bureau, a more systematic and comprehensive plan of [6] HISTORY AND DEVELOPMENT Negro education was undertaken. Joseph Warren, chapiain of a Negro civil war regiment, was appointed superintendent of the Freedman's Bureau schools for the state at large. As to the number of schools and the pupils enrolled therein, Superintendent Warren made the following report on March 31, 1865: *Vicksburg, eleven schools, twenty-two teachers, and an enrollment of 1,854 pupils; camps near Vicksburg, four schools, nine teachers, and an enrollment of 720 pupils; Davis Bend Colony, four schools, nine teachers, and an enrollment of 739 pupils: Natchez, eleven schools, twenty teachers, and an enrollment of 1,080 pupils.
Since there was no state public school system, the teachers were, for the most part, supported by the Friends' Society, United Brethren, the American Baptist Home Missionary Society, the Northwest Freedmen's Commission, the National Freedmen's Relief Association, and the Reformed Presbyterian Board.
Aside from the classical course offered in the college, a scientific course was also available for those who desired it. In both of these courses declamations were stressed and the quarter system used. The buildings consisted of a chapel, three brick dormitories, a number of frame cottages, two college literary halls, president's home and a refectory. The farm consisted of 235 acres upon which cotton and corn were the chief crops grown. A year, peach and apple orchard consisting of 500 trees was started during the 1872-1873 session. The college catalogue for that year states: "It is the intention as it is the duty of the institution to make the farm a model of agricultural beauty and fertility, and to develop a high order of scientific as well as practical agriculture.' "" Under the Agricultural and Mechanical regime, an agricultural course was added and it is interesting to note that the literary courses in high school and college required courses in agriculture. Military tactics was also required of all students in accordance with the requirements of the State law.
[18] The Administration of John H. Burrus, 1882-1893 JOHN H. BURRUS John H. Burrus of Nashville, Tennessee, was elected to the presidency of the institution in the fall of 1882.
Dr. Edwin R. Embree, President of the Julius Rosenwald Foundation, makes this statement:* . . . . In a few years both John and James Burrus were called to the Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College of Mississippi, one (John Burrus) as president, the other (James Burrus) as professor. Working together they built up this institution (Alcorn College) into one of the first modern state colleges for Negroes, maintaining high standards of scholarship and character in both faculty and student body and obtaining larger and larger support from the state treasury for this new kind of public school for Negroes." *Embree, Edwin R., Brown America, page 255.
[19] PUSHING FORWARD Of the ten members of his faculty, all held Bachelors Degrees from reputable institutions, and five of them held the Master of Arts Degree, and one the Doctor of Philosophy Degree from Northwestern University.* An important event during this administration was the passage by Congress of the first Morrill Act amendment, known as the New Morrill Act, in 1890, which gave to the state a direct appropriation out of the national treasury amounting for the first year to $15,000.00, with the provision that this sum should be increased by $1,000.00 a year for ten years when the total appropriation from this source would be $25,000.00 annually thereafter. It was through President Burrus' keen insight and prompt action that, while this Act was before Congress, a rider was put on the appropriation which provided that in states having separate schools for the races the funds allotted to the several states should be divided between the races in proportion to the relative educable population of the races in question. Hence owing to the predominant number of Negroes in the state Alcorn College has always derived from that appropriation a little more than half of the total.
Although the institution was known as the "Agricultural and Mechanical College," unfortunately the mechanical division had been sadly neglected. It was during this administration that the mechanics departments were made realities. In 1893 the departments of carpentry and blacksmithing had their beginnings.
A new combination dormitory for boys and recitation building a frame structure known as Academic Hall, which has since been replaced by a modern fireproof building was erected, and a college hospital was established during this administration. The Literary Gem, a literary society, was organized in 1884 and this organization is still functioning at the college. The Y. M.

Nearly all educated Negroes are inclined to teaching." The graduates of Alcorn College were making wonderful contributions to the Negro race as evidenced in the trustees' report. They reported in 1893 that a graduate of Alcorn was professor of Agriculture at Prairie View Normal School of Texas; another was a minister and editor of a magazine in Tennessee; another was president of Gibsland College of Louisiana; another was a prosperous furniture merchant of Nashville; another was a physician and a clerk in one of the governmental departments at Washington; while in our own state a number were heads of schools or prospering farmers. All of the sixty-two graduates of the college were making good livings, and the most of them were filling with credit to themselves and to their institution positions of influence and usefulness in their respective communities. The president reported that the general conduct of Alcorn students and graduates was one of the chief influences that gave the college constantly increasing favor with the most intelligent, fair-minded and far-seeing people of the state, who recognized it as exerting moulding power for good upon the state's future citizens.
In his biennial message to the legislature in January, 1894, the late Governor Stone, after visiting the institution, had the following to say with reference to the termination of the Burrus Administration: "The difficulty was apparently attributable more to conflicting interest of some of the trustees than to any want of faithful and efficient conduct of the college by the president and the majority of the faculty who supported him. J. H. Burrus who had presided over the institution since 1882 was displaced, I think, without cause and to the detriment of the college. Professor W. H.
Reynolds, a colored teacher of Vicksburg, of good educational acquirements and of excellent reputation, was elected to the presidency." Perhaps the outstanding feature of this administration was the complete grading of the institution as to levels of instruction, and the establishment of a printing department. Of the 261 students enrolled, they represented Louisiana, Arkansas and twenty-eight counties in Mississippi.
[23] PUSHING FORWARD It is interesting to note the worthy object of the college as stated in the catalog of 1893-1894: "The thought constantly kept before the students during their whole course here is that honest toil is honorable, that the occupation of the farmer is among the most honorable of callings; that their business here is to develop skill in their hands; to cultivate the power of thought in their brains, and to awaken and expand in their hearts an unconquerable love for country, and for truth and purity, in order that in after life they may be more prosperous and worthy citizens." Among the outstanding speakers that lectured to the faculty and student body during this administration was the Reverend S. J. Campbell, Millsburg, Liberia, Africa.
He spoke on: "Liberia: Its People, Resources and Needs." [24] The Administration of Thomas J. Calloway, 1894-1896 THOS. J. CALLOWAY Thomas J. Calloway, a graduate of Fisk University, Nashville, Tennessee, was chosen president at the end of the Reynolds-Howard administration. President Calloway, perhaps more than any of his predecessors, conceived the idea of launching the college out upon a campaign of thorough and purposeful statewide advertising. He determined that the people of the state should know of the wonderful advantages the school offered. He put three students on the road to sell and secure subscriptions for the college paper, and thus they carried the story of the institution and its opportunities to every hamlet in the state. The department of shoemaking was added during this administration.
A national incident which came during the Calloway administration and which has meant much to the col- [25] PUSHING FORWARD lege, was the work done in Washington by the Hon. A.
J. Mclauren in 1895, then Senator from Mississippi, whereby he secured an additional grant of land to the state, from the subsequent sale of which this college derives over $7,000.00 annually.
The Alcorn College trustees gave the following report of the school's work to the Governor and State Legislature in 1895: "We feel we know whereof we speak when we assure you that the college was never doing better work than now. . . . The work done in these shops is of a high grade easily taking rank with similar work anywhere. Even with the amounts called for, many improvements needed in order to keep pace with the increasing enrollment and general advancement of the work cannot be made.
"We, therefore, as citizens of the state, appointed by you to look after the state's interest in this college, do most emphatically urge the necessity of appropriating the amounts named in the report of the president of the institution. Knowing as we do the general demands upon our state's treasury and being taxpayers ourselves, we would not make such a request did we not fully appreciate the necessity of it, and we hope you will urge the same upon the legislature." The aim of the college is brought out in the president's report to the Board of Trustees. Says he: "The problem which confronts this institution is a common one-promotion of good citizenship. First of importance comes the necessity to secure the thinking mind.
Then of equal importance comes the necessity of training righteousness. We would graduate 'honest fools' rather than 'wise devils'. But we aim to do neither by directing our efforts to promote in our students honesty of purpose and manly virtues.
"We are strongly committed to industrial education.
Our aim is to secure a skillful hand without sacrifice of scholarship. By requiring all to take some trade we rid ourselves of that element who would seek the school as [26] THOMAS J. CALLOWAY an escape from hard work We dignify all labor as honest." It is interesting to note that at this time the indusrial departments did considerable work for the outside.
The people of the reighborhood and even those in distant parts gave orders to the shops It is recorded that a firm of Atlanta, Georgia made arrangements for the purchase of a number of iron beds from the college shops. Merchants in different parts of the state handled shoes made in the shoe shop. Most of the printing which was done for the immediate Alcorn community was done in the college printing office. It should be observed that all of the work of the shops was done by hand. As an evidence of the faithfulness of the work in the industrial departments, a two-horse wagon from the blacksmith shop was awarded a medal at the Atlanta Cotton States and International Exposition, and a dozen pairs of shoes received a diploma of honorable mention. Shoes were shipped from the college shops to Louisiana; Jackson, Mississippi; Washington, D. C.; Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; Vicksburg and Natchez, Mississippi, and Atlanta, Georgia.
It was President Calloway who started active agitation for the admittance of girls to Alcorn College.
He eloquently plead with the state authorities on behalf of Negro womanhood. In his report to the trustees in 1895 he stated: "In my report to you of May 23, last, I stated that I had visited all the leading schools for colored people in Virginia, North and South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Tennessee, Kentucky and Mississippi; and I am familiar with the workings of those in Louisiana, Texas, Arkansas and Missouri. Alcorn College is the only one, strictly speaking, that is for boys only. Comparing the educational work of other schools with ours, I am satisfied that boys are better trained in the presence of girls.
"It would seem strange if this were a debatable question in Mississippi when all other states in the Union have settled the matter in favor of co-education. As a [27] PUSHING FORWARD matter of fact, it is not a debatable question in our state as the University of Mississippi and the Agricultural and Mechanical College for white youths are both open to the white girls of Mississippi; this, too, when there is a separate institution maintained for them.
"In the face of all this, shall Alcorn College continue a relic of barbarism, when women had no rights, by remaining exclusively for males? Has the black woman, who as a slave nestled in her bosom the infant that grew to be a Jefferson Davis or an L. Q. C. Lamar and in her freedom still proved the most faithful servant in the world, no right to industrial education? Shall Negro girls be given all the opportunities to improve themselves offered to their brothers? Or shall they (God forbid) be barred from opportunities of improvement and made the prey through ignorance of beastly passion and vice? The white people cannot expect to rear their children in ideas of chastity, virtue and industry, if those children are to live among a Negro population, largely outnumbering the whites, unless that population is to be trained in these cardinal virtues. Condemn the black woman of this state to the slavery of ignorance which carries with it the bitterness of vice and licentiousness, and you have put an incubus upon the white citizens that is destined to bring serious results.
On the other hand, throw wide all doors for improvement, give them ar. equal chance, and they will, in the future as in the past, prove the most faithful of friends in adversity.
"Through the Board of Trustees I appeal to the legislature, soon to assemble, and to the kindly offices of the Executive for favorable action on this matter. Behind them, I appeal to the press, the pulpit and fireside to support the lawmakers with a favorable expression of public sentiment that will leave no doubts of the wishes of the most chivalrous people in the world." If President Calloway had done no more than make that very fine plea for Negro womanhood, the writer feels that his administration would have been, histori- [28] THOMAS J. [30] The Administration of E. H. Triplett, 1896-1899 E. H. TRIPLETT President Triplett, formerly a minister in the Baptist Church, was, according to the record, a self-made man who had never attended college. His knowledge of college administration was very limited. His faculty, practically all college graduates and some from the best mid-western institutions, regarded him as having been made president because of favoritism and not because of any fitness which he possessed for the job. Trouble started between the president and the faculty early in the new administration, and the Board of Trustees dismissed practically the entire faculty and gave President Triplett a clean slate. During Christmas week, 1897, the president was shot by an unknown assailant. At the end of the next college session, conditions had not [31] PUSHING FORWARD improved at the college and the trustees decided, as the spokesman for the board put it, "to reverse the order of the sweeping." This time the president was removed and the faculty retained. Thus ended an administration of turmoil and distraction and a general lack of tangible accomplishments. A standard three-year Business Course was offered in 1897-1898.

Each president contributed something to its development, but the late Dr. L. J.
Rowan served longer and guided its larger developments for nineteen consecutive years.
The institution is partly located upon and operates what is known as the General N. B. Furlong Farm-a name given in honor of one General N. B. Furlong who, it seems, came to the state during the Reconstruction days and resided in the city of Vicksburg. He was evidently in the state when Oakland College was purchased from the Presbyterian Synod for the higher education of Negro youth and its name changed to Alcorn University. General Furlong died in the state of New [41] PUSHING FORWARD York after being gcne from Mississippi for many years.
In his will he had remembered Alcorn University (now Alcorn A. M. College) with a bequest. After paying the expenses necessary in probating the will, a large part of the college farm was purchased. This farm is used as a means of providing employment for students, experimental plots and support for the college.
The Smith-Hughes Act was passed by Congress in February, 1917, and provides for the co-operation of the Federal Government with the states in a plan for the training of teachers for positions as leaders, directors and supervisors of the three lines of work most essential to a well-ordered civilization, viz: Agriculture, Home Economics, Trades and Industries. In accordance with the provisions of the Act, Alcorn has been designated the Negro teacher-training institution for Mississippi.
In 1919, Dr. A. E. Butts, now Chancellor of the University of Mississippi, was high in his praise of the work done at Alcorn. Says he:* "The Alcorn Agricultural and Mechanical College has developed steadily since its establishment; the enrollment has increased from year to year; the courses of study have been broadened. and the Negro race has been benefited by the training along industrial lines which the establishment of the institution has made possible." The federal authorities, as well as state officials, who kept in touch with the work done at the college, were complimentary both of the work done by the students and of the economical management of the college.
The agricultural, horticultural and industrial departments were especially emphasized by the college administration. Students were taught useful trades that made them self-sustaining, useful citizens of the state.
They were taught the best methods of farming and trucking, and the proper use of improved agricultural implements and machinery. The Board of Trustees called attention to the fact that the deficit at Alcorn was due to the lack of an increase in support funds to keep pace with the increased operating costs incidental to the continued growth of the college. Investigation showed that the college had been running behind one biennium after another. The Board felt that the time had come to liquidate the college's indebtedness and to increase its support fund by $25,000.00 per year in order to avoid future deficits. Secretary Harmon gave additional reasons for the deficit. He also called attention to the very great need for a gravel road to connect the college with Highway 61, either to Port Gibson or to Lorman. During the winter, the roads were well nigh impassable and, at all seasons of the year, following a heavy rain, people going to and from the college would bog down in the mud. During the session 1930-31 a gravel road was completed which connected the college with Port Gibson and Lorman.
In spite of the deficit, at a special meeting of the trustees the college officials were instructed to continue the operation of the institution, in the firm belief that the legislature in its wisdom would recognize the inadequacy of the last support fund, make an appropriation to pay the deficit, and provide sufficient funds for the next biennium without any deficit whatsoever.

President Rowan in his report to the Board of Trustees in 1929 called their attention to a survey made by the Department of Interior about two years prior to this time of the leading institutions for the higher education of the Negro. According to that survey Alcorn College fell short of the high mark in several matters.
There was not a single delinquency that could not have been removed had the proper financial support been provided. The four weak points in the college, according to this report, were: (1) The organization was not in keeping with modern school organization. Several additional officers were needed-a dean, a registrar, a superintendent of buildings and grounds, a full-time librarian, and a sufficient number of additional teachers to reduce the sizes of classes, and to segregate the high school from the college. At President Rowan's insistent demand the Board of Trustees gave Alcorn a full-time librarian in 1930, and a dean and registrar in 1932.
(2) There was not a strong department of education, nor a standard teacher-training course.
(3) All of the courses, especially the Liberal Arts and Teacher-training courses in Agriculture and Home Economics, needed to be greatly strengthened.

AS A MAN-L. J. Rowan was born of slave parents on August 7, 1871, at Rodney, Mississippi, just four and a half miles from Alcorn College, the very year in which the college was purchased and dedicated to the educational development of the Negro race. His first days were spent in an obscure log cabin, which was previously used as a living quarters for slaves. He was forced to work his own way all through his early life and young manhood. His early training was received [64] L. J. ROWAN in such public schools as Rodney, Mississippi, and St.
Joseph, Louisiana, afforded for Negroes at that time.
Having completed the course of study offered him in these schools at sixteen years of age, on September 18, 1887, he entered Alcorn College. At Alcorn he came in contact with men of Negro leadership and culture, including the Burrus brothers, one of whom was John H.
Burrus. then president of the college. The faculty as a whole was well-fitted to assume the training of future leaders for a race who but yesterday were slaves. It is not strange that the same idealism and nobleness of purpose manifested by his instructors should be inculcated in his thinking and life. Young Rowan's love for music; his ability to write and speak effectively; his keen intellect, manliness and fineness of character; his high respect for law and order-all made him an outstanding student among his fellows, and a favorite of his instructors.
His college career, however, was not lacking of its share of financial worries. He found it necessary to make his own way through school by doing house work for white families, and whatever other work he could find to do. At times he was compelled to leave school in the midst of the session and seek employment for one or two quarters in order to earn support for himself These conditions did not discourage him, but made him more determined than ever to reach his goal.
His college goal was reached in May, 1893, by receiving the degree of Bachelor of Science.
talented Dr. Rowan married Miss Mattie Foote, a young woman, in July, 1897. Mrs. Rowan is a descendant of the well-known and highly respected Foote family formerly of Yazoo City, Mississippi. They are the parents of four girls-Ruth, Hermoine, Pearl and Thelma. All of the children have received a superior education, first at Alcorn College and then elsewhere.
Five years of public school work in the small obscure communities of his state, twelve years as English instructor at Alcorn College, and twenty-five years as President of Alcorn College complete the outer aspects of his labors, the resume of the man's activities them- [65] PUSHING FORWARD selves. One can easily trace in them the logic of events as the mastery of one phase of work and group of problems that prepared the spirit of the man for the next.
The slave cabin, the school, the homes of well-educated white people, the obscure rural community as a teacher's proving ground, the college teacher and president -all were a series of sequences in which the man grew with enlarging function and opportunity.
AS A TEACHER-He early found his calling and dedicated his life to it. Even before he graduated, as soon as he could qualify for public school teaching, he entered the teaching profession. The first five years after graduation he taught with much contentment and joy in the rural schools of the state in the remote sections thereof, located many miles from a railroad. He taught better than he knew; because of services wellrendered in the obscure places he had attracted attention, and consequently in 1898 he was called from obscurity to serve on the faculty of his alma mater. Here was a varied experience and yet exceedingly helpful to him in later years. He was always at his best in the classroom, and exemplified in himself that article of his educational creed which insists that a real teacher should make his subject both interesting and instructive. He rose rapidly from the position of tutor to assistant professor of English by 1901, just three years after his return to the college as instructor. Professor Bowles is a native of Claiborne county, Mississippi, having been born there on a plantation known as Caledonia, in 1869. His parents moved away from Claiborne county up into Warren when the boy was about four months old. There he lived and played and spent his boyhood as other boys, and there was begun the tedious struggle of climbing the educational ladder in the rural schools of his home community, at the age of seven years. His attendance at school was of a rather desultory sort, as the free school system at that early date, after the Civil war, was just getting established and the terms were rarely more than three or four months long, and the teachers poorly prepared.
Moreover, Professor Bowles had been born of slave parents who had received their emancipation only four years before the boy was born, and who recognized that any chance at all to learn the "Three R's" in any sort of school was far beyond anything they had ever enjoyed; and therefore, they could not adequately appreciate the necessity of having their son take advantage of what meager opportunities for real training the poorly-organized school system at that time offered.
It was in the midst of such an uninviting educational environment that the boy was forced to make his initial efforts at education; and early gave evidence of a precocity beyond his years, and stimulated the hopes of his parents, friends, and teachers that he would yet make good and that there was more truth than poetry [113] PUSHING FORWARD in the oft-repeated saying that "Honor and fame from no condition rise; act well your part; there all the honor lies." He was sent to college at the age of seventeen, evidently entering what would now be called the tenth grade, or second year high school. He remained in college seven years, graduating and receiving his Bachelor's Degree in 1895, at the head of his class. He entered the profession of teaching in the rural schools of the state and there brought the same painstaking assiduity to the task of teaching that he had given evidence of in the job of learning.
He assisted in building and organizing the first high schools in Lawrence and Marion counties, where he spent the first ten years of his after-school life, and where the results of his labors are still in evidence in the communities which he served.
After having been away from the college ten years, he was recalled by his alma mater to join the forces of those on the college staff who were to "train Mississippi's future citizens." He returned to serve his alma mater as an assistant professor in the department of English. It so happened that much of the English taught to students at the time when he joined the department was in the form of emphasis upon formal grammar. His keen perception in the appropriate use of words enabled him to blaze new paths in the work of the English departiment and shift the emphasis from that of mere statement of rules and canons to discovery of the purpose of those rules and bring about considerable improvement in the use of the English sentence by the students in the departinent.
Nevertheless, in addition to the new highs which he was able to establish in the mastery of English speech, his many-sided interests in the major purpose for which the institution was established constrained him to discover that the use to which the facilities of the department of agriculture were being put were falling far short of reasonable expectations. He at once began to call the attention of the administration to this serious lack and to insist upon a more purposeful use of [114] P. S. BOWLES the department's facilities towards the training of the students, in the business of farming and upon an enlargement of those facilities for more adequate use.
He had the prescience, if we may call it that, to see that in a State that was predominantly rural, and whose major interest and industry was that of farming, and whose population was more than half Negro and mostly untrained, there was nothing that opened up so great an opportunity for his people as adequate training for maximum achievement in the field of this major industry. He had the faith to believe and the courage to assert that Alcorn A. M. College, which by its name and profession was committed to the training of the Negro citizenship in the vocation of farming, was lying down on her job, and asleep as to her most precious opportunity if she did not turn a greater proportion of her resources and energies to the training of her young manhood for valiant service in this important calling. His position was bitterly assailed and he was maligned and traduced by the majority of the then faculty staff, and denounced as a cheap "time server" and a "player to the galleries" to win favor with the white South. He continued to inveigh against an educational philosophy which sought to prepare girls and boys for stations beyond any probable attainment, or for no particular station at all; and insisted that they should be directed and constrained to prepare for the opportunities that were at hand, and thus by utilizing these to the best of their abilities they would be preparing themselves for places of higher responsibility, if such there be where they should present themselves.

[119] SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS The facts and factors discovered in this study of Alcorn and her graduates will be commented upon briefly.
The rise of Negro leadership and the development of the American Negro depend upon opportunity; but opportunity for him has two aspects: (1) Preparation on the part of the Negro to meet and to make opportunity for himself, and (2) Opportunity provided for him through outside sources.
Part I of this study presents a brief record of what a limited number of the race and one educational institution have achieved. For the individuals who have been students at this institution, the opportunities it has offered them have been provided largely by others.
Part II of the study, on the other hand, cites cases of a few who have met and made opportunities for themselves. In this they are also making and spreading opportunities for others of their race.
All of the schools maintained in Mississippi prior to the civil war were schools established for the education of white children. The first schools established for Negroes were at Corinth shortly after the occupation of that town by the Union troops in 1862.
Among the best element of southern whites, there was a favorable attitude toward Negro education prior to 1871; however, there was also a certain element who opposed Negro education before the year 1871, the reconstruction period. Both of these forces are still in evidence in the south. A free public school system of education for Negroes and whites went into operation [120] SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION in October, 1870, resulting from cooperation between the Negro and white members of the "Black and Tan" convention of 1869, which authorized the legislature to establish this system of education. The legislature of 1870, composed of Negroes and whites, passed the required act which put this system into operation, and Henry R. Pease was elected the first State Superintendent of Education.

The college sent out its first graduating class, consisting of three members, in 1882. Since that date, Alcorn has graduated 833 from its college department.
These graduates are found in 31 states and the District of Columbia of the United States, and in 54 of the 82 counties of Mississippi. They are listed in 40 different occupations. Alcorn graduates have made some outstanding contributions in Negro leadership as evidenced by the facts used in the case studies that appear in this report. These case studies have been recorded in, what has seemed to the author, necessary detail.
L. J. Rowan did outstanding work in the building of the "Greater Alcorn". In 1928 he secured from the State Legislature and the General Education Board of New York City more than $300,000.00 as an initial start in Alcorn's building program.
R. S. Grossley was the first Negro appointed Assistant State Supervisor of Public Schools of Mississippia position created by the State Legislature in 1919. It [121] PUSHING FORWARD was he who started the Rosenwald schools moving at a rapid rate. His real work has been done at the State College for Colored Students in Delaware, where he has been president since 1923.
