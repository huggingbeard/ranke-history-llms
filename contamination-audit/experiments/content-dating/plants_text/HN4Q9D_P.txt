CHAPTER I. WHY WE MISS SCHOOL 1. FINDING THE CAUSES 

The purpose of this chapter is to find our chief reasons for missing school. If we know the dangers to avoid and then do not try to keep away from them, we do not deserve a great deal of sympathy from our friends when we are injured or sick. 

These problems and activities are for you to think about and talk about. You will need to look for information in other books. 

If you do not understand what to do in order to find information, ask your teacher to help you get started.

The other topics of this book are to be studied and discussed as this one is. So these directions will not be repeated each time. If you have a "health and safety notebook," you may want to keep notes in it about what you read, discuss, and do. Always try to think of how you may apply what you learn to what you need to do. For remember this: "To learn and learn, without applying what one knows,

Is like the man who plows and plows, but never, never sows." 

Suggested Problems and Activities

5. Now see if you can tell one or more ways in which these causes for missing school could have been avoided.

APPENDIX A. MY SELF-HELP CHECK LIST 

The purpose of this "Self-Help Check List" is to aid you in knowing whether you are improving and forming good habits of health and safety. 

It will remind you of some things you should do and of others you should avoid doing. 

You and your teacher may also use this check list to review your work once a month or oftener. You may use it as reminders of things you want to discuss in your group meetings. You may revise it either by leaving out some items, or by adding others in which you are interested.

APPENDIX B. AGE, HEIGHT, AND WEIGHT CHART 

Not many years ago, someone made a record of ages, heights, and weights of a great many children; with this information he made a chart to show the weights and heights of average boys and average girls of different ages. If you had been a pupil shortly afterwards, your weight and height might have been compared with the chart, and if your weight had been twenty percent greater or ten percent less than the average for your height at your age, you might have been told that you were too much overweight or underweight.

We know now that the height and the weight of a person depend partly on the race from which he comes and partly on his parents and grandparents. The average Englishman is taller than the average Japanese. You probably know some family in which nearly all the members are tall and thin or a family in which nearly all the members are short and heavy. How tall you grow and how much you weigh depend partly on the kind of family you came from.

Using the Table.-But if you would be strong and healthy, you should give attention to your height and weight. Children should grow a bit taller each month. Measure yourself carefully, at the same time of day, once each month. If you find that, over a period of three months, your height does not increase, the failure to grow is a warning that there may be something wrong with your health or your diet. With that warning, you should ask your doctor to examine you. He may find that you are well, but he may find that you are not.

You should weigh once a month, too. Each weighing should be at the same time of day. It is best to weigh without clothing, but if it is inconvenient for you to do so, you may still get a fairly good comparison of your weight by being sure to wear just about the same amount of clothing each time you weigh. If your weight differs greatly from the weight of other children of your age and height as shown in the table (p. 72), you may not be eating the right kinds and amounts of foods, you may not be getting the right kind and amount of exercise and rest, or you may have an infection. It would be well to ask your doctor. But being above or below the average weight may be natural for a member of your family. The important thing is that your weight should gradually increase through childhood. Compare your weight each month with your weight the month before. If, through a period of two months, your weight does not increase, or increases too rapidly, there is probably something wrong with your diet, your rest, or your general health.

Making Your Graph.-Keep a record of your height and weight on the graph on page 73. The record of an average eleven-year-old boy has been made on the graph to help you. When this boy began his record in September, he was 56 inches tall and weighed 77 pounds. His height and 71

weight increased at an even rate through the year, and in the next September he was 58 inches tall and weighed 87 pounds. 

Of each pair of numbers at the side of the graph, the first stands for height in inches. If you begin your height record in September and are then 

53 inches tall, put a dot on the line under "Sept." at a point halfway between the cross lines 53 and 54. If you are 54 inches tall in October, put a dot where line 54 crosses the upright line under October. Then draw a line to connect the two dots. You may grow more in some months than in others; don't be alarmed about that. But if you don't grow at all for several months, beware!
