ARE SCIENCE AND RELIGION AT STRIFE? 

A Correspondence CRITICISM OF THE ASSERTION OF ARCHBISHOP TEMPLE THAT THERE IS A TENSION BETWEEN SCIENCE AND RELIGION Dr. Horace J. Bridges, Leader, 

Chicago Ethical Society.

Dear Dr. Bridges: 

You paid me an unusual compliment when you suggested that I review two very outstanding books, viz., "Creative Evolution," by Henri Bergson, and "Nature, Man and God," by the Most Reverend William Temple,

Archbishop of Canterbury. 

Henri Bergson, in his great contribution, would seem to have destroyed a tension between science and religion, arising from the assertion that "man is a product of blind✓ force," while the Archbishop, when he speaks as theologian, would seem to have created a new tension between science and religion, resting upon his assertion that "no law of nature as discovered by physical science ✓ 

In this book we find an author who is a distinguished theologian, an erudite theological philosopher, and the faithful shepherd of a huge and devoted flock. This author's religious and philosophical observations would seem to carry Bergson's assertion, that man cannot be the product of blind forces, to its logical conclusion. It is very difficult for a mere layman to review this volume. 

*"NATURE, MAN AND GOD" by William Temple, Archbishop of Canterbury. 

3

Difficult, because the author so challenges the reader's profound respect and admiration; difficult because he seems, according to his own confession, to be so often. speaking in the alternative; difficult because, in so speaking, his utterances seem to be sometimes in opposition; difficult because of the amazing scope and extent of his erudition; difficult because, like many philosophers, he so often fails to define his terms. May I not refer briefly to the inspiring argument in which the author, in effect, accepts Bergson's assertion that man cannot be the product of blind forces and carries the same on upward to its logical conclusion, thus: He announces (page 266) that "whenever we see nature truly we see God manifested in and through it." He here quotes (Page 266, Note 2), with approval, a statement by A. S. Pringle Pattison, that "the world of nature cannot be understood by an intelligent theist, otherwise than as an ever-present working of a divine power." Observing in man the mysterious entity known as mind, the author, in a closely knit and convincing argument (pages 128, 129, 130, 132, 148, 149, 247, 256, 258, 279, 280, 281) points out that a cosmic mind possessing at least all the qualities of the mind of man is a logical explanation of the universe. 

I now come to the problem which disturbs me, and which is the only reason for my intruding upon your busy hours, namely, the contention of the Archbishop that "no law of nature as discovered by physical science is ultimate" (page 267), and that, "therefore, a tension exists between philosophy and religion." But if this allegation be true, then a vital tension will thereby have been created not only between philosophy and religion, but also between science and religion. This because the very foundations of science rest upon the assumption that such laws are ultimate. Confronted by this tension, what can the coming generation do? Must they choose between science and religion?

The author not only alleges that such tension exists, but refers to it (page 54) as "unavoidable" or even as "good". I most respectfully suggest that any such tension must evidence error on the part of religion or science, or both, because if free from error, religion and science must be but paths leading to the same infinite destination, i.e. truth. Therefore a tension can only be "good" if it causes religion or science, or both, to search for and attempt to cure the error creating such tension. 

Prior to his argument proving, as he claims, tension, the author observes that "Divine Personality must be accepted in bitter earnest." I do not question this. Indeed, it is for this reason that I shall take the liberty of suggesting that such divinity, alleged to evidence the tension, cannot be proven by reference to any unusual physical phenomenon, such as the alleged virgin birth. As a justification for this observation I quote from the Archbishop himself the statement (page 250), "Willingly to believe what is suspected to be false is not only felt to be a degradation of the credulous believer's personality, but an offense against the order of reality."

Christ's divinity, if a reality, must be rested upon foundations deeper, more profound, more secure than any such physical incident; namely, his life, his message, his personality.

PERSONALITY 

His Goodness: Christ and the Archbishop are each outstanding shepherds, but how different their heredity and environment! One has a distinguished ancestry, influential friends, a prominent position, an education to excite admiration, and an erudition truly wonderful. The other had none of these-no distinguished heredity, no influential family or friends, no higher education, surroundings forlorn and bare. And yet, from somewhere, Christ caught a vision such that his personality became so radiant, his interest in frail humanity so warm, so human,

so glowing, that any one, then or now, who becomes really exposed to the glow, has received a moral uplift as certainly as our physical humanity receives benefit from the rays of the sun. How do I know this? Have I had a "revelation"? Not one glimpse of a revelation, but, looking around me at others, I have again and again seen such effect with my own eyes.

His Wisdom: The Carpenter of Galilee clearly per ceived that the Creator is Spirit ("God is a Spirit").

Followers inspired by him announced that the Creator is super-sensate ("no man hath seen God at any time").

Centuries before modern scientists made an analogous discovery, they perceived and announced that "the things which are seen are temporal, and the things which are not seen are eternal." What daring and startling statements these were for such individuals to make! 

Coming now to the argument of the Archbishop, he seems to rest his conclusion that "no law of nature, as discovered by physical science, is ultimate" largely upon the premise that (page 299) "The Living God is at work He is at work there as a living person. as a Person

I do not presume to question this assertion. Individually, however, I would prefer the statement of the Carpenter of Galilee that "God is a Spirit." In any event, may it not be agreed that all the qualities known in man as "personality" are undoubtedly derivative from the ultimate Divine Reality? Indeed, in this connection, in a conversation, you once suggested as more appropriate the term "super-personal," which is in the direction of what I am trying to say. The alleged relevance of this claim (that is, of the existence of Person and Personality as attributes of the Creator) will appear from a somewhat careful consideration of the author's argument as we proceed. Thus the Archbishop says, "Personality can only reveal itself in a person." I agree that a person alone, by the very definition, can have person-ality; but might

not the Creator, if Spirit, have at least all of the qualities which we know as personality?

The author adds (265) "The explanation of the world is to be sought in a Personal Reality, or, to use the historic phrase, in a 'Living God'." This statement would seem to presume that a Living God must be a person; which conclusion would seem to me to be subject to grave question.

The author adds (264), "But if I regard the Ultimate Principle as Personal, then I am at least at liberty to interpret as acts of specific self-revelation on the part of that Principle any which can make good their claim to be so regarded." The author now indicates why he believes the question of personality is crucial. Thus he says that (295) "Personality, whether human or divine, insofar as it is immanent, is a principle of variation." 

Whether this is true, so far as it affects the question in issue, would seem to depend, as we shall hereafter observe, upon the reason for such variation. (Page 295) "It must be a principle of perpetual adjustment according to sufficient reason." A sufficient reason, however, for a human being might be, as we shall contend, no reason at all for an Omnipotent or an All-wise Creator. The further argument of the Archbishop will show the relevancy of our last remark. Continuing (page 

267), the Archbishop says, "The naive religious view is that God made the world and imposed laws upon it, which it invariably observes unless He intervenes to modify the operation of His own laws. From this naive view springs the suggestion that it would better comport with the infinite Majesty of God that He should from the outset impose such laws as would never stand in need of modification. But if, as we have seen ground for hold-✅ ing, the World Process is itself the medium of God's personal action, the whole situation is altered. There is nothing majestic about invariable constancy of personal

action which remains unaltered whether the circumstances are the same or not; rather, should it be called mulish. Constancy of purpose is a noble characteristic, but it shows itself, not in unalterable uniformity of conduct, but in perpetual self-adaptation, with an infinite delicacy of graduation, to different circumstances, so that, however these may vary, the one unchanging purpose is always served."

The Archbishop adds (269), "God in the world acts now this way and now that as he carries to accomplishment His unchanging purpose." The purpose remains constant, but the means must vary from time to time to meet unforeseen circumstances and thus avoid becoming mulish.

The fallacy of the author's argument would now seem to become apparent. Is it not clear that the question whether the laws of nature are or are not ultimate, de- ✓pends not upon the existence of the Creator as a person or with personality, but upon the capacity of the Creator? 

That is, if the Creator, whether personal or not, is subject to man's limitations and if, therefore, He cannot foresee events as they occur, then indeed, unless He be "mulish," His laws could not be ultimate, because it would be necessary to adjust such laws from time to time, to meet unforeseen events as they occur. 

While if, as insisted by the author, the Creator is Omniscient, then, whether personal or not, the laws would logically be ultimate because no event would be unforeseen and, therefore, no adjustment would be required. Is it not curious, in this connection, that science would seem to be insisting, in effect, upon an interpretation of the "laws of nature" which connotes an all-wise law-giver, while the Archbishop would seem to be here insisting upon an interpretation valid only if such law-giver is limited in vision, wisdom and understanding?

It may, however, be suggested that if the laws of nature

discovered by physical science are ultimate, and if they do not require the presence and attention of the Deity, then, in such event, the universe will have taken on a mechanistic complexion, one in which the free interplay of personality between God and man is no longer necessary or present. But does this logically follow? 

Fixity of law and freedom of personality do not seem to be inconsistent in human affairs. On the contrary, fixity of law would seem to assist in the creation of human character on earth.

Both Bergson (page 255) and the Archbishop (pages 

239-295) agree that free will has been given to man. If, as we believe, man is the product of an all-wise Creator, then there must have been a purpose behind this wonderful gift to man. What was it? I have reached a question far beyond my understanding. I may, however, still wonder. The Archbishop, with his usual acumen, well observes (page 84) that "The most important of mental disciplines for almost all purposes is not that which distinguishes between certainty and probability, but that which leads to discrimination between degrees of probability."

Could even an Omnipotent Deity make man other than an automaton unless man had been granted free will? 

And may not the existence of actual free will throw a flood of light upon the existence of sin, and therefore upon the cause of this terrible conflict in which we find ourselves?

I think of Ultimate Laws not as necessarily inconsistent with free will, but as a framework within which free will must decide responsibly or suffer the consequences. If so, may not Ultimate Laws be a necessity to man in his evolution upward?

There is, however, an incident, suggests the authornamely, the Virgin Birth—which of itself tends to prove that such laws are not ultimate. Thus says the author

(296) "If He (i.e., the Creator) use some way of becoming, Himself, an historical episode other than that by which other similar episodes are initiated, such, for example, as birth from a Virgin, this is no manifestation of new and usually dormant power, but is due to the same cause as other and normal births; namely, the Will of this same Logos, now aiming at a special and unique result." The author adds (299) "For some it may make clearer the point that is being urged if it is put in the traditional terms of Christian theology. God as immanent is the Eternal Logos. . . . This Logos is the explanation of all things that occur, whether it be the regular and customary growth of the seed into the plant or the birth of His own fleshly tabernacle from a Virgin Mother." 

(Page 296.) "The Personal Deity, universally immanent 

-the Logos-may for centuries act in ways that very imperfectly disclose His Character, yet, when time is appropriate, may Himself submit to conditions which reveal that character as it had always been." 

I might almost call upon the Archbishop (where he speaks "in the alternative") to answer this suggestion. 

Thus (page 46) he observes, "It has ever been the common tendency of mankind to trace the activity of God in the unexpected or astonishing. It was not that other explanations of the normal were accepted, but that no explanation was sought at all. The normal was taken for granted; the astonishing and unpredictable was regarded as the act of 

God.. But Jesus of Nazareth taught men to see the operation of God in the regular and normal." Will it not be agreed that the real miracle is that we are born at all (the regular and normal), and that being born of a virgin adds nothing except the unusual to such miracle? The Archbishop adds, “To cling to some belief when it has no inherent spiritual value . . . on the ground that it is bound up with what has spiritual value is a form of 10

self-assertion which must be condemned by science and religion alike." But is not this precisely the situation presented by the dogma of a virgin birth? That is, a virgin birth would seem to be no more spiritual in nature than the astonishing fact of being born at all. Do not those who cling to it do so because it is believed to be bound up with what has spiritual value, namely, Christ's life, his personality and message?

Personally, I am uninterested in the question of a virgin birth:

First, because I feel confident that such birth, if true, would not tend to prove divinity. Thus, the Archbishop himself suggests that certain scientists contend that a conception might be initiated as the result of a strong emotional excitement. But suppose that a birth so originated should result, as seems entirely possible, would such birth tend to prove divinity?

In this connection, Matthew Arnold made the penetrating observation that a miracle proves only itself; that is, the fact that one was born of a virgin would prove only that he was born of a virgin.

I question the virgin birth further because the founder of our religion never based a claim upon this alleged materialistic event. Indeed, as already observed, and as the Archbishop has so well put it (46), "Jesus of Nazareth taught men to see the operation of God in the regular and normal." Is it not worthy of notice that the Carpenter of Galilee should have perceived that the regular and normal so often evidences a real miracle and that he should have ignored the unusual, which so misled his disciples and followers?

The Archbishop adds, "If men had been ready to follow Him (i.e., Jesus) in this, much of the conflict between 

Religion and Science would have been avoided." May I not adopt this penetrating observation as strongly tending to validate my position? 11

Finally, it seems to me parochial in the extreme to believe that the Creator of a stupendous cosmos would attempt to verify the great mission of the founder of religion by an insignificant physical event such as the one in question.

I have made bold to question the existence of the alleged tension between religion and science as above presented only because such assertion, if erroneous, coming from such a distinguished source, would be, as I believe, harmful to the Christian religion, the fundamentals of which rest so firmly upon reasonable foundations. 

Yours cordially

JOHN H. S. LEE. 

REPLY OF THE ARCHBISHOP TO THE FOREGOING CRITICISM Lambeth Palace, S.E.I. 

Dear Dr. Bridges, July 20, 1944.

There has reached me a document, addressed to yourself, which consists of comments upon Bergson's "Creative 

Evolution" and my own Gifford Lectures, "Nature, Man and God". There is no address except Chicago, apart from that attached to your own name. I am therefore venturing to send to you the enclosed notes upon these comments. In doing so, I should like to express my appreciation of the generous attitude shewn by the writer of the comments towards my book. 

Yours sincerely,

WILLIAM CANTUAR.* 

The main trend of these comments rests upon a misunderstanding of the sentence repeatedly quoted "no law of nature discovered by physical science is ultimate." *It is the ancient European custom of Bishops and Archbishops to write their Christian names followed by the abbreviated Latin term for their dioceses. Thus Dr. Temple's official signature is as given above: William 

Cantuariensis,-i.e., "William of Canterbury." 12

This is treated as if "ultimate" were a synonym for ✓ "true." But that is not the meaning of the word or the intention of the writer. The law may be completely "true" and yet not "ultimate" that is, not the last word.

The last word, on any theistic hypothesis, is the purpose of God. What science does is to find out how God works. 

That sentence has therefore nothing in the world to do with the "tension" between science and religion. The connexion made on p. 8 has no basis in the argument of the book. That "tension" arises from the difference in the angles of approach adopted by science and religion. 

Religion, when vital, always contains assurances which science cannot warrant and is therefore right (for its own. purposes) to reject. Of course this tension springs from error or defect in our science or religion or both (see 

N. M. & G. pp. 54 and 55). When each is perfect there will be no tension; but that is a long way, perhaps infinitely, far off. 

Similarly the references to the argument about the Virgin Birth invert that argument by assumption before handling it. It is nowhere suggested in N. M. & G. (or in the book which treats this more fully Christ the Truth) that this alleged historical event is evidence of Christ's divinity. The whole argument is that His divinity✓ is evidence for that event - the event can be believed if, and only if, Christ is independently believed to be divine. No theologian so far as I know has held the position that M. Arnold ridiculed for at least half a century. 

The value of the belief is chiefly, in Karl Barth's terms, a token value. It is a vivid symbol of the truth (if Christianity is true) that Christ did not emerge out of History but broke into it. No human will was actively concerned in His birth but only the passively receptive will of Mary: see John I, 13, where the reference to the birth without human paternity is evident even though we do not (with 

Tertullian) read "who was born." 13

The question about Omniscience and Fore-knowledge is too big to start on in these notes. But if the argument of 

Lecture VIII (Process, Mind and Value) is valid, Omniscience would not involve complete Fore-knowledge, because it would be an eternal comprehension of Time, not a knowledge at all points of time concerning all other points of time. But on any ground there seems to be no spiritual value in unvaried monotony of action. And after all the modern scientists have abandoned the claim that the generalisations called laws of nature are exact statements of invariable behaviour and offer them as norms (from which the events do not diverge very far) based on statistical averages. With this I hold my argument about the Virgin Birth to be strictly compatible viz: that if Christ is divine there is no a priori ground for rejecting the Virgin Birth: here is a unique fact, it is congruous that it should have a unique origin or "cause". 

That proves nothing, but leaves the way open for the specific evidence, if any. And in any case the mere event of the Virgin Birth is no more truly an act of God than any other birth, but it is a different act. 

WILLIAM CANTUAR. 

OBSERVATIONS UPON THIS REPLY Dr. Horace J. Bridges 

Leader Chicago Ethical Society

Dear Dr. Bridges: 

Thank you for your kindly letter with the enclosures from the very distinguished author, the Most Reverend 

William Temple, Archbishop of Canterbury. I had, at your suggestion, as you know, forwarded to him a copy my letter to you, and under separate cover had written an explanatory note regarding the same, which latter must, however, have suffered the hazards of war. of 14

The fact that the most distinguished leaders in science. and theology, I speak of Professor Albert Einstein and of the Archbishop of Canterbury, have condescended to comment upon an ordinary layman's observations upon these matters, would seem of itself to prove that these eminent men consider these questions vital to religion and science.

THE QUESTION AGAIN IS WHETHER THERE IS A TENSION BETWEEN SCIENCE AND RELIGION. 

The question of such tension would seem to be peculiarly imminent at the present moment; this because the nations of the earth, having perceived that brute force offers no hope for solving the problems facing humanity, may very presently turn to nations which profess Christianity, not only for material help but also for spiritual guidance.

This same world, however, looks with profound respect and admiration at the strides of modern science. 

Will not, therefore, such world be perplexed, if not confounded, if, as the Archbishop insists, it is assured that "An acute tension exists between science and religion, and that such tension is not only necessary but also good?" 

You have kindly suggested that I comment upon the Archbishop's reply to my letter, together with anything 

I may care to add.

I fully appreciate that the Christian religion consists primarily of a "Way of Life", not something resting upon traditional theological dogma. Dr. P. A. Sorokin, in "The Crisis of our Age", proved to me, however, that what a man or nation believes, determines what he or it is and does. Even so, I would hesitate to question any dogma (as, for instance, the Virgin Birth) had it not been claimed by the Archbishop that this evidenced a special intervention by the Deity which, in turn, created a tension between science and religion. 15

May I not, therefore, first comment upon the reply which the Archbishop so very kindly made to my letter to you? I had suggested that if, as insisted by the 

Archbishop in "Nature, Man and God," page 267, no law of nature as discovered by physical science is ultimate, then, in such event, a vital tension would seem to be created between science and religion; and this because the entire structure of modern science rests upon an opposite belief and assertion. 

To this observation the Archbishop replied, "That the word 'ultimate' is treated [i.e., by me] as if it were synonymous with 'true'." "That," adds the Archbishop, "is not the meaning of the word, or the intention of the writer. 

A law may be completely true and yet not ultimate, that is, not the last word. The last word on any theistic hypothesis is the purpose of God."

I can easily understand how a law may be apparently true and yet not either ultimate or the last word; as witness the law of gravity as interpreted by Sir Isaac Newton, and as more recently and more adequately (so far as I am able to judge) interpreted by Professor Albert Einstein. I cannot, however, quite see the logic of the Archbishop's above observation. For if it be admitted that any law of nature is completely true, must it not so far as it goes, register the purpose of God and, therefore, be ultimate? But, if so, would this not be all that either religion or science could insist upon?

As to my further observation that if, as claimed by the Archbishop, the Creator is omniscient, His laws would logically be ultimate as claimed by science; that is, they would not require change or modification from time to time at the hands of an omniscient Creator to meet "unforeseen circumstances," because no such circumstances would be unforeseen. To this the Archbishop, in his letter, replies, "Omniscience would not involve a com- 16

plete foreknowledge (i.e., on the part of the Creator) because it would be an eternal comprehension of time, not a knowledge at all points of time concerning all other points of time." But if the Creator's laws are not ultimate because the Creator is ignorant of certain "points of time," is this not tantamount to a confession that the 

Creator is not omniscient, but only almost omniscient?

Such an admission might have been grasped with avidity by Henri Bergson, but would seem to be inconsistent with the assertion made by Christian theology. If, however, the Creator is omniscient, and if, therefore, He has not only an inclusive comprehension of time, but also of all points in time, then would His laws not necessarily be ultimate, as science alleges that they are?

The question of a tension between science and religion seems to me to be so vital that I shall accept, I confess with trepidation, your suggestion that I might add a further comment to what I have already said. The Archbishop, in the profoundly stimulating work under discussion, clearly shows why he believes that such tension exists. He says (page 293), "A purely transcendent God who intervenes often to give special direction to the course of events, is incompatible with a scientific apprehen- ✓ sion of the world, while a purely transcendent God who never intervenes at all, or has done so only once or twice in recorded history, is incompatible with vital religion." The Archbishop then adds (page 294), “If uniformity in nature is not only usual but universal, still more, if it is necessary, the Personality of God is denied while if the Personality of God is affirmed, this affirmation carries with it the essential variability of nature." "There is," says the Archbishop, "a sharp choice to be made here; the tension between the habit of mind congenial to religion and the habit of mind congenial to science is acute." The way to mitigate this tension, adds 17

the Archbishop, is to recognize the necessity and origin of these views, so that neither science nor religion may encroach one upon the other.

I find trouble in accepting these two alternatives as embracing the entire problem; that is, I hesitate to believe the Creator either intervenes to give special direction in the sense which the Archbishop has indicated, or, in the alternative, that He never intervenes at all. I think of the Creator as constantly intervening, but that such intervention will be in accordance with His all-wise laws. 

The presence of universal law does not, as already observed, indicate to me the existence of a mechanistic universe, especially when I observe the presence of free will in man.* Rather, it indicates a creative order, instead of chaos, which latter would logically be expected, if the universe were the product of blind forces. 

Turning again to the argument of the Archbishop, when I find it unnecessary to believe in special intervention on the part of the Creator, I find it unnecessary to believe in the materialistic incident (i.e., the Virgin 

Birth) referred to by the Archbishop as evidencing such intervention. I notice in this connection that in his letter in response to my letter to you, the Archbishop frankly admits that the Virgin Birth would in no way prove Christ's divinity. But if so, why may we not leave this incident, which has, through the ages, become clothed with beautiful connotations, to be accepted or rejected by the given individual, rather than insist that it be believed and accepted as an article of Christian faith?

Again, finding it unnecessary to believe in a special intervention, I cannot follow the Archbishop's statement referring to the Virgin Birth, when he says, page 315, "Revelation is at its highest where God finds occasion for unusual action." I feel deeply convinced that the opposite is true. For illustration, the birth of a human. *The Quantum Theory seems consistent with the assertion of "free will." 18

being would certainly seem to be a "usual and normal" event, and yet, upon reflection, could any revelation be more mysterious and unthinkable than this? Here, almost as it were before our very eyes, two tiny cells called genes proceed without conscious guidance to produce a human body and brain possessing literally astonishing powers and potentialities.

The Archbishop perceives clearly what I am trying to say. Thus, as already observed (page 46) he remarks, "Jesus of Nazareth taught men to see the operation of God in the regular and normal. The normal was taken for granted (i.e., by ordinary men). The astonishing and unpredictable were regarded as acts of God." . . . "If men," adds the Archbishop, "had been ready to follow Him in this, much of the conflict between science and religion would have been avoided."

Oddly enough, the Archbishop would seem to have himself fallen into the very error which the author of the Christian religion avoided; for he rests his assertion that there is a tension between science and religion, not upon the "regular and normal," that is, not upon events happening according to the laws of the Creator, but upon an event alleged to prove a special intervention on the part of the Deity.

Finally the Archbishop in his reply alleges that tension arises from the "difference in the angles of approach. adopted by Science and Religion." Such difference of approach exists and is real. Science arrives at the truth by means which Sir Arthur Eddington brilliantly describes as "pointer readings," that is, by entities which may be verified, which may, in a sense, be weighed and measured, and the results of which operation may be represented by a mathematical equation. Religion, on the other hand, is primarily concerned with Goodness, Beauty, and certain aspects of Truth, which cannot be so 19

measured. The difference of approach should, however, of itself, create no tension between the two. "Such tension also exists," adds the Archbishop, "because Religion always contains assurances which 

Science cannot warrant." I have reserved this consideration until the last because it is a most difficult one for a layman to face. Assurances may be made on behalf of both Science and Religion which create a vital tension between the two, as, for illustration, when one claiming to speak on behalf of Science asserts that man is the product of blind force. Science should, however, not be pilloried because one individual has sought to so speak on its behalf. So when the Archbishop claims that the 

Virgin Birth evidences a special intervention on the part of the Deity, tending to prove that "no law of nature as discovered by physical science is ultimate,” he manifestly creates a tension with Science. You have assured me that the Author of the Christian Religion never claimed that he was born of a virgin. With his clear understanding he would have perceived that such incident, if true, would have been irrelevant. He saw over and beyond material phenomena. He announced that the Creator is a spirit. 

His sincere but simple-minded apostles are credited, it is true, with a creed containing various materialistic assertions not authenticated by him. Why should he be held responsible for such assertions? Why should we cling to the materialistic assertions in the Apostles' Creed, which assertions are not authenticated by the author of the Christian Religion? We would, in attempting to understand the philosophy of Immanuel Kant, look primarily to what that great philosopher himself said rather than to anything which his faithful servant, Lamke, may have believed.

The Author of the Christian Religion did give an assurance to which I shall refer, namely, that self-con- 20

sciousness may, in certain instances, persist after death.

Such assurance may be erroneous. It is, however, in any event neither materialistic nor trivial.

The fact that Science and Religion may not be able to warrant each the assurance of the other, should not, however, necessarily create a tension between the two, or prove that either is erroneous. Thus, Professor Albert

Einstein, on behalf of Science, has given an assurance of the validity of the Quantum Theory, as well as that of Relativity. When interpreting certain "laws of nature," he has announced the amazing abstraction that "the ten coefficients of curvature are zero in empty space." The 

Christian religion can scarcely warrant the accuracy of these assertions. They cannot be measured by the means upon which religion relies to reach the truth. No tension. would, however, seem to be created between the two because of such assertions.

The Author of the Christian Religion has given the assurance already mentioned, that is, that self-consciousness may, at least in certain instances, persist after man's physical body, upon which during life it seems to depend, has fallen into decay. Science cannot, in turn, vouch for the validity of this assurance. It cannot be verified by "pointer readings." It cannot be represented by a mathematical equation. It would, however, seem to create no necessary tension with Science, and seems to contain certain evidences which point in the direction of its validity. 

May a layman, alive to his profound limitations, make bold to refer to several of these considerations?

In the first place, we laymen must frankly acknowledge that we are not dealing with mathematical certainties; that apart from faith, we are merely attempting to weigh probabilities. We must also frankly confess that we are intensely materialistic in mind and thought. Our life has been closely associated with "substance and matter,' 21

What is matter?) with entities which we touch and which we believe that we can see. We confuse the concrete with the real. We fail to observe that the above quoted assurance made on behalf of Science, that "the ten coefficients of curvature are zero in empty space," is, by its very terms, dealing with an entity void of "substance." Nor do we remember that during our own life 

Science has deserted the material atom as the corner-stone of the cosmos, and has become far less dogmatic with regard to "substance and matter" than was formerly the case. Thus, Sir Arthur Eddington, in his illuminating volume entitled "The Nature of the Physical 

World," observes (page 259), "The Victorian physicist felt that he knew just what he was talking about when he used the terms 'matter' and 'atoms'." Atoms were tiny billiard-balls, a crisp statement which was supposed to tell you all about their nature in a way which could never be achieved for transcendental things like consciousness, beauty or humor. But now we realize that 

Science has nothing to say as to the intrinsic nature of the atom. The author adds, page 274, "In the scientific world the conception of substance is wholly lacking, and that which most nearly replaces it, viz., electrical charge, is not exalted as a star performer above other entities of physics. For this reason the scientific world often shocks us by its appearance of unreality." 

Again, we laymen ignore the profound mysteries which surround man on every side and concentrate our attention only upon phenomena which we believe to be unusual. 

Thus, we ignore the profound mystery of birth, a phenomenon which might of itself tend to prove that man cannot be the product of blind force, and become aroused only when a particular birth, no more or less mysterious (virgin birth), is alleged to have occurred.

So, also, of the assurance now under consideration. We 22

ignore the profound, almost infinite mystery arising from the existence of self-consciousness. We do this only because self-consciousness seems to us to be "usual and ordinary," - because we possess it, because it is the very essence of our being. We become highly skeptical, however, when we are assured that this amazing entity may exist, as would be the case in life after physical death, in a different location (i.e., when not in man's brain) from that in which we have found it, and when no longer associated with a particular "substance" with which we are assured by anatomists it is associated, i. e., carbon. Our skepticism arises, therefore, not because of the profoundly mysterious character of self-consciousness itself, but only from the fact that we are assured that it may exist and continue when no longer found in the particular location and association believed by us to be regular and normal. 

Turning, then, to the assurance under consideration, is it not illogical, almost parochial, to believe that this marvellous gift of self-consciousness exists only on our earth, an infinitely small speck in a stupendous cosmos, and that it exists, even here, only when associated with a certain substance (carbon)? If this were true, what a mysterious, priceless entity this marvelous substance would be! Is it not more logical to believe that the self-consciousness which we possess is only one aspect of something permeating space? Herbert Spencer, with his keen analytical me mind, attempted to explain the origin of self-consciousness in man. In the end, however, he acknowledged defeat and admitted that consciousness probably exists throughout the cosmos. The course of evolution on this earth seems to prove this same thing. It also tends to throw a flood of light upon the assurance under consideration. In this connection I came only recently upon an address given in 1923 by George Santayana, at Oxford; it was the "Herbert Spencer Lecture" of that year. It seems so directly in point that I shall venture to quote briefly from 23

it. The speaker there said: "Evolution, in the proper sense of that word, is not a mere flux expected to be endless. Evolution must have a goal. It must unfold a germ in a determinate direction towards an implicit ideal." 

To speak exactly, however, evolution itself can have no goal. It can have no determinate direction towards an implicit ideal. It can mark only the path of either blind force, or of an intelligent creative effort.* It is precisely because blind force can have no goal, can possess no implicit ideal, that the existence of a conscious Creator seems a logical if not necessary explanation of creation. 

In "A Layman Looks at Evolution," I attempted, from a layman's point of view, to develop this thought. I there pointed to considerations which seem to me to be highly suggestive, and which also bear upon the assurance now under consideration, namely, that evolution evidences a determinate direction, that it proceeds from diffusion to unity, from simplicity to complexity, that it proceeds not downward, nor horizonally, but upward, that it tends to prove affirmatively the existence of a Creator who possesses a "goal," an "implicit ideal" that it would seem to prove further that such goal is not physical, as evidenced by man's body, nor even solely mental, as evidenced by his prodigiously marvellous brain; but that such goal seems to be moral or "spiritual," (if a layman may be permitted to use that term,) and that the latter may be evidenced by that strange quality of recent appearance called "conscience," a necessary ingredient of character.** ("Dawn of Conscience," by Dr. Breasted.)

Observe now the light which the answer to the question just considered seems to throw upon the assurance under consideration. If, on the one hand, man is the product of *There may be some third alternative, but, if so, it is beyond my understanding. ** The writer was very much encouraged by the comment of Professor Albert Einstein that his effort above mentioned was "intelligent and eloquent." 24

blind force, then we must conclude that Science, as well as Religion, is a myth and delusion, this because science rests upon the existence of law, and law and blind force are mutually exclusive concepts. If, on the other hand, we believe that man is the product of a Creator, having a "goal," an "implicit ideal," then, in such event, the assurance of the continuance of an invisible life, no longer associated with "substance and matter," would seem to be logical because such Creator is himself invisible, and not associated with substance or matter. 

If, again, man is the product of such a Creator, why would it be reasonable to believe that man's body can represent his goal, especially when mind, personality, self-consciousness, and memory are manifestations evidencing that the Creator has already shot beyond this physical goal? Or, why would it be unreasonable to believe that a Creator purposing this goal would not grant to certain individuals, worthy of that gift, the opportunity of striving further to reach the same? To say that the door is closed the moment when man's body disappears, would almost seem to be a sad anti-climax to the preceding mysterious and splendid effort. 

The tension, as it seems to me, therefore, is not between the teachings of the Author of the Christian Religion and Modern Science, but is one between a crass materialistic conception of the cosmos on the one hand, and Modern Science and enlightened Christian opinion on the other. If, as alleged by materialistic philosophy, man, is the product of "blind force," then, as observed, mind, self-consciousness, memory, personality, etc., of which we are personally so intimately aware, become curious, inexplicable, illogical phenomena. In that case, the further existence of that extraordinary gift possessed only by man, i.e., free will, would witness only a unique accident. Then also the opportunity of one possessing free will to become, 25

in a real sense, a man possessing conscience and character, rather than an automaton, would entirely lose its value as a possible evidence of purpose on the part of the Creator. 

If, on the other hand, as affirmed by the Author of the Christian Religion, man is the product of a conscious power behind and responsible for phenomena, then the penetrating intuitions of the Author of the Christian Religion would seem to become logical and reasonably probable.

I hope that what I have been trying to say is not merely an idle suggestion. I am, however, here reminded of the statement of Socrates (related in the "PHAEDO") where he said, "The difference between him [i.e., a certain partisan] and me at the present moment is only this: whereas he seeks to convince his hearers that what he says is true, I am seeking to convince myself." I am here seeking to convince myself that there is no real tension between Science and the Christian Religion, and that there are considerations pointing towards the validity of the assurance above considered. 

It was at your suggestion that I wrote my comments on "Nature, Man and God," and later forwarded them to the Archbishop. It was again at your suggestion that I wrote this comment, somewhat delayed, upon his kindly reply. The Archbishop, for whom you and the Christian world had such a profound admiration has, alas! been taken from us. I am therefore making bold to lay the issue of a tension between Science and Religion, and particularly the reasonableness of the assurance above considered, before you, who are so free from the fetters of dogma, and in a true sense so deeply religious. 

I hope that if you have the opportunity, I may have your conclusions thereon. I know that you will appreciate the fact that all I have said is tentative, and that I 26

have been journeying in an unfamiliar country traversed by deep waters. 

Yours most cordially,

JOHN H. S. LEE. 

CONCLUSION BY DR. HORACE J. BRIDGES 

November 21, 1945. 

John H. S. Lee, Esq. 

Dear John:

I have re-read with keen pleasure your statement in reply to Archbishop Temple's "tension" argument, and with still deeper interest your pages on the question of survival. What follows is merely a postscript, heartily endorsing your position and briefly supplementing your argument.

I think it important that the things you have said should be said to the widest possible public*. It isn't merely that they are true, but that they deal with the most vital and fundamental issues with which our distracted age is confronted. The matter at stake is no less than this: 

Is there any warrant in Reality for the ethical law, "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself"? Is conscience an authoritative reality (like reason, when it tells of the difference between true and false), or is it a delusion or an imposture, the survival of primitive superstitions played on by self-interested kings, priests, etc.? 

What you call the "blind-force" view of the genesis of the world and man leads straight to the latter view; and the latter view, as recent history has shown, leads straight to Nazism and its sequels. Now it is this that gives such tremendous practical importance to what may seem at first blush a merely academic discussion. Men,civilized men, cannot live either individually or collec- I had suggested to Dr. Bridges that I doubted whether my consideration of this subject would be of any general interest. 27

tively without an Ethical Religion; and this means not a mere code or set of precepts (which might be quite arbitrary, like primitive taboos), but a solidly justified body of convictions.

Now, of course, Dr. Temple's whole life-work and every word of his books was devoted to vindicating this great truth. So far you and I are heartily at one with him. But we two are convinced that by certain of his contentions he imperils the cause for which he is fighting. 

If so, it is of primary importance to correct him on those issues. What you, in your modesty, don't realize is the immense value and importance of having this argument presented by a layman. 

With regard to the Virgin Birth: Throughout the centuries theologians have in fact used the doctrine of the Virgin Birth to prove the divinity of Christ. Now 

Dr. Temple simply turns that argument upside down and stands it on its head. The divinity of Christ, he tells us, proves the Virgin Birth and is not proved by it. I believe with you that the Virgin Birth, if true, would attest no special intervention on the part of the Deity, and is no evidence of divinity. 

I agree also that there is no tension between the teachings of Christ and modern science. I will limit myself here to saying that any tension, when it exists, can only arise from unwarrantable assertions made on behalf of either science or religion or both. 

To work this out fully would involve an analysis of, e.g., the extra-scientific utterances too often indulged in by scientific men, and the unfortunate readiness of many readers to clothe these "obiter dicta" with the authority which justly pertains to the speakers only so long as they remain within the sphere of their expertness and report what they can verify within that sphere. For instance, you quote some unnamed scientist (and it is very kind of you not to name him) as asserting that "man is the product 28

of blind force". That is sheer dogma, whoever says it; it is not only baseless, but contradicts the very basis of rational thought. As Schopenhauer long ago said, "It is the absurd undertaking of materialism to derive the subject from the object".

On the other side, religion (and specifically the Christian religion as set forth by its Founder) has too often been most unconscionably burdened with heavy accretions of dogma for which no warrant or basis exists in the recorded teaching of the said Founder. It was much later that the religion of Jesus was transformed into a metaphysical theology about Jesus; and this not only without his authority, but in defiance of sundry indications that he would have deprecated such procedure. The tendency to such "development" may have been natural, but it is nevertheless unfortunate, and has led to very lamentable results.

With regard to the argument of the Archbishop resting upon the assertion that "God is a Person," or upon "the Personality of God," the whole trouble arises from our persistent habit of prisoning the ultimate (and inscrutable) Reality within the framework of our idea of Personality. Since all we know of personality is the conceptual translation of our experience of ourselves, we fall into inevitable and inexplicable confusion and bewilderment when we undertake to construe the whole in terms of the part which emerges within it. This is where we need the sort of correction which is partly supplied by Bergson's philosophy.

Once at a meeting of the Aristotelian Society in London to which a friend invited me, I had the good fortune to meet the late Professor Wildon Carr, the friend of Bergson who translated some of his books. In the course of the discussion that evening, Dr. Carr found occasion to remark that Bergson's was a philosophy which sought to 29

account for intellect "within the whole of Reality, but not as the whole of Reality."

The Archbishop persistently talks about "the Personality of God" in a fashion implying that Personality in that connection expresses a clear-cut and fully comprehended idea, insomuch that we can confidently say what sort of behavior on the part of "Nature" and "Nature's Laws" is consistent or inconsistent with it. (Cf. the quotation from "Nature, Man and God," page 294, given in your letter). Now I protest that all this is unwarranted dogmatism, and that the central idea in it stands in contradiction with manifest facts. The Ultimate 

Reality produces all that is, including human personality; and that (which means you and me and the rest of us) is made what it is by its limitations. 

What happens when we start to apply this experience of ourselves to the Ultimate Reality?-i.e., when we talk of the personality of God and proceed to reason from it as from a known quantity in mathematics or a defined premise in logic? Why, we begin by cancelling from personality the very elements upon which its meaning depends, the very terms by which it is defined. . . . 

As to the question of self-consciousness existing and being manifested apart from physical bodies such as ours, we must begin by frankly recognizing, as you do,--that proof, in the scientific sense, is out of the question. (If 

I remember rightly, Archbishop Temple somewhere makes the shrewd observation that scientific proof of man's survival of death, if it were possible, would be positively undesirable. I think a strong case could be made for that contention.) But when it comes to balancing probabilities, I feel very strongly, and the more strongly the older I grow, that the preponderance in favor of an affirmative conclusion is overwhelming. Arguments for this view are scattered through several of my 30

books. Perhaps in addition to the considerations you have mentioned the most impressive is the unity and continuity of personality, as contrasted with the multiplicity and discontinuity of the body. (I mean multiplicity of parts and organs, and of the elements into which these are analyzable.)

The body is a process of change, so rapid and complete that after a few years, the scientists tell us, every atom of it has been displaced and replaced. How, then, account for the continuity of memory, on any hypothesis which would make self-consciousness a product or effect of the body? And if self-consciousness can, as we see it does, survive the perpetual changes of its physical substratum, and not merely survive but progress toward ever fuller integration and richer content, isn't it preposterous dogmatism to affirm, on the strength of no evidence at all, that its survival of the ultimate physical change called death is impossible? Granted, we can't prove that it does survive; still, such analogies as experience affords suggest that it may do so. 

That is on the purely "scientific" plane. There are besides grave metaphysical, ethical and religious considerations, outside the scope of physical science, for maintaining that it should. With some of these you have dealt, as when you point out the "sad anti-climax" to the ascending evolutionary effort that the extinction of selfconsciousness by bodily death would constitute. There are such impressive evidences of purposeful reason in the world that we should not lightly convict it of ultimate irrationality. Yet the blind destruction of its highest products (mind and conscience) would be hard to interpret otherwise than as proof of such irrationality,or at least of an indifference to the values it has brought forth which would seem to negative any ethical character in the world-process and its cause. 31

So for my part I put my trust in the intuitions of the greatest souls who have illumined man's dark pathway. 

Dr. Einstein tells us that his own marvelous mind works by the intuitive method. He first has his insights, and then verifies them. Well, Socrates and Jesus (to name only two of the supreme spirits) had this intuition. They trusted the "intimations of immortality". Finding not one jot or tittle of scientific evidence to prove, or even hint, that they were wrong, I am content humbly to trust that they were right,—and that the world is not irrational enough to have proved them wrong by extinguishing their infinitely precious spirits. 

Cordially yours,

HORACE J. BRIDGES.
