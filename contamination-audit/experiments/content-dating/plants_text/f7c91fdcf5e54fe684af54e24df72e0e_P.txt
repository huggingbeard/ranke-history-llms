INTRODUCTION This report has been prepared with the idea of aiding city attorneys in solving a presently existing and very practical problem. Many cities are planning or have completed plans for a housing program in cooperation with the United States Housing Authority. Section 10 (a) of the "United States Housing Act of 1937" provides in part: "That no annual contributions shall be made, and the Authority shall enter into no contract guaranteeing any annual contribution in connection with the development of any low-rent housing or slum-clearance project involving the construction of new dwellings, unless the project includes the emilination by demolition, condemnation, and effective closing, or the compulsory repair or improvement of unsafe or insanitary dwellings situated in the local or metropolitan area, substantially equal in number to the number of newly constructed dwellings provided by the project; except that such elimination may, in the discretion of the Authority, be deferred in any locality or metropolitan area where the shortage of decent, safe, or sanitary housing available to families of low income is so acute as to force dangerous overcrowding of such families." At the present time 33 states have passed statutes which provide for the creation of municipal housing authorities to cooperate with the United Sattes Housing Authority in carrying out the program of the "United States Housing Act of 1937." There are about 159 local housing authorities already in existence.
Although municipal housing authorities can secure money to erect low-cost-housing projects now, the above-quoted provision forbids annual contributions from the housing authority unless unsafe and insanitary dwellings substantially equal in number to the number of newly constructed dwellings are demolished, vacated, or repaired. Therefore, those municipalities which have applied for and secured, or which intend to apply for and secure, funds from the United States Housing Authority and the annual contributions contained therein should, at this time, begin making plans for elimination by demolition, vacation, or compulsory repair of unsafe or insanitary dwellings.
Each municipality desiring to participate in a USHA program will, therefore, be practically concerned with the ways and means through legal process of accomplishing this requirement at the lowest possible cost to the city. The particular purpose of this report will be to present, in both its practical and legal aspects, one of the available legal powers for accomplishing this purpose, i. e., the police power.
This report presents the actual experience of cities in dealing with comulsory repair, vacation or demolition problems, the interpretations of the courts of the various acts of the municipalities directed toward this end, and the limitations upon the use of the police power when invoked.
While emphasizing the immediate relation of the problems here treated to the USHA requirements, no city should overlook the great value of exercising to the fullest extent the police power in eradicating slum areas as a matter of purely local welfare regardless of the National housing program.
The city attorney whose city does not plan a housing program will be interested in re-examining his local ordinances in the light of the experience and law herein restated with a view toward cleaning up slum areas as a general municipal improvement. The cost to municipal governments of police, fire and health protection in slum areas is higher than in the less concentrated areas of the city, and the municipal tax income from slum areas is lower than from decent dwelling or business areas. To provide a plan for getting rid of such a liability would be rendering a real service to the city by any municipal law department.
In collecting the materials presented in this report, a questionnaire was sent out to representative cities with specific questions relating to their experience in solving the problem of Compulsory Repair, Vacation or Demolition of Dwellings.
The report reflects the actual experience of 21 cities. If the police power can be used to meet slum-clearance problems, the foundation for such use must be found in the actual experience of municipalities up to the present time. To tabulate and enumerate the experience of all cities would, of course, be an impractical and cumbersome task. It is our hope that, by presenting a cross-section of the experience of representative municipalities, the city attorney will be able to get a clear picture of the problems here involved and be able better to work out a solution which will meet the needs of his municipality.
As a general statement, it may be said that most municipalities have had some experience in dealing with Compulsory Repair, Vacation or Demolition of Dwellings, and that, with the passage of recent housing legislation, cities are more than ever concerned with this problem. It is our hope that by use of the materials herein presented cities can proceed to use to the utmost extent the police power to cause eradication of slum buildings and at the same time provide a procedure which will make the cost to the city of such action very low or non-existent.
Because the use of the power of eminent domain implies that the city will pay for property acquired under such power, the power of eminent domain is not discussed herein (except as referred to in cases arising under recent Federal-state and municipal legislation). There is presented as an appendix to this report a comprehensive summary of reported cases interpreting recent municipal state-Federal housing and slumclearance legislation. Police power is treated herein as a separate and independent means for housing control. Its economic importance as a bargaining weapon in conjunction with the use of the power of eminent domain should not be ignored.

This report, by dealing with the experience of cities in using the police power in Compulsory Repair, Vacation or Demolition of Dwellings and thereby presenting the actual use of that power, the administrative procedure used, court interpretations and limitations of the power and the necessary procedure, and a digest of decisions interpreting recent Federal-state and municipal legislation, presents materials city attorneys will find of value in directing the legal action of their cities in these matters.
We might indicate that the major questions raised as to the use of the police power in this matter are: (1) When is the use of this power justified?
(2) What administrative procedure is required?
(3) What standards for administrative action are necessary?
(4) Are notice and a hearing essential?
(a) When are they not necessary?-When necessary?
(5) How can administrative procedure be set up so as to avoid extended court litigation?
(6) How can an administrative finding of fact that a building is a nuisance be made final if based upon evidence to support it so as to avoid a different finding of fact by a jury if the city is later sued for damages for demolishing a building found to be a nuisance by the administrative board?
(a) Involved here, of course, is whether or not an appeal to the courts is or can be provided under state law from the administrative boards decision-thus complying with due process requirements.
(7) How can the costs of compulsory repair or demolition be assessed against the property owner-thus eliminating expense to the city?
From the experience of the representative cities and the legal materials herein set forth, we hope city attorneys will receive helpful suggestions as to the proper solutions to the above problems.
We have included in the appendix a suggested model ordinance drafted to offer assistance to city attorneys in working out any amendments they might deem necessary to their existing ordinances. Criticisms of this ordinance are invited. The ordinance will be criticized by city attorneys attending the midyear meeting of the Institute on July 18 and 19, 1938, at Washington, D. C. A supplemental report will then be issued containing the criticisms and suggestions of this proposed ordinance, and in the supplemental report the ordinance adopted at the Institute's mid-year meeting will be set forth, fully annotated.

GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF LAW APPLICABLE TO THE EXERCISE OF POLICE POWER IN MUNICIPAL COMPULSORY REPAIR, VACATION OR DEMOLITION OF DWELLINGS Mcquillin on Municipal Corporations (2d ed.), in Section 1020, states as follows: "Removal of Buildings. Usually buildings and other structures erected in violation of valid fire ordinances may be removed summarily by the municipal corporation, without resort to formal judicial proceedings, since such power is not a forfeiture of private property, but is merely the exercise of police jurisdiction. Sometimes notice and hearing is required. Ordinances often require unsafe buildings to be repaired, pursuant to municipal regulations or else be torn down by the corporate authorities. Obviously such requirement is strictly within the police power and essential to the public safety. However, in the exercise of such power the acting officers must observe the property rights of the owner. Unless it is apparent that delay would imperil life and limb, usually the offending building must be condemned, and notice and hearing given the owner or agent, and an adjudication that the structure is a nuisance declared. However, it should be borne in mind that 'police powers of this character are of a high order, and when a case arises should be speedily exercised. Delay incident to the ordinary process of law' would seriously impair their efficiency and could not be tolerated.
"In the execution of the provisions of ordinances, without judicial investigation, these facts should appear: First, that the building or structure was either erected or permitted to remain in violation of law; and, second, that in tearing it down reasonable care was taken to preserve the materials, and avoid unnecessary damage." In Section 938, the same author states: "To employ, in substance, the language of the Supreme Court of the United States in a leading case, under the police power it has been judicially declared repeatedly that the state may order the destruction of a house falling to decay or otherwse endangering the lives of passers-by; the demolition of such as are in the path of a conflagration; the slaughter of diseased cattle; the destruction of decayed or unwholesome food; the prohibition of wooden buildings in cities; the regulation of railways and other means of public conveyance, and of interments in burial grounds; the restriction of objectionable trades to certain localities; the compulsory vaccination of children, the confinement of the insane or those afflicted with contagious diseases; the restraint of vagrants, beggars, and habitual drunkards; the suppression of obscene publications and houses of ill-fame; and the prohibition of gambling houses and places where intoxicating liquors are sold." A comprehensive digest of the cases involving questions of municipal Compulsory Repair, Vacation or Demolition of Dwellings is given herein after the section on actual experience of municipalities in dealing with problems of Compulsory Repair, Vacation or Demolition of Dwellings.
See also Section 956 of Mcquillin, on Municipal Power to Declare and Define Nuisances; and Section 959, on Power to Abate Nuisances-costs.
SOCIAL AND ECONOMIC EFFECT OF SLUMS ON URBAN LIFE "Slum areas are the breeding places of disease which take toll not only from its denizens, but, by spread from the inhabitants of the entire city and state Juvenile delinquency, crime and immorality are there born, find protection, and flourish Enormous economic loss results directly from the necessary expenditure of public funds to maintain health and hospital services for afflicted slum dwellers and to war against crime and immorality Indirectly, there is an equally heavy capital loss and a diminishing return in taxes because of the areas blighted by the existence of the slums." The above quotation from the case of New York City Housing Authority v. Muller, 270 N. Y. 333, 1 N. E.) Numerous extensive surveys have demonstrated the clear relation of slum areas to the health, safety, morals and general welfare, not only of the inhabitants of those areas, but of the public at large, at least within the general local area. Slum and blighted areas are now universally recognized as fertile breeding grounds for crime, moral delinquency and disease.
In addition to the social costs resulting from the negative effects of these factors, the actual cost to local authorities in serving slum areas is grossly disproportionate to the contribution from those areas to the general support of government.
This situation is the direct result of the character of the areas themselves. The facts demonstrating the relation of bad housing to the general welfare are clearly stated in P.W.A.
Housing Division Bulletins 1 and 2, by Edith Elmer Wood.
See especially Bulletin No. 1 at pages 7-16.
The recognition of governmental responsibility with respect to slum areas is not a new or startling concept. Much difficulty has been experienced, however, in the effective methods of dealing with the problem. Original attempts of local authorities at slum clearance were unsatisfactory and produced little result. The general conception of government as a policeman acting properly only to avoid conflict between individuals has proved entirely inadequate. Private initiative and government regulation as a means of housing control and slum clearance are insufficient. The use of the police power in the demolition of dangerous dwellings is not new. The early examples of the exercise of police power in this connection, however, have been limited to the removal of fire hazards in specific and individual cases. The facts establishing and spelling out the relation of slum problems to the general welfare clearly establish the necessity for the use of both the police power and the power of eminent domain as governmental instruments for dealing appropriately with the housing problem. The situation is one which has become recognized as of national importance and uniformly national in scope. The Federal Government has inaugurated a housing program on a long term basis. Because of the local character of the immediate issues involved, however, this Federal program leaves to the municipal authorities the responsibility and initiative of dealing with their problems. Any study of the governmental powers appropriate to the solution or control of the housing problem must be approached in view of the present Federal program. The particular problems and principles which must be considered, however, are basic problems of the scope and limits of the exercise of the local police powers and powers of eminent domain.

A. What Standards Have Been Passed for Buildings Used for Residential Purposes?
All cities referred to their building codes. The building codes referred to are, of course, quite lengthy and no useful purpose could be served by quoting them extensively here.
In general, as to new buildings, they provide regulations and restrictions as to the location, areas, dimensions, and standards as to light, ventilation, bathroom facilities, electrical fixtures, exit facilities, materials of construction used, load factors of floors, wind resistance factors of walls, etc.
As a general rule, the ordinances referred to provide that where a building is unsafe, dangerous or a fire hazard, it shall not be used for dwelling purposes. Some of the codes provide that in cases where the building is damaged to an extent of 50 per cent it shall be vacated, torn down, and not used for any purpose. Other than the standards laid down for new buildings being erected, the codes do not make any distinction between buildings used for residential purposes and business purposes. The emphasis is placed upon the dangerous character of the building for whatever purpose used.
In working out standards to guide administrative discretion for different purposes some distinctions may well be taken between terms. To ground summary abatement of a housing nuisance, the officer acting is usually guided in his discretion by the broad standard, "emergency requiring immediate action." To justify vacation orders the standard of "unsafe, unsanitary, dangerous, or unfit" for human habitation or occupancy is usually applied. To justify orders to repair the standard is vague or not expressed in the ordinances examined, but the discretion of administrative officers in ordering repairs should be guided by the effectiveness of repairs to remove the unsafe, unsanitary, or dangerous condition which threatens the occupants if the building is to be used or the public in any case. The usual standard to be applied in ordering demolition rather than vacation or repair is "damage beyond 50 per cent of the normal condition".
Citations to the sections of the building codes here referred to are found in the appendix.

What Administrative Procedure Is Provided to Determine the Necessity of Repair, Vacation or Demolition? Is a Court Hearing Necessary?
Twenty of the cities whose experience is here recorded have ordinances requiring express notice to the owner, occupant, mortgagee, or lessee of the building affected. The City of Seattle, Washington, reports the following: "The city does not usually initiate such proceedings in the first instance. Petitions for abatement are from time to time received by the City Council, whose procedure it is to refer them to a committee composed of representatives of the Building, Fire and Health Departments. Such committee investigates the facts and reports to the City Council with recommendation for or against abatement.
"For some time this city followed the safe but rather expensive and cumbersome policy of legislatively declaring the individual buildings to be nuisances because of certain 'nuisance' features and bringing a civil action to abate the same in each instance; individual judgments were entered and the Sheriff directed by the court to abate the premises.
"The present procedure which has been in effect for the past two or three years involves a petition to the City Council by protesting property owners, a reference to the abatement committee above referred to, a report back from such committee and the passage of an ordinance declaring the nuisance and the basis of such legislative declaration and authorizing an administrative officer of the city, usually the Chief of the Fire Department because most of the buildings concerned are fire menaces, to summarily abate the nuisance by demolishing the building." Chicago reports: "No preliminary court hearing is necessary. The Building Department and Board of Health administer the ordinances leading to repair or vacation. The Department of Buildings is concerned with demolition. Steps in process: (a) Notice of violation is sent to owner and tenant as a result of investigation by sanitary inspector or building inspector.
(b) Then a 'No Compliance' letter is sent to owner and tenant.
(c) If still no compliance, a hearing is held in which the owner is required to show cause why he has not complied with order. Owner may ask and receive extension or the case is sent for prosecution.

From this brief outline of the chief provisions of the administrative procedure provided to determine the necessity of repair, vacation or demolition, it would seem that the usual procedure requires a notice to the owner to repair, vacate or demolish the building itself, and in a few instances, such notice is followed by a hearing. It might be pointed out that, in further analysis of the specific provisions of the building codes here under discussion, in most cases a provision is made for a building inspector whose duty it is to inspect buildings or to investigate complaints made by residents of the city about buildings which are dilapidated, unsafe, dangerous or insanitary, and it is, in practically all cases, such building inspector who issues the original notice to the owner to repair, vacate or demolish the building. "Tenants of houses that are demolished are generally housed under private or semi-private auspices." In many cases owners of buildings have stood by and watched cities demolish their buildings, ignoring the notice and order that the property owner himself do so, and then have sued the city or person doing the demolishing in the civil courts on the theory that the building was not, in fact, a nuisance, so the city had no right to abate it. In those cases, hereinafter cited and analyzed, the courts invariably say that the question as to whether or not the building was, in fact, a nuisance is a question inquiry into which is not precluded in cases where the city has only notified the owner to remove, demolish, vacate or repair, without granting to the owner a court hearing or, at least, an administrative hearing from which an appeal can be taken to the courts. To forestall such after-the-fact judgment, an administrative procedure should be worked out and followed which would make the administrative determination as to the existence of the nuisance conclusive on the facts, or in the alternative, provide for an appeal which would allow reversal of an administrative determination which was clearly founded upon no evidence and wrong. Administrative procedure and court review of administrative procedure should precede actual demolition of the building in question. Special provisions for summary action to take care of emergency situations must, of course, be provided and judicial review allowed after the fact. In drafting the model ordinance set forth in the appendix we have taken the administrative procedural problems set forth above into consideration.

B. Ordinance Authorizing Action Beyond Charter Powers Is Invalid.
In the case of City of St. Louis v. Kaime Bro. Real Estate Co., 180 Mo. 309 (1904), there was involved an action by the City of St. Louis to recover a fine from the defendants for violation of a city ordinance. Defendants were convicted and appealed. The facts show the city passed an ordinance providing in substance that the Mayor should notify the building commissioner of unsafe and dangerous buildings where he learned such buildings were in a condition endangering property and lives of persons in their vicinity. The commissioner was then to investigate such conditions and report to the Mayor. If the report so recommended, the Mayor was to notify the owner or his "agent" to have the building removed or "otherwise properly secured" within three days, and if the owner or "agent" failed to comply with such notice he would be guilty of a misdemeanor, the city would demolish the building and collect the cost through the city attorney. The defendants in the case were "agents" not owners. The City Charter gave the city power to require "owners" to remove dangerous buildings.
The court held that the ordinance relating to "agents" was not justified under the Charter provisions of the city authorizing action against owners only, so defendants were improperly convicted and the ordinance was invalid. The court did say at page 320: "If buildings are unsafe or dangerous, the city has full power to remove them or put them in a safe or secure condition, and make the owners of the property pay for them.
This sufficiently answers the complaint that the respondent would be without remedy." C. Ordinance Powers Required Before Repairs Can Be Compelled.

E. Ordinances Void Because of the Administrative Procedure and Standards Provided.
The case of Lux v. Milwaukee Mechanics Ins. Co., 322 Mo.
In Crossman v. City of Galveston, 112 Tex. 303, 204 S. W. 128, 26 A. L. R. 1210 (1919), an ordinance of the City of Galveston providing for the tearing down of dilapidated buildings was held invalid. The court declared that municipalities had authority under the police power to abate nuisances, but the mere fact that the building is dilapidated does not make it a nuisance. That part of the ordinance which gave to administrative officers of the city power to grant or refuse leave to repair dilapidated buildings was held invalid as allowing arbitrary administrative action, no standard being provided to guide the administrative officer in performing such duties.
In Rowland v. State, 176 So. 545 (Fla. 1937), there was involved a Section of the Building Code of the City of Miami under which one Martin had been arrested for unlawfully continuing to occupy a condemned building after notice by the Building Inspector to vacate said condemned premises.
He sought his release by a writ of habeas corpus.
The ordinance authorized the Building Inspector to examine property and determine whether or not it was safe or unsafe, and thereupon, if he saw proper to do so, to condemn the building and to enforce his orders of condemnation. In its opinion the court points out: "... There is no provision in the ordinance whereby the owner or occupant of the building is given any opportunity to be heard, and there is no provision in the ordinance requiring a determination of the question as to whether or not the building or structure is in such condition to warrant condemnation or in such condition as to be a public nuisance or is a menace to the health or safety to the community by any tribunal, board, or officer except the building inspector. The ordinance attempts to make the building inspector the informant, the judge, the jury, and the executive officer, and provides no means of redress in the event this official should wrongfully evict an owner or tenant either because of bad judgment or bad motives. It purports to give the building inspector the power to declare that a thing is a nuisance, though it is not declared so to be by statute." (Italics supplied. It is true that the ordinance here under consideration provides for notice to be given to the owner or occupant of the building, but, to be valid, it should go further and provide that after notice an opportunity will be given the owner or occupant to be heard and have his rights adjudicated.
"We recognize the fact that the police power is a very broad one and that its exercise is necessary for the welfare of the community, but the right to exercise police power does not carry with it the unqualified right to destroy private property without due process of law. In emergencies such as those which are often caused by conflagrations, floods, windstorms, etc., it may become lawful to destroy private property without notice and without hearing for the protection of the public, and in such instances the right of the individual will yield to the right and for the benefit of the public at large, but in the normal and orderly course of events this necessity does not arise, and the rights of the citizen to the acquisition and enjoyment of property must be respected at least to the extent of giving him the right to be heard before his property is taken without compensation and without a hearing before some tribunal authorized to determine the necessity and propriety thereof." In the case of Rosenberg v. Sheen, 77 N. J. Eq. 476, 77 A.
1019, referred to in the above decision, the court said: "The adjudication of the building department of Atlantic City in condemning the building in question as unsafe is made the basis of the claim of right upon the part of defendant Sheen to destroy the building. That adjudication is manifestly void. It is neither in accordance with the ordinance under which it is made, nor in accordance with well-established and fundamental rules of law. The condemnation ordinance contemplates that an unsafe building shall be repaired or torn down, unless the building inspector shall declare that the building cannot be made safe, in which latter event it may be ordered torn down.
In Rowland v. State, ex rel. Martin, 176 So. 545 (Fla. 1937), there was a prosecution for violation of an ordinance of the City of Miami. The court said: that both notice and a hearing are required to sustain action of local officers in condemning property. This case is quoted in detail in section C, supra, as it is a leading case on the question of the administrative procedure required in condemning unsafe buildings.

In City of Portsmouth v. Snell, 8 N. H. 338 (1836), under a statute authorizing such action in some cases after notice, the "fire wards" of the City of Portsmouth made such repairs to defendant's buildings as made them safe from fire.

The statute authorized demolition of buildings which were dangerous and buildings decayed beyond repair.
The court held that a judgment for the plaintiffs ("fire wards") for the value of repairs made must be reversed as no notice was given defendant, for defendant had no opportunity to make the repairs themselves, and defendants were entitled to such an opportunity before being required to pay the cost of repairs by the "fire wards".
In Ward v. City of Murphysboro, 77 Ill. App. 549 (1898), an ordinance of the City of Murphysboro required that a notice be given to the owner before summary destruction of his building where such building was wrongfully in the fire limits of the city. The court said that if plaintiff's building was removed without notice, the defendant city would be liable to plaintiff.
The case was sent back to let the jury decide the questions of fact as to whether notice was in fact given.
In Swett v. Sprague, 55 Me. 190 (1867), the plaintiff sued for the value of a building in the city of Bath destroyed by defendant under order of the Mayor and Aldermen of the city, because of its dilapidated condition. The Mayor and Aldermen acted under authority of a statute after notice to plaintiff to remove the building and plaintiff's failure to do so.
Held: defendant's acts and the statute valid.
J. Finding of Existence of Nuisance Not Conclusive Without Notice and Hearing From Which Appeal Can Be Taken-City May Be Held Liable If No Nuisance "in fact" Found by Jury to Have Existed.
In Reagan v. City of Texarkana, 238 S. W. 717 (Tex. Civ.
App. 1922), the City Charter authorized the City Council to declare dilapidated buildings to be nuisances and to direct them to be repaired, removed, or abated as the Council shall prescribe.
In an action for the value of a building destroyed under the provisions of this ordinance, the court held that the issue of whether or not a nuisance did in fact exist was a question of fact for the jury, the City Council's finding not being conclusive in an action for the value of such destroyed building.
The court said in part (p. 719): "Any party, whether a private individual, or a municipal corporation, or a public officer, who destroys private property upon the ground that it is a nuisance, proceeds at his peril, unless the thing destroyed has first been judicially condemned as a nuisance." The court continues: "It is also true that notice was given, and an opportunity to be heard was accorded to the owner of the property, and that after hearing evidence tending to show that the property was a nuisance the city council rendered its judgment of condemnation. But the further question arises: Did the city council have the power to exercise such judicial functions?" The court answers this question, No.
In Mcmahon v. City of Telluride, 79 Colo. 281, 244 Pac.
1017, 46 A. L. R. 358 (1926), the city officials acting under provisions of an ordinance, summarily abated by destruction a building which the City Council had found to be a nuisance.
The court held it was error to grant a nonsuit to the city because the issue of whether or not the building was in fact a nuisance was one of fact for the jury.
The court conceded that abatement of a nuisance was a governmental function, but overruled the city's contention that in the instant case the city was not liable because it "was acting in a governmental capacity." The court said in part (p. 1018): "Where the property is not in fact a nuisance, if the city is not liable in tort, because of the rule above mentioned and relied on by the city in the instant case, the municipality is nevertheless liable upon the theory that it must grant compensation for private property it takes for public use. If certain property is in fact a nuisance, its destruction as such may not give rise to any right to compensation; but if property is destroyed under a mistaken belief that it is a nuisance, when in fact it is not a nuisance, it is taken for a 'public use' within the meaning of the constitutional provision, and the loss to the owner should be made good." In Stephens v. City of Ellijay, 171 Ga. 612, 156 S. E. 253 (1931), the plaintiff sued for an injunction to restrain the city from destroying his building. An injunction was denied on the ground that after notice and a hearing the plaintiff's building was ordered demolished and the plaintiff should have appealed from the order of demolition declaring his building to be a nuisance and ordering it to be abated as he had a right to do under statutes of the State of Georgia. This case indicates a method of preventing "after-the-fact" judgments as in the above cases.
Compare the case of Micks v. Mason, 145 Mich. 212, 108 N. W. 707, 11 L. R. A. (n. s.) 653, involving an ordinance of the City of Gladstone, Michigan. The court held there that, where the plaintiff had been acquitted of violating an ordinance in erecting a wooden building within fire limits fixed by city ordinances, such acquittal was not res adjudicata on the question as to whether or not the building was a nuisance, and the court denied to the plaintiff an injunction restraining the city from abating such buildings as a nuisance after his acquittal.
In Town of Bloomfield v. West, 68 Ind. App. 568, 121 N. E. 4 (1918), an ordinance provided that the Town Marshal shall after notice to the owner abate by demolition and removal buildings which have been declared to be a nuisance under the terms of the ordinance. Buildings "unsafe for occupancy, or dangerous or detrimental to life, health, or safety to property" were declared to be a nuisance.
The Board of Trustees declared appellee's barn to be a nuisance in violation of the ordinance and ordered its removal.
Appellee did not remove the barn, appellant city destroyed it and appellee sued for damages.

Held: that the judgment for the plaintiff (appellee) must be affirmed as the facts show no nuisance in fact existed.

K. Ordinances Providing Court Action in Carrying Out Compulsory Vacation and Demolition of Dwellings-Cases Involving.
In People v. Foerst, 10 Cal. App. (2d) 274, 51 P. (2d) 455 (1935), City Attorney John J. O'Toole of the City of San Francisco instituted an action in the name of the people to abate a public nuisance the defendant was maintaining on her premises. The action was taken pursuant to a resolution of the Board of Supervisors of the City and County of San Francisco after the Board had, upon notice thereof, given a hearing to defendant as to whether or not her premises constituted a nuisance. There was a judgment for the People and defendant appealed, and in affirming the judgment, the court said in part: "Appellant does not question the power of a municipality to abate a public nuisance nor challenge the legality of any of the proceedings leading up to the commencement of the present action. She contends, however, that because the findings did not, in express terms, declare the wooden structure to be a public nuisance, that portion of the judgment directing that the same be demolished is not supported by the findings and is against the law. The trial court did in terms find, however, that said structure was old, dilapidated, unsafe, defective, abandoned and extremely dangerous; that the chimney thereon was dangerous, extremely defective and in an unsafe condition; that practically the entire structure was rotten, broken, did not conform to building laws, and was a menace to life and limb of passers-by and to the property adjacent thereto; furthermore, that it was an extreme fire hazard and insanitary. Clearly the above findings warranted the trial court in directing that the structure be demolished.' In the case of Polsgrave v. Moss, 154 Ky. 408, 157 S. W.
113 (1913), there was involved an ordinance of the City of Frankfort, Kentucky, entitled "An ordinance to prohibit the maintaining, use, occupancy or letting of dwellings which are so insanitary or out of repair as to be dangerous or unhealthy, and providing for the abatement thereof". This ordinance, which is quoted in full in the opinion, provides in a comprehensive way for the compulsory repair, vacation and demolition of insanitary, out-of-repair, and unhealthy buildings. Briefly, the ordinance provides that if a building is in such a condition that its maintenance violates the provisions of the ordinance, upon complaint of three or more persons in writing to the Health Officer of the city, the Chief of Police shall notify the occupants to vacate in 10 days and notify the owner to remove and abate the dwelling within 20 days. Any person violating the terms of the ordinance shall be subjected to a fine of not more than $20 and $10 additional for every 24 hours the building remains unremoved.
If the owner disregards the notice to abate, the Mayor is required to cause the owner to be arrested on a warrant issued by the Police Judge of the city. If the owner is convicted, the Mayor, in the name of the city, shall contract with some person to remove the structure at the cost of the owner.
Under this ordinance the plaintiff was ordered to vacate, remove and abate a building belonging to him and filed this bill in equity against the Mayor, the Health Officer and the City Council to enjoin enforcement of the ordinance on the ground that it was void and unconstitutional. The lower court held for the plaintiff and the defendants appealed.
In reversing the judgment of the lower court, the Supreme Court of Kentucky held the ordinance valid and unconstitutional. The court said in part on page 414: "In the exercise of the police power by the city, property which is a menace to public safety or health, may be destroyed without compensation when this is necessary to protect the public, but the public necessity is the limit of the right." In the case of City of Seattle v. Seibert, 129 Wash. 346, 225 P. 67 (1924), the city sued to abate a nuisance under a special ordinance declaring defendant's premises to be a fire hazard, after failure of defendant to abate the nuisance in five days as required by a notice given to defendants to do so. The defendants complained the ordinance was void.
The Supreme Court of Washington held that the lower court erred in holding the ordinance void and ordered a new trial to determine whether or not the facts warranted a determination that a fire hazard exists on the defendants' premises which should be abated.
In the case of In re Jenkins, 115 N. Y. S. 385 (1909), there was involved a provision of the Building Code of the City of New York providing that on refusal of an owner of an unsafe building to make the same safe or remove it, a report of the building shall be made to a court, which court, if it finds the building to be unsafe, shall command the Commissioner of Buildings to tear it down or make it safe. The Building Code also provided that the expenses thereof shall constitute a lien against the premises. The court held that the city in the instant case could not recover for the cost of removing debris of a collapsed building and bodies of persons buried thereunder, as recovery of costs in such a case was not provided for by the Code.
In the case of Village of Louisville v. Webster, 108 Ill. 414, 5 Am. Eng. Corp. Case 367 (1884), an ordinance of the Village of Louisville, Illinois, provided that if a building was damaged by fire, decay, or otherwise to an extent of 50 per cent of its value, a complaint should be made to a Justice of the Peace and he should summons the owner to appear before him. The issue of whether or not the owner's building was in this condition was then to be tried by judge and jury, and if the issue should be found against the owner, the Justice of the Peace was required to issue a writ to the village constable to remove any person in possession of the premises and advertise for sale the buildings thereon and the purchaser should then remove the building. The costs were to be paid out of the moneys received for the building and the balance, after such costs were deducted, was to be paid to the owner.
If no purchaser bid for the building, the constable was to remove it himself.
The court held the ordinance was invalid because it did not give the owner of the property a chance to remove it himself after an adjudication that the property was defective and a nuisance.
L. No Judicial Proceedings Necessary.
In the case of Mckibbin v. City of Fort Smith, 35 Ark.
352 (1880), the plaintiff sued to enjoin the city from removing certain buildings erected in violation of the city ordinances. The ordinance provided for abatement at the owner's expense. In affirming a judgment for the city, the court said at page 359: "We are, therefore, of the opinion that no prosecution or judicial proceedings of any kind against the owner of the building was necessary or proper before the council could order its removal." The court points out that if a building is wrongfully removed, the owner could recover damages.
In Lemmon v. Guthrie Center, 113 Iowa 36, 84 N. W.
986, an ordinance authorized certain fire limits and provided that buildings erected in violation of the ordinance should be removed by the owner upon two days' notice from the Mayor or Marshal, and if the owner did not heed such notice, the city would abate it at the owner's expense.
The court restrained the city from abating plaintiff's buildings before he could veneer it to comply with the terms of the ordinance. The court stated: "A building erected in violation of an ordinance fixing fire limits may be torn down or removed without any judicial proceedings whatever . . . Delays of that character might endanger the public safety, and ought not to be tolerated." Compare Section "M", infra, "Cases Holding Ordinances Valid Which Provide for Summary Abatement." M. Cases Holding Ordinances Valid Which Provide for Summary Abatement.
In Security Ins. Co. v. Rosenberg, 227 Ky. 314, 12 S. W.
(2d) 688 (1928), where an Insurance Company resisted payment for total destruction of the house on the ground that the house had been destroyed on order of the Building Inspector of the City of Covington, Kentucky, acting under an ordinance which did not provide for a hearing before demolition, and the further claim that the ordinance was invalid because it authorized summary action, the court said: "The ordinances of the City of Covington providing for a condemnation of fire menaces and dangerous buildings and their abatement, without a prior opportunity being afforded the property owner to be heard on the question, are not unconstitutional, since, if the order of condemnation is improvidently entered, the owner has his remedy by injunction to enjoin its enforcement, or, if the alleged fire menace or dangerous building has been abated, the owner may, by an action against those responsible for the abatement, recover such damages as he may have sustained by reason of the unwarranted abatement. In the case of North American Cold Storage Co. v. City of Chicago, 211 U. S.
306, 29 S. Ct. 101, 53 L. Ed. 195, 15 Am. Cas. 276, it was held that due process of law is not denied the owner or custodian of food in cold storage by a municipal ordinance under which such food, when unfit for human consumption, may be summarily seized, condemned, and destroyed by municipal officers without a preliminary hearing being afforded the owner of custodian . . .' In the case of Hine v. City of New Haven, 40 Conn. 478 (1873), an injunction was denied to the owner of a building which building was being maintained in violation of an ordinance within the fire limits fixed by the ordinance. The court in approving summary process for the removal of such a building said: "The ordinance in question is not only reasonable, but essential to the protection of the city. A plain and unquestioned violation of its provisions, as in the case before us, demand immediate action. To await the action of the courts through the ordinary process of law might prove disastrous." In the case of Porter v. City of Lewiston, 41 Idaho 324, 238 Pac. 1014 (1925), a suit was brought to enjoin the city from demolishing two wooden buildings. The city council, acting under the provisions of the ordinance, had declared the buildings public nuisances and had given the owner 15 days to remove it or show cause why it should not be removed. A hearing had been held by the city council also.
The court, in denying an injunction to the plaintiff, stated that the ordinance in question was constitutional and that, where such nuisances actually existed, an ordinance need not, to be constitutional, provide for notice or a hearing before summary abatement of the nuisance.
The court followed the rule laid down by North American Cold Storage Co. v. City of Chicago, 211 U. S. 306, 29 Sup.
Ct. 101, 53 L. Ed. 195, in approving summary abatement, pointing out that the plaintiff could protect himself, as in the instant case, by injunction if summary abatement was unwarranted.
In Baumgartner v. Hasty, 100 Ind. 575, 50 Am. Rep. 830, 8 Am. Eng. Corp. Cas. 353, there was involved an ordinance of the City of Huntington providing that the Mayor should, on report of the City Marshal, order removal of wooden buildings existing in violation of the city ordinances.
The Marshal notified plaintiff to remove a wooden building.
The plaintiff refused to do so. Under order of the Mayor the Marshal himself removed the building.
In holding that the Marshal was not liable when sued by the plaintiff, the court said: . . a wooden building is not in itself a nuisance, but when erected in a place prohibited by law, and where it endangers the safety of adjoining property, it may become a nuisance.
"But it is well settled, without dissent, that without a special grant of authority public corporations may, as a common law power, cause the abatement of nuisances, and if the nuisance can not otherwise be abated, may destroy the thing which constitutes it. The authorities do, indeed, go much further, for they declare that it is the duty of the corporation to abate public nuisances. It is one of the oldest of the common law rules, that an individual citizen may, without notice, abate a nuisance, and, if it is necessary to effectually abate it, destroy the thing which creates it." (Italics supplied.) In King v. Davenport, 98 Ill. 305, 38 Am. Rep. 89, an ordinance of the city of Jacksonville, Illinois, prohibited erection or repair of any building of combustible materials within the fire limits fixed by ordinance and provided for summary abatement of such buildings. The ordinance provided that an owner of such a building who failed to remove it after reasonable notice should be subject to a fine of $100 for each week the building remained unabated, and if the city itself removed the building, the expense of removal might be recovered from the offender.
In violation of the ordinance the plaintiff placed a shingle roof on her building. The Marshal notified her to remove it.
She failed to do so, and the Marshal removed it himself. In reversing the judgment for the plaintiff the court said at P. 309: "The sole question here presented is upon the validity of the ordinance." The court concludes at page 317: "We do not, then, find the provision of the City Charter authorizing the City Council to declare the structure in question a nuisance, and to abate it summarily, to be in conflict with the constitution, and therefore hold the ordinance to be valid, and that it was a justification of the acts of the defendants in the removal of the roof." See also Eureka City v. Wilson, 15 Utah 67, 48 Pac. 150, where no notice is specifically required in the ordinance before summary abatement.

APPENDIX Decisions of the Courts Construing Specific Housing Legislation In order that this report might be as complete as possible, we have collected in this appendix the decisions of the courts interpreting specific municipal, state and Federal legislation providing for Housing schemes. The legislation and decisions herein deal with the problem of whether housing is or is not a "public purpose" or "public use" to justify the expenditure of public funds or the taking of private property under the power of eminent domain, as contrasted with the police power hereinbefore examined.
A. Decisions Under Municipal and State Housing Legislation-Before Federal-State Municipal Cooperative Housing Legislation.
The problem of slum clearance and housing was considered by municipal and state authorities long before any real effort was made by the Federal Government to deal with this problem. in the interest of the public health, safety and morals certain blocks of unsafe and unsanitary dwellings should be replaced by new housing facilities con- ...
structed in accord with proper standards of sanitation and safety and provided with appropriate parks and playgrounds." The ordinance recited the willingness of the Prudential Insurance Company to acquire lands upon which objectionable dwellings stand and to construct approved housing facilities, and to convey certain parts of the land to the city for park purposes for the sum of $1,200,000.
The ordinance was challenged as the use of public funds for a private purpose, but the court held that the ordinance is valid, that it is not an expenditure of public funds for a private purpose, as the public will in fact benefit from this housing scheme, so the funds are expended for a "public purpose only." In Opinion of Justices, 211 Mass. 624, 98 N. E. 611, 42 L. R. A. (n. s. C. A. 402 (d), 403 (a) (1933)). It is argued that Congress, being the sole legsilative authority for the District of Columbia, is possessed of the police power for the District of Columbia which elsewhere rests in the states. It is recognized that Congress has such power, but it is not seen that Congress undertook to exercise the power in the enactment of the National Industrial Recovery Act. . . . The police power is a power to regulate and control and is not a power to take private property. It is a power quite apart from the right of eminent domain.
Block v. Hirsh, 256 U. S. 135. The right of a government to take private property for a public use rests upon a right inherent in government. The power to regulate and control the use of private property arises from the police power.

D. The United States Housing Act of (U.S.H.A.) The United States Housing Act of 1937, commonly known as the Wagner-Steagall Housing Act, creates the United States Housing Authority as a permanent corporation in the Department of the Interior. The purpose of this Authority is to promote the general welfare by relieving present and recurring unemployment and to extend financial assistance to public housing agencies for the housing of low-income families who are living under unsafe, insanitary or slum conditions. To initiate this program $500,000,000 will be made available for loans by the issuance of federally-guaranteed bonds of the Authority. In addition, the sum of $26,000,000 is authorized to be appropriated for the purpose of providing funds to meet Federal annual contributions and capital grants and to pay administrative expenses of the Authority.
The Act contemplates a non-Federal program consisting entirely of projects locally constructed, locally owned and locally operated. Unlike the Housing Division of the Public Works Administration which undertook a program of Federal construction, the United States Housing Authority has no power to construct projects. It is limited in its functions to furnishing financial and technical assistance to local public housing agencies.
As this report is being written, 33 states have passed legislation providing for co-operation with the Federal Government in the housing program. Already 128 local housing authorities have been created under such state legislation. Of the $500,- 000,000 fund only about $116,000,000 has not yet been earmarked for specific housing projects, and about 5 contracts, as distinguished from earmarkings, with local housing authorities have just been signed.

 An ordinance providing for the Vacation, Removal, Repair, or Demolition of any Building or Structure which is or threatens to be a Public Nuisance, Dangerous to the Health, Morals, Safety, or General Welfare of the People of the City of...
or which might tend to constitute a fire menace; and for the Assessment of the Cost of Vacation, Removal, Repair, or Demolition thereof as a Municipal Lien or assessment against such Premises, and/or to provide for the recovery of such costs in an action at law.
WHEREAS, in the City of...
there are or may in the future be buildings or structures which are dilapidated, unsafe, dangerous, insanitary, a menace to the health, morals, safety, and general welfare of the people of this city, and which might tend to constitute a fire menace, and which are a public nuisance: BE IT THEREFORE ORDAINED BY THE CITY COUNCIL OF THE CITY OF..
as follows: 1. Dangerous Buildings Defined. All buildings or structures which have any or all of the following defects shall be deemed "dangerous buildings": (a) Those which list, lean or buckle to such an extent that when a plumb line drawn together through the center of gravity falls outside of the middle third of the base of the structural part so listing.
(b) Those which, exclusive of the foundation, show thirty-three (33) per cent or more, of damage or deterioration of the supporting member or members, or fifty (50) per cent of damage or deterioration of the nonsupporting enclosing walls or covering.
(c) Those which have improperly distributed loads upon the floors or roofs or in which the same are overloaded, or which have insufficient strength for the purpose used.
(d) Those which have been damaged by fire, wind or other causes so as to have become dangerous to life, safety, morals, or the general health and welfare of the occupants or the people of the City 
(e) Those which have become or are so dilapidated, decayed, unsafe, insanitary or which so utterly fail to provide the amenities essential to decent living that they are unfit for human habitation, or are likely to cause sickness or disease, so as to work injury to the health, morals, safety or general welfare of those living therein.
(f) Those having light, air, and sanitation facilities.
which are inadequate to protect the health, morals, safety, or general welfare of human beings who are or may live therein.
(g) Those having inadequate facilities for egress in case of fire or panic or those having insufficient stairways, elevators, fire escapes, or other means of communication, light, or ventilation.
(h) Those which have parts thereof which are so attached that they may fall and injure members of the public or property.
(i) Those which because of their condition are unsafe, insanitary or dangerous to the health, morals, safety or general welfare of the people of this city.
(j) Those buildings existing in violation of any provision of the Building Code of this City, or any provision of the fire prevention code, or other ordinances of this City.
2. Standards for Repair, Vacation or Demolition. The following standards shall be followed in substance by the Building Inspector and the Building Commissioner in ordering repair, vacation, or demolition: (a) If the "dangerous building" can reasonably be repaired so that it will no longer exist in violation of the terms of this ordinance it shall be ordered repaired.
(b) If the "dangerous building" is in such condition as to make it dangerous to the health, morals, safety, or general welfare of its occupants it shall be ordered to be vacated.
(c) In any case where a "dangerous building" is 50 per cent damaged or decayed, or deteriorated from its original value or structure, it shall be demolished, and in all cases where a building cannot be repaired so that it will no longer exist in violation of the terms of this ordinance it shall be demolished. In all cases where a "dangerous building" is a fire hazard existing or erected in violation of the terms of this ordinance or any ordinance of this city or statute of the state of..
it shall be demolished.
3. Dangerous Buildings-Nuisances. All "dangerous buildings" within the terms of Section 1 of this ordinance are hereby declared to be public nuisances, and shall be repaired, vacated, or demolished as hereinbefore and hereinafter provided.
4. Duties of Building Inspector. The Building Inspector shall: (a) Inspect or cause to be inspected semi-annually, all public buildings, schools, halls, churches, theaters, hotels, tenements, commercial, manufacturing, or loft buildings for the purpose of determining whether any conditions exist which render such places a "dangerous building" within the terms of Section 1, supra; (b) Inspect any building, wall or structure about which complaints are filed by any person to the effect that a building, wall or structure is or may be existing in violation of this code; (c) Inspect any building, wall or structure reported (as hereinafter provided for) by the Fire or Police Departments of this City as probably existing in violation of the terms of this ordinance.
(d) Inspect annually buildings in the following sections of this city, to determine whether they are "dangerous buildings" within the terms of section 1, supra.
(e) Notify in writing the owner, lessee, agent, or occupant of any building found by him to be a "dangerous building" within the terms of section 1, to change, alter, repair, vacate, or demolish such building within such time as may be reasonably judged by the work necessary to be done, but not over 30 days time shall be given.
(f) Set forth in the notice provided for in subsection (e) hereof, there shall be a description of the building, wall, or structure deemed unsafe, a statement of the particulars which make the building wall or structure a "dangerous building," and an order requiring the same to be put in such condition as to comply with the terms of this ordinance within such length of time, not exceeding 30 days, as is reasonable.
(g) Report to the Building Commissioner any noncompliance with the "notice" provided for in subsections (e) and (f) hereof.
(h) Appear at all hearings conducted by the Building Commissioner and testify as to the condition of "dangerous buildings." (i) Place a notice on all "dangerous buildings" reading as follows: "This building has been found to be a dangerous building by the Inspector of Buildings. This notice is to remain on this building until it is repaired, vacated, or demolished in accordance with the notice which has been given the owner, occupant, mortgagee or lessee of this building. It is unlawful to remove this notice until such notice is complied with." (j) Report to the City Attorney the names of all persons failing to comply with the notice provided for in subsections (e) and (f) hereof, or who remove the notice provided for in subsection (i) hereof.
5. Duties of Building Commissioner. It shall be the duty of the Building Commissioner to: (a) Upon receipt of a report of the Building Inspector as provided for in Section 4, subsection (g) hereof, to give written notice to the owner, occupant, mortgagee, or lessee of, to appear before him at a day certain within 10 days of the date of the notice to show cause why the building or structure reported to be a "dangerous building" should not be repaired, vacated, or demolished because of the conditions set forth in the Building Inspector's notice provided for herein in Section 4, subsections (e) and (f).
(b) Hold a hearing and hear such testimony as the owner, occupant, mortgagee, or lessee offer relative to the "dangerous building." (c) Make a finding of fact as to whether or not the building in question is a "dangerous building" within the terms of Section 1 hereof.
(d) If the building is a "dangerous building" within the terms of Section 1 hereof, he shall issue an order commanding the owner, occupant, mortgagee, or lessee to repair, vacate, or demolish said "dangerous building," said order to conform in substance to the provisions of this ordinance.
(e) If the owner, occupant, mortgagee, or lessee fails to comply with the order provided for in subsection (d) hereof, within 10 days, the Building Commissioner shall cause such building or structure to be repaired, vacated, or demolished as the facts may warrant, under the standards hereinbefore provided for in section 2 of this ordinance, and shall with the assistance of the City Attorney cause the costs of such repair, vacation, or demolition to be charged against the land on which the building existed as a municipal lien or cause such costs to be added to the tax duplicate as an assessment, or to be levied as a special tax against the land upon which the building stands or did stand, or to be recovered in a suit at law against the owner; except in cases where such procedure is desirable and any delay thereby caused will not be dangerous to the health, morals, safety, or general welfare of the people of this city, the Building Commissioner shall notify the City Attorney to take legal action to force the owner to make all necessary repairs or demolish the building.
(f) Report to the City Attorney the names of all persons not complying with the order provided for in Section 5, subsection (d) hereof. Duties of Police Department. All employees of the Police Department shall make a report in writing to the Building Inspector of any buildings or structures which are, may be, or are suspected to be "dangerous buildings" within the terms of this ordinance. Such reports must be delivered to the Building Inspector within 24 hours of the discovery of such buildings by any employee of the Police Department.
Note: Provisions for court review of the notice and order of the Building Commissioner are necessary in order to prevent a successful civil suit against the Building Commissioner based on the ground that he caused a building not condemnable under the police power as a nuisance to be demolished, etc.
STATE ENABLING ACT The Legislature of the State of Mississippi on March 30, 1938, passed House Bill No. 693, Laws of 1938-"An act authorizing cities, towns and villages to adopt ordinances relating to the remedying and elimination of dwellings unfit for human habitation; providing for the remedies and procedure in connection with action taken under such ordinances, and for other related purposes," which act provides in part: "Any person affected by an order issued by the public officer may apply to the Circuit Court for an injunction restraining the public officer from carrying out the provisions of the order, and the Court, or any judge thereof, may, upon such application, issue an order restraining the public officer pending final disposition of the cause. Hearings shall be had by the court on such applications within twenty (20) days, or as soon thereafter as possible, and shall be given preference over other matters on the Court's calendar. The Court shall hear and determine the issues raised, and shall enter such final order or degree as law and justice may require. In all such proceedings, the findings of the public officer as to facts, if supported by evidence, shall be conclusive. Costs shall be in the discretion of the Court. The remedies herein provided shall be exclusive remedies, and no person affected by an order of the public officer shall be entitled to recover any damages for action taken by the public officer under such order or because of noncompliance therewith.
