COMMUNITY PROMOTION Once of the most important activities of a local retail association is the promotion of the community as a trading center. This is perhaps even more true of the retail interests of a town than of the other business interests which generally find representation in a Chamber of Commerce.

Certain activities aimed at increasing trade fall naturally, however, into the hands of a retail group. If independent, the retail association ordinarily will work closely with the Chamber on civic betterment projects. But whether independent or not, the retail association takes on certain tasks in an attempt to stimulate retail trade a project in which the Chamber's general membership is interested somewhat less directly than are the retailers themselves.
- The most common form of merchant cooperation in community promotion is the holding of annual events -- special community-wide sales. The ways in which funds for these events are raised has been described in the section on assessments.
These community events bear numerous and varied labels.
Most frequest are Dollar Days -- on which days all the stores run specials priced at a dollar. Some towns have Dollar Days once a year, others twice, others three times.
Woonsocket, R. I, for instance, has Dollar Days in January and July; while Easton, Pa. has them in February and August. Cedar Rapids, Iowa, has two Dollar Days and two Bargain Days.
Spring and Fall Openings are frequently the subject of community-wide promotions.
Many towns have special two or three day events labelled with the town's name, e.g. Coatesville (Pa.) Sales Days, Laconia (N.H.) Bargain Carnival or Fall Festival, Hartford (Conn.) Day.

ADVERTISING AND SOLICITATIONS In Question 9 dealt with agreements concerning ADVERTISING. This question, our inquiry showed, was linked in very large degree with agreements concerning SOLICITATIONS, generally. Some questions involving the nature of advertising will be referred to in the question on "Better Business Bureau" activities.
the majority of instances, however, where associations reported in response to our heading "Advertising", they are connected closely with our heading "Solicitations". These two subjects (the question on solicitations, even more than that on advertising) brought forth the best response of any question asked on the questionnaire.
Out of the total of some one hundred and forty (140) responses received, one hundred and eighteen (118) reported some agreement in regard to solicitations as being in effect, and eighty-three (83) stated that they had some sort of an agree ment with regard to advertising.

-20The nature of the solicitations agreements vary considerably. In a great many instances, the work of deciding which are proper agencies to receive contributions in the form of donations in cash, or the purchase of tickets, or the lending or giving of merchandise, or the taking of advertising space, etc., is entrusted to a secret committee of the merchants' association and all members are instructed not to make any such donations, unless the applicant can present a card stating that his cause has been found satisfactory by this committee.
Several associations report that violation of this agreement on solicitations is punishable by a money penalty. The rigidity of the rules varios considerably in many cases. Some associations absolutely prohibit anything even remotely touching the subject of donations. Others permit the purchase of blocks of tickets in the firm's name, but prohibit advertising in programs, etc. The agreements or resumes of them are usually prominently posted in the offices of the member stores.
The agreement not to subscribe or donate without the committee's approval, lists certain definite exceptions, e.g. publications issued with certain regularity, and such donations as Community Chest, Red Cross, etc.
Before citing any of the replies received to this question, we should mention that the famous "Truth in Advertising" and "Honesty in Merchandising" agreements and codes (on the Syracuse and Wilkes Barre patterns), are being adopted by a number of local associations which so indicated in their responses to our question on advertising.

--- -31ITINERANT VENDORS The itinerant vendor has brought forth great activity by retail associations throughout the country. The responses on this question indicate a tremendous interest in the problem and there has been a great deal of work done by local merchants to solve their difficulties in this respect.
Seventy-seven (77) Retail Secretaries stated that their towns have made some regulatory effort in regard to the itinerant vendor. Perhaps the most famous of the itinerant vendor ordinances is the Green River Ordinance. A number of towns have indicated that they have patterned their ordinance on this.
works on the principle that an uninvited canvasser or salesman is a nuisance and makes their activities a misdemeanor. The other most common device is the establishing of a heavy license fee on itinerant vendors. The great difficulty mentioned by a number of cities is in dealing with the itinerant vendor who takes orders for goods to be delivered from another state. Not only do the merchants' associations act to secure the enactment of these ordinances but they also in many cases are the active policing agency to see that the ordinances are complied with. Great care must be taken in the enactment of itinerant vendor ordinances to avoid the danger of unconstitutionality under the State or Federal constitutions.
Dayton, Ohio Itinerant vendors selling house to house or on the streets pay a $1.00 daily license fee. However, there is no fee when they are operating from a store room or hotel room.
Schenectady, N. Y.
The Bureau secured an itinerant pedler's ordinance (license) and merely calls City Hall to check on any itinerant vendor.
The public knows that it can phone the Chamber of Commerco and have solicitors and itinerant vendors checked on. This helps the merchants and police department keep track of such operators.
Sioux City, Iowa The Merchants' Association discourages renting space to itinerant vendors by apartment houses and hotels, and has been instrumental in securing the passage of an ordinance requiring the itinerant vendor to post a bond and pay a small license fee.
In Pennsylvania, another ordinance of a type which is becoming increasingly common was passed in 1937. This is the Pennsylvania Fire Sales, Going-Out-ofbusiness and Bankruptcy Sales Act, which regulated this type of activity, which had become a major racket.

 (p.67) MANUFACTURERS SELLING AT RETAIL A JOINT STATEMENT ISSUED BY THE WHOLESALE AND RETAIL COMMITTEES OF THE PHILADELPHIA CHAMBER OF COMMER CE AS AN OPEN LETTER TO EMPLOYERS Certain large Philadelphia plants have encouraged their employees to take full advantage of "special prices" offered them by manufacturers and distributors who have overlooked the necessity of maintaining a dependable and constant retail outlet.
Manufacturers and business firms have permitted the formation of "Buying Clubs" and have fostered the distribution of "special price" catalogues throughout their plants.
Still others have permitted their employees to use "purchase cards" with the name of the manufacturer prominently printed thereon.
These and other practices have been so widely developed in Philadelphia that it is now almost a rarity when an employee of the majority of our large concerns buys strictly at retail prices, particularly when an appreciable sum of money is involved.
What is the "buying club" or the "special price privilege" but a form of consumer cooperatives. Is it not the privilege gained through mass purchase at a special discount because quite naturally the taxes and overhead, borne by the legitimate retail and wholesale outlet, are not borne in the manufacturing plant.
It seems that the encouragement of "industrial selling" is a direct encouragement to the development of the consumer cooperative with special privileges and special discounts.
It is but a single step from the "buying club", fostered by the manufacturer and business man himself to the formation of consumer cooperatives, particularly if such clubs are allowed to develop when they can be controlled by the manufacturer without any appreciable detriment to his employee policies. Those special privileges to employees encourage the formation of mass movement against private business and the profit motive. They are also resulting in efforts to pass legislation to endeavor to send all retail sales through retail channels, and all manufacturing through manufacturing channels.
It is possible that practices such as industrial buying by large concerns for the benefit of their employees, or manufacturers or wholesalers selling at retail to the detriment of their retail customers, may come within the scope of (cont'd.) -68- (Manufacturers Selling At Retail, Philadelphia, cont'd.) Senate Bill 1077, signed by the President March 21st, which enlarges the powers of the Federal Trade Commission to cover the regulations of industry vertically, as well as horizontally, to expedite proceedings before the Commission and to cut the costs of the enforcement of the Act.
Thus the business man permitting industrial selling or "wholesale buying" is not only encouraging the cooperative, he is also inviting restrictive legislation which he, through his efforts, has the power to avoid. Courses of instruction for Jacksonville retail merchants and their employees have been made available through the help and cooperation of J. H. Gabrielle, Director of Vocational Training, and W. R. Scheel, Co-ordinator of Evening Extension Classes, Department of Public Instruction. This high school program has been -78- (Training, Detroit, cont'a) extended so that, during the present semester, we have 24 such retail classes in thirteen high schools with an enrollment of 840. It is hoped that some of these classes soon will be conducted on a part-time work, part-time school basis.
Not only are these classes of value in the better training of potential retail employees but they also provide future consumers with a better appreciation of merchandise and the business of retailing. The rapidly spreading interest and enrollment in these high school courses and the urgent need of qualified and experienced teachers is one of the reasons for the sponsoring of the teacher-training courses previously mentioned.
Co-operative Courses for Selected High School Graduates In an effort to provide interested stores with especially trained high school graduates, a start was made in 1932, and continued, whereby a representative of the Board of Education and the personnel directors connected with the cooperating stores, carefully select certain graduates among those evidencing an interest in retailing. The young people who are accepted are given instruction in store operation, salesmanship, and merchandise during the mornings.
During the afternoons and on Saturdays they are employed in the stores at regular hourly wages. Fourteen department and specialty store members of our Association have been co-operating in this development and already have provided 388 selected individuals with a spolendid start in the retail field. In most instances these students have been given very acceptable full time employment after completing the course.
Evening Classes for Store Employees When Wayne University agreed in 1931 to start an evening class for store enployees it was as an experiment and executives from various stores led discussions on different evenings. When we secured 30 enrollments for that first class it appeared as though the effort was justified. With fine co-operation from the University and the support of management, additional store employees became interested so that courses on Retail Merchandising and Salesmanship have continued each semester since 1931 with an average enrollment of 49.
After several years of agitation by various retail groups such as ours, late in 1937 federal and state funds became available for training in the distributive occupations. Fine co-operation was rendered our program by the Michigan State Board of Control for Vocational Education and with the continued help and stimulated interest of our Board of Education, a School of Business Administration was established in Wayne University. This new department set about to help us justify the allotment of special funds for retail training, by appointing a co-ordinator to assist with the promotional work, to organize new classes, develop instructional material and co-ordinate the instruction and work experiences of the persons enrolled.
Most of the instructors actually are engaged in the field in which they are (cont'd.) : -79- (Training, Detroit, cont'd.) teaching and truly are masters of their occupations. The degree of interest manifested in this new training program appears practically to guarantee its continued development next fall by both the State Vocational Department and the Detroit Board of Education. Store management must prepare to ive every possible encouragement to all of these activities.
 Similar courses have likewise been offered in the Evening School Division of the Detroit Public Schools.
Guidance and Placement Since 1921 the Detroit Public Schools have operated a placement service which has enjoyed the confidence and the continued patronage of many of Detroit's employers. In 1930 the placement service was brought into a closer relationship than formerly existed with the counseling service in the high schools to the end that the two activities which are considered of prime importance to young people as individuals, namely, guidance and placement, have become very closely affiliated in fact as well as in name.
As a result of this closer relationship the Division of Guidance and Placement at the Board of Education each semester receives detailed reports from the high school counselors giving essential facts about the training which the young people have received who are about to be graduated. Such items are included in this report as general scholarship, records in certain subjects contributing directly to employability, extra-curricular activities, and ratings in various personality traits. Thus the Division is able to furnish to prospective employers not only the specific facts obtained by the placement counselors in the central offices from our registrants but also quite definite credentials from the high schools where these young people have spent the last three or four years.
We feel that with the recent addition of the retailing training courses in our high schools, a most desirable enlargement is provided to the office training and technical training already available and thus the school system now is better qualified to furnish to the employers of the community an adequate supply of employees in the junior field.
Testing Service for Placement Another feature of the educational service now available for employers which many consider of unique value is the testing service for placement purposes made possible by the co-operation of the Psychological Clinic of the Detroit Public Schools. Tests now being used include those of general mental ability, mechanical aptitude, typewriting ability, and manual dexterity. Experimental work is now under way locking toward a program of testing for aptitude for retail store occupations.
