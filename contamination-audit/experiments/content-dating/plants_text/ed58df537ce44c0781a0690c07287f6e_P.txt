NEW FACTS ABOUT AN OLD FRIEND Most students today know why they are in school. They expect to learn facts and habits there that they can use for the rest of their lives.
The dictionary habit is among the most valuable ones you will learn in school. You have been using the dictionary yourself for several years now. You find it easy to use in checking spellings, pronunciations, and meanings.
Probably, however, you have not yet learned a number of facts that can make you a still more capable master of dictionary service. The dictionary excerpt on page 55 has been borrowed for you from A Dictionary for Boys and Girls, to help you master some new facts about dictionary service.
1. What to Call Things in the Dictionary Each dictionary help has its own name. Do you know and use the ones below?
ENTRY. Each word that is explained in the dictionary is called an entry. It is entered in the proper alphabetical order, among the other entries. The word com·partment is the first entry in the dictionary excerpt. The term entry is used to mean either the word itself or the word with all the material that is given to explain it.
RESPELLING. The second spelling of an entry, in which diacritical marks are used, is the respelling. It is placed in parentheses after the entry and is useful in pronouncing the entry correctly. The respelling for the entry com part'ment is kom pärt'měnt.
KEY WORDS. You may check any marked letter in the respelling by finding the same marked letter in one of the words across the foot of the dictionary page. (See page 52.) These are key words, and each key word contains only one marked letter. The three key words necessary to help with the pronunciation of com·part'ment (kom.pat'i.ble (kom-păt'i-b'l), adj. Harmonious; agreeable; suitable.

DEFINITIONS. The meanings following each entry are the definitions that help to explain it. Some entries, such as com'pass in the excerpt above, have numerous definitions. Some entries, such as com pa'tri-ot, have only a single definition.

ILLUSTRATIONS. The pictures found on many dictionary pages help to illustrate one or several of the forms that an entry may have. These illustrations are especially helpful when there is a great variety among definitions, or when the entry is an uncommon or little known one.
2. Where to Find Things in the Dictionary A dictionary can give you much more help than simply the spelling, pronunciation, and meanings of words. Have you ever needed to use the helps below?
ABBREVIATIONS. A dictionary always explains for you the abbreviations which are used in it. In some dictionaries this explanation is given at the foot of alternate pages; in others a list of abbreviations is given in the front of the book.
LANGUAGE RULES. A discussion of the rules of spelling, pronunciation, grammar, and the like is given at the front of most dictionaries.
SYMBOLS USED IN RESPELLING. A complete list of the diacritical marks and the letters that help with pronunciation is given in every dictionary, usually in the front.
FACTS ABOUT THE WORLD. Every dictionary contains a section devoted to information about our own country and other countries. You may find here such facts as the names of the states, the names of foreign countries, the flags of the world, the population of the world's large cities, and so on. This section is usually at the back of a dictionary. A large dictionary, of course, contains more kinds of interesting information than a smaller one can.

 MAKING OUR WORK EASIER While we have been learning to spell, we have been learning also a few rules that can make our work easier. It will be wise to remember them and to keep them in a special place where we can easily refer to them. Here they are.
1. PLURALS a. Plurals of most nouns are made by adding s to the singular forms.
boy, boys bill, bills night, nights b. To make pronunciation easier, the plurals of some nouns are made by adding es instead of s.
dress, dresses dish, dishes c. A noun ending in y after a consonant forms its plural by changing the y to i and adding es.
baby, babies city, cities lady, ladies d. A few nouns make their plurals by changing their form, usually by changing the vowel.
foot, feet mouse, mice 2. CONTRACTIONS man, men calf, calves An apostrophe is used to show the omission of a letter or letters in a contraction.
can not, can't you will, you'll you are, you're 3. ABBREVIATIONS An abbreviation is always followed by a period.
Mister, Mr.
Company, Co.
pound, lb.
4. POSSESSIVES Friday, Fri.
yard, yd.
gallon, gal.
a. The possessive of a singular noun is formed by adding the apostrophe and s.
John, John's mother, mother's dentist, dentist's b. The possessive of a plural noun ending in s is formed by adding only the apostrophe.
boys, boys' girls, girls' MAKING OUR WORK EASIER 5. SILENT E a. A word ending in silent e usually drops the e before a suffix beginning with a vowel.
freeze, freezes cure, curable lose, losing chose, chosen b. A word ending in silent e usually keeps the e before a suffix beginning with a consonant.
sure, surely advance, advancement nine, ninety care, careful, careless 6. WORDS ENDING IN Y a. A word ending in y after a vowel usually keeps the y before a suffix.
day, days obey, obeyed buy, buying b. A word ending in y after a consonant usually changes the y to i before a suffix, except a suffix beginning with i.
try, tried (trying) fly, flier (flying) marry, marries (marrying) 7. DOUBLING THE FINAL CONSONANT a. A one-syllable word ending in one consonant (except x) after one vowel usually doubles the consonant before a suffix beginning with a vowel.
big, bigger, biggest run, runner, running b. A word of more than one syllable ending in one consonant after one vowel usually doubles the consonant before a suffix beginning with a vowel, if the accent is on the last syllable.
