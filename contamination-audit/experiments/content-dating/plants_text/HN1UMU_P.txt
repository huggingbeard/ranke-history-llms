Just how it was all accomplished no one not present at the ceremony's beginning ever knew. But it had evidently been decided to initiate the dog, and initiated he was. The campus was humming drowsily after dusk when there came the prolonged, railroad-whistle blast of the red racer's horn, a furious howling and rattling of tin cans around the interior of the barn, and the campus, moved by a common impulse, started en masse for the scene of the uproar. But before the foremost had proceeded half the distance, there came a flash of flame, a sudden roar, followed a moment later by a blinding explosion that jarred the earth and seemed to light the world for a moment, and with the explosion the old barn, the dog, and the new red racer disappeared forever. The windows rattled in the dormitories, and an occasional pane, jarred beyond endurance, tinkled into tiny pieces.

It had not been a large explosion, even then, for, as Professor Stone later explained, there had been only about a gallon of gas in a can in the building, the car's

tank being empty. It had been sufficient, of course, to produce all the effect that could be desired, and the damage, aside from the barn, car and dog, had been a few hundred dollars. That no serious fire resulted was due to the fact that there were no other buildings near the old barn.

It was all over in a few minutes; after which came the inevitable investigation. It seemed clear from the first that it was a student's prank, as all of the first sounds so betokened. That the explosion had been expected by those responsible was doubtful. Somehow it had occurred in the excitement. The further fact that no one was hurt seemed to indicate that it had been planned and executed probably hours before the actual occurrence. The dog could have been chloroformed, and Professor Stone, who had gone into the old barn a couple of hours before the explosion, recalled that the dog had seemed to be asleep in a corner. 

He also recalled leaving part of a box of matches lying on a can that stood near some waste. 

The details were clear enough; besides which, the outrage had actually occurred. What the school was interested in was the culprit. Expectancy, in point of general interest, had long centered strongly in Compton. And presently, despite the reluctance of the chatterers to say anything now, the circumstantial evidence began also to point strongly to Compton. Several students had seen him, shortly after dusk, near the old barn. There was no doubt of his identity. He had not been around the campus at all during the evening. No one knew where he had been otherwise. His attitude

while near the building had appeared to be skulkingand, found in the debris of the explosion, was part of a notebook with the name of Compton written on the fly-leaf. 

CHAPTER II THE LEAF IN THE NOTEBOOK 

THE Faculty was justly indignant; Professor 

Stone of course being the most so of any of them, as he had suffered the loss. At Assembly the next morning the expected blow fell. Professor Matthews, the principal, arose and, after a short summary of what had occurred, invited the parties responsible to stand up and admit their guilt. "We realize," he said, "that nothing worse was intended than merely to give the dog an uncomfortable half-hour with a tin can, and the additional blowing of the horn, perhaps. While this is bad enough, and a student's prank of the kind that is not thought well of, still it fades to insignificance when compared with what actually happened. But we are willing to be as lenient as justice tempered with mercy can possibly be, if those responsible will now take a straightforward stand and admit their guilt by standing up." 

No one moved.

The principal swept each face with his keen-eyed glance, but there were no pale, frightened faces. Whoever was guilty was an adept at keeping a straight face. Mr. Matthews tried another method.

"We are not prepared just at this time to state who is guilty, but we have some very strong evidence, if it becomes necessary to use it. We can and will find and punish the guilty person or persons before we have finished with this matter. If they will now stand up here and admit their guilt, we will, as promised, be lenient. But if we have to search them out like criminals, there will be no mercy with justice. Expulsion is the harshest sentence we can give, and it will surely be given when the guilty ones are found-unless they now admit their guilt.”

Again he waited a long moment, then spoke sharply. "Mr. Compton, please stand up." 

Compton arose. "Are you, or are you not, guilty of this charge?" asked the principal suddenly. 

Compton flushed. "Not guilty, sir," he replied. "Sorry," interrupted Professor Stone bitingly, "but we'll have to try you just the same. Please report at seven this evening at the faculty council. Assembly dismissed." 

That evening it was Professor Stone who asked most of the questions. "Were you near the barn early last evening?" he began. "I was," admitted Compton, "shortly after dusk. I was near it for about twenty mniutes.' "What were you doing?" 

"I was searching for a notebook that I had lost." "Is this the one?" demanded Professor Stone, hold-

ing up the charred notebook that had been found after the explosion. "That's the one," agreed Compton. "When did you lose it, and how did you come to lose it there, by my old barn, if I may inquire?" "I had walked past there during the forenoon. 

Later I found that I had lost the book, and judged that it must have been near the barn." "If you walked straight past, why did you have to search all around it for so long?" "I did not walk straight past. I had walked two or three times around the barn, viewing it carefully." "What was your reason for this?" "I desired to note it in my mind, because the style was old and somewhat odd. It was not for the purposes you suspect. I know nothing more of what occurred than you do, and am not guilty in any way or degree." "Indeed?" There was biting sarcasm in the professor's voice. "Did you not rather note down in the book something about the barn, rather than note it in your head? Or perhaps you had thought the evidence to be all destroyed. But, though charred, the evidence is plain, on one page. Do you care to listen? 'Cans are tied to dog's tail, chloroform being first used. Ruse successful. Faculty scandalized. Br-'. "That's all there is left of the writing, but its meaning is plain enough. Have you anything to say now?" "Nothing, except that the note had nothing to do in any way with what happened, and to repeat that I am not guilty." "Is that all?”

"Absolutely." "Yet the act is well in line with your past record while at this school, and that past record tends to incriminate you." "That is past and over with, sir, two years ago,” retorted Compton with spirit. "It seems to me that you are rather disrespectful,” commented Professor Stone. "Also, your tale sounds improbable, at the least. I think, gentlemen, that probation is the lightest punishment that we can let him off with. If you are later proved actually guilty, Compton, you will be expelled. If you are able at any time to produce convincing proofs of your innocence, I will be ready to apologize for doubting your word."

So Compton went on probation. But the school, as a whole, could not believe him guilty.

For a time the boy reflected rather bitterly upon this shadow which had come over him, one which there seemed to be no chance to dispel. Then, with a spirit quite unlike that of the Compton of two years before, he sought his rooms and went to work. If barred from athletics by probation, he at least had more time for writing, and he needed the money-needed it badly, for sales had been falling off. Probation also barred him from leaving the school grounds for any great distance, but this also gave him more time for work . . . so it might have been worse, he reflected.

There were some others in the school who did not accept things with so calm a view. Among these were 

Kennedy, the coach, and Stanwald, captain of the eleven. All during the weeks of practice they had fully

planned to put Compton in as left guard on the second team, and with the intention of using him in several of the big games on the varsity, for, if he had been good varsity material two years before, he was even better now-the best man of any for the place, even better than Miller. But Miller, too, was assured of a place, for the schedule would require both. Compton was needed, badly needed.

The second team had been definitely chosen on the first afternoon that Compton was absent, and the third squad dismissed, with the exception of Burr, left guard. He was retained, and during the next few days was, with Grant, of the second team, given a very thorough trial, but, as Stanwald sorrowfully remarked, the two together wouldn't make one Compton, and they were utterly unable to give the varsity any such opposition as it needed. Thus it was that, a few afternoons later, as Compton was coming from studies and planning a quick dash for his rooms, Stanwald found and intercepted him. "Hurry right along, old war-horse," he said, "but get your togs and make a break for the field then. 

Kennedy saw the faculty last night, and you can play substitute now in practice. I know that's a pretty raw deal for you, but if you'll do it it'll be a wonderful help to the varsity. The first game is only a few days away, and we need your help, badly." "All right," agreed Compton, "I'll be right there."

He was there, that afternoon and the next several days, and, as Stanwald had said, he was a vast improvement over either Burr or Grant. But one fact

soon became noticeable: though he played hard, and was quite evidently not sulking, he was not as good as he had been. Rather he seemed tired, as if hard driven. 

The keen-eyed coach noticed this, and cautiously commented on it, but Compton laughed it off, declaring that he felt fine. As a matter of fact, hard-pressed for time, he was sitting up later nights than was good for him. Having a room by himself, and away from the dormitories, he could and did work unmolested. The ten o'clock limit for athletic men did not bother him now. While he respected the rule, still, while he needed the extra time and could not play in any real game where condition counted, it did not so greatly matter. 

On the evening of the day that Coach Kennedy had spoken to Compton, he noticed the light in the boy's room as he chanced to pass. A thoughtful pucker creased his brows as he came to the campus beyond, warm with late autumn's choicest weather, and a-swarm with students. Very few were studying, this evening. Returning near eleven o'clock that night from a long stroll, he looked again. The lights were still burning.

After that, Kennedy made it a point to watch near eleven o'clock, for several nights, and on nearly every night he found the telltale gleam. But he said nothing more, and Compton's playing was improving. It was puzzling, in a way-and then a series of incidents began to happen which added still more to his bewilderment, for again various student pranks were beginning to become the order of the night. For several days after the disastrous episode of the barn and car, there

had been a marked absence of any mischief. Now they were commencing after a period of calm. However, none of them were serious in themselves, as yet. 

Heeding the danger signals, the faculty again warned the students against mischief, and repeated that any serious offense, if proved, would lead to expulsion. But still they continued. Kennedy wondered if Compton could be connected with them. The lights might be a blind—and they might not. They were strange enough, in themselves, for Compton took nobody into his confidence regarding them. In the end the coach, having learned wisdom, said nothing. He had no proof, and there was nothing serious; the faculty had issued sufficient warning. He needed Compton on the second team. 

Then, the day before the opening game with Baker,

Miller, in a short and supposedly easy practice scrimmage, hurt his ankle, so that the doctor declared he could not play on the morrow. Barr, substitute right guard, was little better in the place. Rumor had it that 

Baker was strong this year, and the given weight of her players seemed to indicate that the rumors might have foundation in fact. Compton was the man who was needed, so, being a man who tried to get what he wanted, Coach Kennedy went to the faculty. He pointed out to them the full extent of the afternoon's disaster, told the one way to remedy it, reminded them that Compton had never been caught in a lie, that he declared himself innocent, and that they had no actual proof against him. Probation amounted to nothing. 

Let them remove it, and let him play. If he was guilty,

it would some day come out, with the proof, and then he could be justly punished. If he was innocent, probation now was worse than useless. So let them remove it.

Coach Kennedy being a convincing talker, and a decidedly valuable coach as well, the faculty complied, and he started at once to hunt up Compton. 

It was close to ten o'clock when he came to Compton's room, but there was no light burning there now. 

Kennedy knocked, then, receiving no response, and the door being unlatched, looked in. Compton was not at home, though probation required that he should not leave his rooms after nine at night. Filled with a sudden thought, Kennedy recrossed the campus to the dormitories. He had a pretty clearly defined idea in his head as to who those other members of a "gang" would be, if there was such. Investigation proved their rooms to be likewise deserted. Inquiries and a search of the campus failed to disclose anything of their whereabouts.

CHAPTER III THE STRAIGHT EVIDENCE 

ALMOST too well satisfied with his detective work, Kennedy returned to Compton's rooms. They were still in darkness, but, as he approached, he saw a dim figure move in the darkness at the side of the house. 

There seemed something furtive in the air. Carefully

the fellow came to the door, opened it, and slipped within. Kennedy had recognized him in that moment. 

It was Compton. But why had he been so furtive about it all? He must have heard the coach approaching-but he had not wanted to meet him, or to be seen, but, still less had he wanted to be away from his rooms if the coach should come in, so he had sneaked in. 

Still undecided, Kennedy turned away. What was the cause of all this, anyway? He'd get to the bottom of it-and then he stopped to stare off in the darkness. 

Half a mile away, in an old, all-but-deserted portion of the town, the light of a fire had suddenly sprung up. 

And Compton had come from that direction.

The flames continued to mount higher as Kennedy approached. Already the fire department was out, and the school and town, aroused from early slumbers, were beginning hastily to appear. But the fire itself, while big enough for a few minutes, was unimportant. 

An old, deserted house, stored nearly full of ancient magazines and papers, of little value in themselves, especially as their collector, an old man with a hobby, had died some months before. The papers, catching fire, would soar into the air for a moment, hover, and go out in the darkness. With the fire department on hand, there was no danger of the blaze's spreading, but the papers burned slowly, prolonging it all. Kennedy fumed to himself. The first game of the season to be played on the morrow, and here was the eleven out at midnight, with the rest of the school. The coach immediately set about rounding them up for bed again. 

But, once the excitement had died down and the

school was at least in bed again, whether asleep or not, the coach continued to walk about the darkened campus. Early comers at the fire had declared that the interior of the building had all the appearance of being used as a club house, with alley-ways between the high stacks of magazines which littered the rooms. Mystic symbols were painted within. To the coach, long experienced in such things, but one meaning was leftthere was a gang, an order of mischievous night-raiders, among the student-body. Again their plans had missed fire in the small degree resulting in actual fire. 

The thing was serious. Was Compton a member of this gang? And if so, was he the leader?

Kennedy sighed as, after a long walk, he sought his lodgings in the main dormitory. From the darkened halls still came a low buzz of conversation. The students were discussing the known facts. On the morrow or to-day, for dawn was not far off-he would have a tired team with nicely frayed nerves. And, whether he suspected Compton or not, Compton must play. 

There was no one else. 

As the hour of the game approached, Coach Kennedy knew that his worst expectations were about to be realized. The team was tired, nervous, unstrung. The night before, they had been confident. Now that confidence was gone. The contest was to be played on Orion field, and when the Baker boys arrived, shortly before noon, there was nothing about them to quiet jumpy nerves. They were big and beefy, and in practice before the game were both swift and sure, calmly confident. Most of them were from the last season's var-

From the pipe of the whistle, Baker started in to carry the war into the enemies' territory, and during the first quarter they succeeded with disheartening success. Orion made but one during the game, and that was by Captain Stanwald, who came out of a mixup and raced down the field in the most approved style-resulting in an almost unexpected victory.

Compton played poorly. His eyes were red-rimmed, his face haggard. Kennedy watched in silence, and passed without comment the fact that Baker gained ten of their seventeen points "through" him. The final score found the victory Orion's by four points.

This particular prank was evidently intended to be a rehearsal for Hallowe'en, still some weeks off, but close enough to be thought of in that connection. The first street off the campus of the school proper, and the last street of the town, was called "Faculty

On this night an entertainment was being given on the campus for the professors and their families, therefore every house was dark and empty, and it afforded an excellent opportunity for conspirators to work.

CHAPTER IV COMPTON GOES 

COMPTON's trial, held the next morning, was soon over. Those who were responsible for the events of the previous night did not bother to come forward and

admit their guilt. While no actual damage had been done, other than to Professor Stone's dignity, still he was decidedly angry, and the faculty could not but believe the evidence which appeared so plain. Therefore, as Compton realized from the start, he had no defense but his word, and that would not be believed. The evidence was too strong against him in every case. "You say you are not guilty," said Professor Stone. “How, then, did you come to be there at the moment the fire started, so as to put it out?" "I had been taking a walk. That was certainly leaving the best fishing for Clyde, but "Shows how good he thinks he is!" Clyde growled to himself. "He wants to let me see that he can catch a prize fish out of water that I've already failed in, and then brag about it."

The fact that there were plenty of other fishermen after the prize meant nothing to Clyde. The real rivalry, as he saw it, was between Edgar and himself, and this time he was determined to be the winner. He had a real respect for Edgar's prowess with hook and line, and knew that if he could best Edgar, he would stand an excellent chance of carrying off the prize.

So far, his luck had been just average. He had taken several nice strings of fish, which were appreciated at home, and he had caught a few big ones, his biggest being nineteen inches. But one fisherman had brought in a twenty-two inch specimen, which, so far, held the honors in the contest. Clyde reflected with satisfaction that Edgar had not taken in any nineteeninch fish or better for exhibition. 

But the winning of the prize, in the few weeks remaining, meant catching a fish at least four inches longer than the big one he had already brought in. 

And that, as Clyde knew, was a job for anybody.

Nineteen inches was a mammoth rainbow trout, for these waters. The largest on record as having been caught in the community was twenty-three inches ... the size he needed to win. And the big fish of this season was the second largest ever caught hereabouts.

Added to that was the fact that practically every stream and pool had already been fished over several times, and was still being whipped regularly by other enthusiasts. Most of the big ones who would bite this year had done so. The others were wary, almost impossible to hook. 

Two weeks before the closing date of the contest,

Clyde caught another big one. He had set out at midafternoon. About ready to quit for the day, with the shadows of evening lengthening, he had seen a white moth drift down over a pool, then the great leap of a monster trout. Instantly he was all excitement, and presently, using his utmost skill, he sent another white moth drifting down over the pool, but this one was encased in a snell hook.

Once again the trout leaped, and, with careful flexing of the wrist at the one split-second of time when it would count, Clyde had him hooked. Thereafter, he waged a battle royal for the next twenty minutes, in an effort to keep his fish from darting under a sunken log at the far side of the pool. Clyde guessed, from the frantic efforts of the trout to reach that log, that he had already broken many a line there, and knew that he would snap this line as quickly, if he could but reach it.

It required all of Clyde's skill to control the singing

reel and leaping fish as well, but he succeeded. His biggest thrill, aside from the final landing of the fish, came a minute before the final victory, when, in a last desperate lunge toward freedom, his giant trout somehow disturbed another king of the pool. The other trout leaped high, and for one fleeting moment, while his own fish was in the air as well-the line from its mouth furnishing positive identification-Clyde saw, and gasped.

For his own trout was a monster-subsequently it proved to measure twenty inches-but the other fish, beyond any question, was several inches longer. 

Clyde was thoughtful on his way home. He had heard no reports of the big fish in this pool, which meant, evidently, that they did not rise to ordinary lures or at ordinary times. They were doubly wary, and especially that big one. He hadn't risen to any lure, natural or artificial, tonight. Only the maddened leap of the other fish had surprised him into showing himself. If Clyde could find the way to get him then the prize was as good as won. For the remaining days of the contest, Clyde knew but one ambition, to hook the king of the pool. Ordinary methods of fishing, he knew, would be utterly useless here. The big trout would rise to no ordinary lure. 

The first thing, then, was to study his habits, and find out when he did his feeding, and on what. So, for such time as he could find for the next two days, Clyde lay hidden in the grass and brush beside the pool and watched patiently. Not once did the big trout rise during his hours of vigil.

So Clyde changed his tactics, and watched the next night. Almost convinced at last that the big fellow either never ate or that he had left the pool, he finally was rewarded, a little past daylight. In the clear water he saw another trout, perhaps eight inches long, rise, saw a greater swirl, a tremendous strike . . and the king trout dipped back to the depths again. “Aha!" Clyde muttered. "So you're a cannibal, eh, and early morning is your favorite meal time? I'll remember that."

With this information, he felt that he was well on the road to victory. But one thing bothered him in the ensuing days. Edgar Dodge still persisted in fishing somewhere near him, though always carefully avoiding getting ahead of him or coming too close. Two or three times Clyde had caught a strange look on Edgar's face, as if he had been about to speak, but had thought better of it.

But now, he knew, he must be doubly careful. 

Otherwise, Edgar would notice him fishing only the one pool, and would guess at the truth. Accordingly, after his early morning try, Clyde was careful to fish other stretches for an hour or so.

Each dawn, equipped with a choice imitation trout of average length, he was on hand to try his luck. And three times the great fish struck. Yet he seemed to be a little faster than even the flexing of Clyde's wrist, and on two of the three occasions he got cleanly away. 

The third time he was partially hooked, but again broke away. And after these experiences, he would

warily refuse to rise again for any lure for two or three mornings. 

For four days straight he had sulked. The last day of the contest dawned, and Clyde set out, grimly determined to win today, somehow. He noticed that Edgar was already out fishing as well, despite the earliness of the hour, but this time Clyde took no pains to hide his destination. It was win or lose this morning. 

The twenty-two inch trout caught earlier in the season still held the record.

Again, using all his skill, Clyde sent his lure temptingly over the nose of the king of the pool. It was a cloudy gray morning, with the feel of rain in the air, an ideal day. And the results were all that he had hoped for. With a swirling rush the great fish leaped, struck, and the long imitation trout which served as bait had disappeared in an instant. The king was hooked! 

Thereafter, as Clyde instantly realized, he had the fight of his fishing career on his hands. Finding himself caught, the king seemed to go mad. But it was a crafty, fighting madness, and, like its lesser brother who had been caught two weeks before, it knew the uses of the sunken log on the opposite side of the pool. 

More than once it all but succeeded in getting there and breaking the line. It was fighting every second, and the strain on Clyde's arms was terrific. Within ten minutes they were aching, and the big trout seemed as lively as ever.

Setting his teeth, Clyde kept up the battle. He wasn't going to lose now, with victory in his grasp.

The king was tiring now. Yet he still had plenty of vigor. Finally, afraid that he would still escape if he sought to use the net, Clyde decided to risk an attempt to throw him out on the bank. A thrill shot through him as he succeeded.

But his joy was short-lived. In the excitement of the moment he had forgotten a tiny stream which trickled out from a little spring just behind him, and ran some thirty feet down to the pool. As luck would have it, the big trout landed squarely in this little stream, and he had fallen off the hook as he fell. 

Dropping his rod, Clyde gave a desperate leap. Already, the king fish, curiously cramped in this little bit of water, had straightened out, was starting to head down it and back to the pool. The water in the rivulet was hardly deep enough to nicely cover him, and Clyde had a good look at his prize, and gasped anew. He grabbed a large flat rock which chanced to lie handy, clapped it across the stream, and had his prize penned up safely.

Then, able for the first time to stop and catch his breath, he paused for a moment to gloat over his catch.

He drew a tape measure from his pocket and succeeded in measuring the big fellow, still as full of fight and life as ever, but helplessly penned up. Then he could scarcely restrain a whoop of joy. Two feet and a fraction over. Two full inches to the good!

He stood up, more soberly, picked up his dropped rod and stared at the empty hook. It was sheer luck that he had gotten the big fish out when he did. A moment more and it would have been lost.

ALMOST AS LARGE AS HIS OWN.

Clyde was still staring at the empty hook when a step caused him to turn. Edgar was coming up, grinning, a fish in his hand that caused Clyde a moment of jealous uneasiness. It was, without any question, a monster trout . . . almost as large as his own. But not quite, he decided, though still uneasily wondering if, after all, Edgar had once again surpassed him. "Here," said Edgar, and, to his astonishment, thrust the trout into Clyde's hands. "I want you to take it Clyde. Don't say no, now. It's twenty-three inches, and I just caught it. A full inch to the good, and it will be sure and give you the prize." He stopped 

Clyde with uplifted hand, went on breathlessly: "Let me finish before you say anything, old fellow. 

I've felt awful cheap ever since I made that remark last spring. It was a mean thing to say, and I don't blame you for being mad. I've been mad at myself. And I wanted some way to square myself. I know that you want this prize money for Agricultural College next fall, and I decided that I was going to fish this summer, too, as two stand twice the chance of one, and if I could get a big enough fish, I'd bring it to you for a peace-offering. That's why I've been fishing near you all the time so I could get it to you right away. "Now, don't say no," he added. "I don't need the money. But we'd planned to go on to college together, and I'm counting on that. And friendship well,

I've discovered that it's about the biggest thing in the world. You've got to take it, Clyde, for the sake of that."

Clyde hesitated. If he took the fish, that meant keep-

ing still about his own bigger trout, letting it go back to the pool. It meant that Edgar would still believe himself the better fisherman-Clyde caught himself up sharply. What of it? He'd know, for his own part, that he had caught the biggest fish and earned the prize fairly. And if Edgar was willing to sacrifice the prize and all the glory, for the sake of friendship. The big trout, penned up, stirred uneasily. Edgar looked around. "What was that?" he asked. "I've got a couple of fish on a string, down in the water there." Clyde explained hastily. "You're a prince, Ed. Go bring the string up, will you?"

As Edgar moved obediently to get the others, caught farther up stream in his search for bait for the big one, Clyde removed the flat rock, and with a flirt of his tail, the king of the pool slipped back to his old resting place.
