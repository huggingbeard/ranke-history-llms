CHAPTER XI 

Four months elapse without incidents other than trite, every-day affairs. As was their habitual custom once or twice each week, Linda Dugan and

Beryl were brushing their respective husbands' suits, and arranging things neatly in the clothes closets. 

The bedrooms were adjoining ones, thus making it less difficult for sounds to be heard by either occupant. Beryl, naturally could not help hearing the sudden exclamation that Mrs. Dugan uttered. The sudden thought that her mother-in-law had been unexpectedly taken ill, brought her to the former's doorway. "What is it, mother?" she queried anxiously. "Are you all right?" "Only but for a shock," was the answer. She was standing in the middle of the room holding a pair of trousers by the cuffs, and gazing abstractedly at some articles on the floor. Beryl caught the full portent of her mother-in-law's words when her scrutinizing gaze rested on the lipstick, rouge and powder on the floor at the woman's feet. "What do you make of it?" she asked, shifting her gaze to Linda's angry face. "Well, it leaves plenty of room for suspicion about these so-called business meetings that have been calling him out nights of late. One thing I'm not going to do-fret and bite my nails over this, but Dan will have a lot of explaining to do!"

Beryl, seeing the seriousness of the situation, tried to pacify her. "A practical joke, no doubt," she suggested. "That remains to be found out," snapped Linda, still seeing red. 

Optimism would not be accepted for the time being, Beryl could well sense, and she excused herself to her own room where other duties awaited her.

Deeply in thought, she took one of Elmo's suits out of the closet for a brushing. To her complete surprise, articles duplicating those she had seen on 

Linda's floor, came tumbling out of the pockets.

Some of the items laid where they fell, while a small round rouge box rolled under the bed, sending 

Beryl down on her hands and knees to retrieve it. 

To insult was added injury; in her excitement she struck her head a sharp blow while crawling out from under the bed. Assuming a sitting position on the floor, she began crying bitterly, meanwhile rubbing the bruise on the back of her head. 

Linda lost no time coming to her aid. She was presently helping Beryl from the floor to the edge of the bed, where she sat with both hands to her face, weeping. In answer to Linda's demand for an explanation, she took one of her hands away from her face and gestured toward the articles on the floor. 

This time it was Linda's turn to offer sympathy, but she did not. Instead, she gave way to a fit of anger. "So Elmo's up to the same tricks, is he?" she fumed. "Just wait until they both get home! Bad enough to have one cheat in the family, but here

we have two of them. Well, don't worry dear," she patted Beryl's shoulder, "we will settle this affair once and for all." She turned abruptly and stalked out of the room, and descended to the drawing room. 

A few minutes later, Beryl could hear Linda playing the piano. Although the piece she played was of a soothing nature, the notes were forced ones, as though the instrument were being used merely as a means of giving vent to the player's feelings. It was during those few minutes that Beryl made her decision, jumping up, she accented the thought by a stamp of her foot. Her mind was made up;

Elmo would not make a fool of her, not by a long shot. Without further meditation, she took her portmanteax from the closet and began packing it with her clothes from the bureau drawers. This done, she attired herself in navy-blue travelling clothes and started downstairs, debating with herself about announcing her intentions to Linda. Yes, it would be better that way.

Bag in hand, she hesitated at the drawing-room door. "Mother, I-I'm going to. . . ."

Linda wheeled about on the piano seat, and saw the dejected face of the heartbroken girl. "Beryl, my child!" she exclaimed in complete surprise. "You're not going to do that?" she rose and quickly crossed the room to put her arms around Beryl's shoulders. "I must have frightened you when I spoke angrily of Danny. But I had reason to because of all the trouble he used to cause me when he was drinking. And now when I thought he'd got-

ten over that, he seems to have fallen into another bad rut. Whether its a case of dotage or what it is, I'll get to the bottom of it. Here, give me that bag," she took it from Beryl's hand and put it down on the floor, while the latter felt as though the earth were crumpling at her feet, "and take off that travelling suit, my dear," again, Beryl permitted herself to yield to Linda's wishes, as the woman went on reasoningly. "Think of all the worry I've had with my Danny. This is your first real disappointment and you want to run out. Well, I'm not going to let you do such a thing, nor will I, myself.

Instead, we'll work shoulder to shoulder to keep our home, and if it comes to a show-down they will have to find another place to hang their hats, that's all." "I do hope there's some mistake," Beryl managed to say. "And so do I," Linda shook her head perplexedly, "but my mother used to say, 'you can live with a man until doomsday, and still never really know him,' and there seemes to be a lot of truth in that." 

She sank down on the davenport and leaned back on the cushions, and Beryl followed. Both sat in silent thought for several minutes, with their bodies apparently relaxed. It was Linda who was the first to take leave for her immobility, by jumping to her feet with an exclamation.

Come "We may be fools, but not for long. 

Beryl," she reached for her hand and nearly jerked the girl to her feet, "We are not going to let ourselves slip from our standards by fretting over this matter. Common sense is common sense even in 

1933, and we are going to exercise some of ours.

Now here's my plan: Let's each take a warm bath in perfumed bath salts, it will relieve us both and make us feel like a million dollars. What do you say?" 

Beryl snapped her fingers and her eyes danced. "A splendid idea, mother. You're right, there is no use in letting ourselves down. Come on, are you with me?" Beryl joyously caught up her bag and dashed into the hall, and up the stairs to her room. 

Linda chased after her like a happy child, and was breathing heavily when she reached her room. 

Having bathed in turn, and dressed themselves in afternoon gowns, they both looked as refreshed and invigorated as they actually felt, when they emerged from their rooms and met in the hall. "Well, I feel just as fresh as you predicted," 

Beryl announced. "There's nothing like a perfumed bath for sore feelings. Why I could face anything now-wildcats included." 

Linda laughed and caught her around the waist affectionately. "I knew you would feel better after . . ." she stopped short and turned her head in another direction. "Did you hear someone calling?" "It did sound like someone at the front of the house calling you. We can see from the front bedroom window."

Entering the room Beryl suggested, they looked out of the window. What they saw below opened their eyes in amazement. Beverly and a group of her girl friends were clad in rusty-colored corduroy trousers after the fashion of the popular Hollywood fad. They also wore white corduroy lumber-jack-

ets, and red and white caps with a brass button in the front. Having abandoned the bicycles they had used as vehicles, they formed a circle, in readiness for Linda's appearance at the front of the house. 

She raised the window and called to them. "What kind of a party is this supposed to be?" she asked laughingly. 

Beverly turned her beaming face up at her mother. "We are celebrating the return of beer,' was the gay reply. She then turned to the group around her. "All ready, girls!"

At the given signals, they gave three rousing HURRAHS. This was followed by a mad scramble for the house. Linda and Beryl met them in the hall. Beverly was the first to come forward. "Mother, dear, I've a confession to make. The other day I borrowed a pair of father's trousers and a pair of Elmo's for two of the girls until they got pants of their own. I put them back where I found them last night." 

Linda turned to Beryl and gave her a knowing look. "There's our answer," she said in a low tone.

Beryl nodded solemnly as she thought of how that little prank had nearly caused a catastrophe. 

No more, she resolved, would she form hasty opinions at the slightest provocation. 

Linda returned her eyes to Beverly, and burst out laughing. "And you've been celebrating the return of beer." "Yes, we had a pants party at Fanny's house. 

I'm going to take off this jacket," she took hold of the waist part and squirmed comically while she pulled it over her head. On her back was re-

vealed a sketch of a man holding a stein of frothy beer.

Upon seeing this, Linda had to give way to laughter again. "That is appropriate for the occasion, I must say." Then her eyes shifted to the hats the girls wore. They were designed into pretzels, spigots and steins. "This is some get-up, all right. And there are probably many more people who are thankful in their hearts for what Roosevelt has done. First, the stabilizing of the banks, and now the return of beer, which will doubtless put sorely needed funds back into the treasury, instead of putting it into the pockets of racketeers and lawbreakers. "It takes a man of Roosevelt's courage and ability to put a 'New Deal' into the people's hands, but not without our whole-hearted support. It will take the tireless efforts of each and every loyal American to pull our storm-battered ship out of the waters of turmoil; we can all help our president guide our ship of government back to the harbor of better times." 

Beverly turned to her friends, raising her arms dramatically. "Come my hearties, let's give three rousing cheers for our new President and for 1933, the year of the new deal, and the turning point in the battle against Old Man Depression !" 

"HURRAH! HURRAH! HURRAH!" Was the response in chorus.

She went to Danny and put her arms around his neck. "How silly of us to think such things of our husbands," she teased.

Danny made a few comical attempts to loosen her arms in indignation, but found himself a victim of her kisses.

Off to one side of the same room, Elmo was experiencing the same difficulty with Beryl. The onlookers, receiving their cue from Beverly, stealthily made their exit. And, at this happy moment, we also will do likewise.
