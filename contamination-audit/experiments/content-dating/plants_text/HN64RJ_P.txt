They stowed their gear aboard Foote's big motorboat, set a course for Deer Island light and shoved off amid some good-natured kidding from the rocking chair commodores on the dock. 

Just inside Deer Island light, they came alongside the old Boston Lifeboat Station, which formerly was moored off the South Boston beaches, and reported to Chief Bill

They were told to tie up alongside, where, in the gathering dusk, young Coast Guard enlisted men, most of them fresh out of boot camp, looked scornfully overside at the civilians playing sailor. 

The log of the first night read, "No events". 

But Flotilla 1-412 had been launched.

II 

There was such spontaneity to those early days, that exact dates have been lost in the first incomplete logs, and the names of some of the men who got the ball a-rolling may be overlooked, unintentionally. 

But Ranny Foote had the first inspiration, and he quickly rounded up such stalwarts as Lee A. Doble, Pierre Dumaine, Alton F. Philips, John H. Ferris, the Magnon brothers, Kenneth and John; Frank Masselink, Frank Ward, Arthur 

Gerald and Bob Reid.

For a time, almost every member of the yacht club who owned a motorboat was enrolled in the auxiliary, and put in time patrolling. Occasionally, the coverage was thin, but it was there, and undoubtedly the presence of those patrolling craft, like a policeman on a tough beat, discouraged what could have been early sabotage. 

The flotilla first was given the assignment to patrol Area

A, which included the waters from Reid's boatyard to Deer Island light, including the airport and the inshore side of 

Governor's and Apple Island. 

At times, it seemed like a waste of time, but then, on 22 June, an Army plane crashed on the flats between Apple 

Island and Winthrop. 

John Ayer and Louis Cloutier, 3rd, on duty aboard the

CGA 4G162, sped to the scene, picked up the slightly injured pilot and took him back to the club, whence he was taken to a hospital. Flotilla 1-412 was beginning to earn its salt. 

A month later, another alert crew saw lights flashing from a house on Cottage Hill. The Army was notified. The next night, a crew of the 4J843 took some Army Intelligence officers out to the same spot for a look-see. There was no more flashing from that house.

Meanwhile, the flotilla had begun to take on some semblance of organization. Foote became commander, Doble, vice commander; Fred Tucker, secretary, and Ken Magoon, commissary steward.

The new organization met at the yacht club on 27 Sept. 

Wind clouds were scudding across the sky. A southwest gale began to kick up, tearing at mooring lines. The meeting was adjourned for active duty. Available were Ken Magoon, Frank Ward, Dick True, John McElroy, Bill Hooper, Al Dodson, Earl Peterson, Ed Hoole, Paul Gaffney, Ted 

McCarthy, Ralph Somerby, Arnold Kiely, Millard Smith,

Jr., Henry Trongone and Charley Moore. 

Putting their seamanship to good use, they checked and secured mooring lines, rescued two youngsters from a capsized Indian, and when one of the club floats broke loose and threatened to crash through the bobbing fleet, the men secured it with new lines and so saved thousands of dollars worth of craft. 

But it was only the beginning.

III 

In the early days of the Coast Guard Auxiliary, members were entitled to wear certain insignia and to buy special uniforms to wear on duty. Many of the members availed themselves of this privilege, but it was not compulsory. 

Events, however, were developing rapidly. The armed forces of the United States were fighting a defensive action

in the Pacific where the Japs, with decades of planning and preparation, had gotten the jump.

The regular Coast Guard was needed for work among those thousands of islands in that vast theatre of operations. 

Once again, it turned to the auxiliary and said, "Men, you've been doing a great job, but we've got to ask you for more. 

Eventually, you may have to take over virtually the whole job of coast guarding the home shore. We can offer you temporary enlistment in the reserve. That will give you the military status you will need for the jobs we have in mind, although you will retain you civilian status at all hours off duty."

Boat owners were made coxswains or boatswain's mates, second class. Part owners became second and first class seamen. Regular Coast Guard uniforms were issued. Food allowances were made for feeding men on duty. Rations were bought in Winthrop stores and the flotilla began to assume the basis of a regular Coast Guard unit. 

Meanwhile, Ranny Foote had entered the regular reserve as a chief boatswain's mate and Ken Magoon was called to the job of flotilla commander. A veteran of the first world war and a man with a flair for making other men do what he asked and holler for more, Magoon injected new life into the setup. He was made a temporary chief boatswain's mate.

Frank Ward was picked as vice commander and Dick True junior commander. Able men themselves, they took much of the small detail work from Chief Magoon's shoulders, enabling him to pitch in on the job of making military men of part time Coast Guardsmen. 

IV 

One of the first problems confronting Chief Magoon was that of boats. The normal yachting season had just about ended and most boats had been hauled up for the winter. 

The 4L606, formerly Mrs. David Harrigan's Arual II, was Flotilla 1-412's pride of the fleet. A 39-foot motor sailer built at Bill Reid's yard, she was broad of beam and solid in the water.

She could sleep four comfortably and was fitted out with 10

a Shipmate galley stove, hot water heating system, refrigerator and sink. Many were the juicy steaks that cooked on that stove in those days before beef rationing. 

But Dave Harrigan, willing to lend his boat to the government for the duration, had no guarantee that there would be anything left but matchwood as the weather grew wilder. 

Finally, on 17 Oct., he delivered a ultimatum.

The wheels of government move slowly. In the nick of time, however, word came that the Arual had been accepted as a Coast Guard Reserve patrol boat.

It took more time to complete all the business of transferring papers, but eventually and officially on 17 Nov. 1942, she became the CGR 1020, a comfortable, fussy old gal who was to make the harbor patrol base gold braid sit up and realize that Flotilla 1-412 was on the job around the clock. 

Not for nothing had the government issued uniforms and permitted ration allowances to the new temporary members of the Coast Guard reserve, for by fall, Flotilla 1-412 had been assigned permanently to the Lifeboat Station to assist with boarding duties in the harbor. 

When an incoming vessel answered the challenge of the outer examination vessel, at the end of the North Channel, off Nahant, word was flashed to the Harbor Entrance Control Post on the hill above Fort Dawes, on Deer Island. 

This information, in turn, was relayed to the Lifeboat Station with instructions to board the incoming vessel as soon as it came through the submarine net at the entrance of the inner harbor.

Fishing vessels were checked as to crew lists, to make sure the radios were sealed-unless the skipper happened to be a confidential observer, in which case the radio was permitted to remain unsealed—and to make sure the vessel had not carried excess food or fuel to sea. Outgoing fishermen likewise were checked. 

Freighters, colliers, and tankers in addition to having their radios sealed, also submitted armament reports as to the number of guns and ammunition aboard, and the captains made confidential reports as to the trip and other pertinent information to commissioned officers of the Coast Guard, who also were attached to the Lifeboat Station. 

Bringing the CGR 1020 alongside these vessels in the oft times choppy ship channel tested the seamanship of the 11

temporary members of the reserve, but so well did they do their job, that not one serious accident was reported. 

On 7 Dec., after two months of vicious gales and high seas, a 26-foot gig from the Lifeboat Station was driven aground off Deer Island light, but with consummate skill, the crew was rescued and the gig towed back to the unit by the 

CGR 1020, manned by John Kirby, Jack McCabe and Charley Johnson. 

Five days later, the CGR 1020 was given her first vacation, a trip to Goodhue's yard in East Braintree, where she was fitted with new guard rails and her bottom copper-sheathed against damage by ice. 

Then, on the day before Christmas, she returned to active duty. The early Christmas watch saw John Kirby, George

Beattie, Frank Masselink and Doc Mayo on duty. They were relieved at midnight by Fred Tucker and Freeman 

Harvey. During Christmas Day, Chief Magoon, Dick True, Paul Gaffney and Earl Benson were the crew.

The New Year's Eve watch comprised Fred Tucker and 

Freeman Harvey, with 1942 going out with Flotilla 1-412 on the job.

VI 

The dawn of the new year brought a rapid rise in the number of personnel, the first inkling that the Coast Guard meant it when it said it might have to leave the home shores to the temporary members of its reserve. 

Night after night, the CGR 1020, weighed down by tons of ice, came in from duty to tie up for standby watch at the club, the radio telephone left open for messages. And morning after morning, she went out again "through storm or strife or howling gale..."

Late in January, because of ice conditions at the yacht club, it became necessary to shift the base of operations to the Harbor Patrol Base at Lewis Wharf, in Boston, and the men continued to report there at 0600 or 1800 hours for the 

12-hour tours of duty.

Regulations grew stricter and tighter as new faces continued to swell the ranks, until finally recruits were not permitted to go on duty until they had been issued identification cards and drawn uniforms. 12

The men also were learning what it meant to be in the armed forces while on duty, for a grim-looking Thompson sub-machine gun now stood in a rack in the cabin of the 

CGR 1020 and two business-like .38 caliber Smith & Wesson revolvers were kept in a handy drawer nearby. 

In early February, the flotilla met at the First Corps of Cadets armory in Boston, where all hands spent an entire day learning how to handle and fire the Thompson, the Springfield rifle, the revolver and the Colt semi-automatic pistol.

Meanwhile, classes in navigation, equipment and rules of the road were being offered at the meetings, and ratings became available to qualified men. 

Through all this period and later, the one man in the regular service who did most and meant most to the members of the temporary reserve was Chief Duggan, a small, wiry, black-haired Irishman, who was in command of the 40 regular reservists at the Lifeboat Station, but who taught the TR's many a trick of the trade.

A man of infinite patience with new comers, he won the respect of the entire flotilla and undoubtedly contributed much to their sticking it out through the long, bitter winter. 

VII 

The probable peak of the winter weather came during the night of 14 February and extending until late the following night. Henry Trongone was skipper of the CGR 1020 on the morning of 15 February and as he poked his head out of his peajacket collar long enough to read a thermometer, he had to look twice to believe it was 18 below zero. 

Like a true Coast Guardsman, he tried to get down the harbor to the Lifeboat station. The mist rising from the water was thick as any pea soup fog and he set watches both port and starboard as he maneuvered the boat out into the stream.

Less than 100 yards from Lewis Wharf, his skipper's keen nostrils detected an odor that spelled trouble and he was forced to turn back. The seacock had frozen and it was a matter of hours to thaw it out again. Next was a trip to the gas barge, but the pump out there had frozen, too. 

A third time, late in the afternoon, the CGR 1020 nosed out into the harbor. This time, it was possible to find a 13

gasoline barge that had a pump in working order. The 

CGR 1020, with a full load of fuel, then headed down the harbor.

The wind was from the northwest so that the trip down was quiet enough, but as the CGR 1020 reached the Lifeboat Station, Trongone found a field of ice moving in. Gingerly, he picked his way to the unit and reported. There was an ailing regular aboard. He was transferred to the CGR 1020 and the trip back began.

Into the teeth of the wind she ploughed, spray dashing and freezing across the bow and windshield until Trongone was navigating by the shouts of his port and starboard watches as they sighted one buoy after the other. 

That was the last trip down the harbor for 48 hours, but the backbone of winter had been broken. 

VIII 

Lewis Wharf continued to be the base of operations for 

Flotilla 1-412 until 16 April, when the Cottage Park Yacht

Club floats again were free and clear of ice. 

Meanwhile, the CGR 1020 was given another breathing spell by being taken to Tringali's yard at East Boston, where old hands and new alike pitched in to clean and paint the ship from keel to military mast. She was there from 

1 March to 11 March for various reasons, not all of them explainable, but eventually the yard help finished its work and the flotilla returned to active sea duty eagerly. 

Three days before the CGR 1020 returned to basing at the yacht club, the weather gave one real, grand farewell part to winter. The wind went on a drunken spree that lasted for two days, with a hangover that persisted for another one, at least.

High point of the orgy came during the night of 13 April, with Jim O'Connor, Clark Wallace, Pappy Holman, George 

McGarvey and Edgar Baker aboard the CGR 1020 as crew.

The wind increased in fury as the night closed in and the seas in the harbor cut and lashed at the shore and tugged on anchor chains and mooring lines. At first, the CGR 1020 crew tried to tie astern of the Lifeboat Station to avoid being smashed to pieces against the unit.

Finally, they cast off to seek shelter over behind Spectacle Island or farther up the harbor. Green water poured across 14

the deck and surged through the open windows of the pilot house, where Skipper Baker sought to navigate to safer waters.

Seawater poured down into the engine room and flooded it, killing the motor and leaving the CGR 1020 at the mercy of the waves. The anchor was broken out and heaved over. 

The line parted and the CGR 1020 was more helpless than ever. At long last, however, the crew managed to coax enough life out of the engine to make some lee at Governor's 

Island. 

Morning broke with the seas still running high, but at least the CGR 1020 crew could see their way around. Pale, drawn and shaken, the five-man crew got their boat to Lewis 

Wharf and staggered ashore, sad men but wiser.

There were various theories of what might have been done on that wild night but none of the men who actually were out in it would have done much different. They couldn't have, in that storm.

IX 

From April on, the spring of 1943 saw rapid changes in the harbor patrol, and as usual, Flotilla 1-412 was right in the thick of things. Dick True had been called into the service. His post as junior commander was taken over by 

Eugene Proctor. He and Frank Ward were made chief boatswain's mates. They had earned the rank, at sea as well as ashore.

Chief Magoon was barking at people who tried to make his importing business interfere with Coast Guard matters. 

The paper work involved in Flotilla 1-412 was piling up and the Magoon business address at 148 State Street was beginning to look like a branch office of the Coast Guard recruiting station, as, indeed, it really was.

Transfers of regulars from the Lifeboat Station had begun. Members of Flotilla 1-412 slowly were taking over the actual boarding duties in the harbor. Before the spring had ended, the CG 38521, a regular picket boat, had been turned over to the exclusive care of the TR's. 

Expansion brought the flotilla into operational possession, too, of the CGR 848, a 50-foot cabin cruiser that in carefree times once had been the property of Edmund L. Dolan and 15

James M.

Curley, former treasurer and mayor of Boston, respectively. 

The CGR 848 was a twin-engined job originally. One motor was missing when it was handed to Flotilla 1-412 for use on the East Boston dock patrol. The flotilla was asked to provide men who could man the CGR 848 for 24-hour stretches and the flotilla responded.

More often than not, the one remaining motor of the CGR 848 developed asthmatic tendencies and the boat repeatedly was tied up at Lewis Wharf on standby duty. It was irksome, and the rapidly changing personnel at the base invoked new rules and regulations that chafed on men accustomed to operate from their own base, at Winthrop. 

Finally, however, a conference was held at the base and out of it came the decision that Flotilla 1-412 would concentrate its efforts at the Lifeboat Station, taking over more and more duties as regulars were transferred. 

The ill-starred CGR 848 was left to the tender mercies of others, while Lewis Wharf, for the summer, at least, became only a port of call for supplies and liberty parties from the 

Lifeboat Station down the harbor.

Flotilla members were concerned with learning how to seal radios, to fill out armament reports, to learn how to stand deck and radio watch at the unit and even to scrub decks and wash waist high stacks of dishes.

A freighter received clearance to enter the submarine net shortly after midnight on the morning of 15 May, 1942. The 

CGR 1020, with Gus Frank, coxswain, at the wheel, cast off from the Lifeboat Station and moved out into the channel to deliver a boarding party that included an ensign, a gunner's mate and a radio sealer.

As the patrol boat poked her nose out into the bright, moonlit water, the TR crew kept a sharp watch. In anchorage No. 2, another freighter swung silently at her mooring.

Suddenly, Frank spotted something in the water. It could have been a small cask bobbing on the swell. Frank look at the ensign and pointed. "Let's get the freighter," he was told. 

The CGR 1020 delivered the boarding party aboard the freighter. As soon as the mission was completed, the party 16

started back to the Lifeboat Station. Frank continued to keep a sharp lookout for the object.

He spotted it again and swung his wheel to the spot. The object proved to be a sailor, with a Mae West life jacket strapped on. The man was blue and there appeared to be no sign of life. By radio telephone, the Lifeboat Station was notified.

As the CGR 1020 approached the unit, there were signs of activity. Chief Duggan had blankets warming on the galley oven. Men were standing by with a stretcher to transfer the victim.

He was carried in and placed on the mess table. Then 

Chief Duggan went to work, doing most of it himself but issuing quick, calm orders for whatever he could not reach. 

Finally, at long last, there was a flicker of eyelids and signs of breathing. The man was still alive. He had been saved in the 59th minute.

He could have been a saboteur who had become numb while trying to swim around the anchored freighter, seeking a place to set a time bomb. He could have been trying to get ashore to wreak sabotage. It developed that he was just a homesick youngster trying to desert ship. That was merely a coincidence. Alertness, at any rate, had paid dividends.

ΧΙ Boat work is only part of the Coast Guard story. Within a few weeks after headquarters had decided that the Lifeboat Station would be Flotilla 1-412's particular job, the outfit was called on to supply men for beach patrol from 

Point Shirley to General Edwards bridge, Point of Pines.

This opened up a new field for recruiting and men were encouraged to enlist for beach patrol only. The first man to volunteer for this new endeavor was Howard S. Reynolds, another funeral director.

By this time, incidentally, the clergy was about the only human field unrepresented on the roster of Flotilla 1-412. 

The total enlistment had passed 300 and still was surging upward.

The beach patrol meant sharing work with Flotilla 414 at Point of Pines and operating from the beach patrol base on upper Revere Beach. In true 1-412 fashion, however, the patrol was manned faithfully. 17

Chief Magoon and his aides found their paper work virtually doubled and their time left for business and home life approaching a vanishing minimum. Beach patrol meant meetings Tuesdays, Wednesdays, Thursdays and Sundays for a while. In addition, there were target practice and drill periods to be sandwiched in between times. 

There was only one solution. The beach division had to have separate, capable leaders to co-ordinate the unit with the boat side of the flotilla. Carroll Bickford of Everett was the man chosen. He was granted a chief's rating and told to go ahead. He fought for better conditions and more co-operation on the part of the beach patrol station executives, and eventually he got it. Reynolds, virtually a veteran through early and often beach pounding in the opening weeks, became assistant personnel officer under Bickford, with a coxswain's rating.

Headquarters in Boston successively closed and then reopened enlistments, but with a minimum of gaps, the new beach patrol unit carried on the 1-412 tradition of being "always ready for the call" and kept its areas covered. 

XII 

Training became the watchword as the Coast Guard auxiliary entered its second summer. A program was set up under Richard Cunningham, boatswain's mate second class.

Cunningham arranged lectures, training movies and classes in all subjects required for the quick taking over of the Lifeboat Station. Clayton Nickerson was set to work training men to read and send messages by blinker; John 

McElroy, chief boatswain's mate, concentrated on seeing that efficient motor mechanics for the two boats was developed. Other specialists appeared from time to time in other fields.

Coast Guard headquarters offered a week's indoctrination school at the Buzzards Bay Barracks on Cape Cod Canal.

Flotilla 1-412 sent down 11 men to the opening of the school May 30. It was the largest delegation in the district and its several members returned with the highest average marks for the school. Top student of the school was Allerton 

Cushman, coxswain, a hydraulic engineer in civilian life, who knocked off 98. 18

The other Flotilla 1-412 representatives were Freeman O. 

Miller, William Campbell, Charles R. Clark, Frank Perrault, William Nolan, John McKay, Herbert Permatteo, Parker

Greenlaw, Henry Gilbert and John Fenton. 

These men found that the school was primarily concerned with training beach patrol and dock watch specialists, but all agreed the week under real Coast Guard discipline was not amiss. Each man returned with some new skill, even if it were only the learning of the international code flags through the good-natured goading of a Winthrop young man, Richard Lindsey, signalman first class in the regular reserve and an instructor in the school. 

Not the least of the training program was marching. 

Time and again, Flotilla 1-412 was called on to take part in parades in Winthrop and elsewhere. One of the most memorable occasions was the Wakefield Tercentenary parade in the early summer of 1944, with Chief Chuck Schlaufman as host.

Always, when Flotilla 1-412 was on parade, its members were led by a kilted, Scottish bagpipe band, under the directions of Chief Specialist Jim Wilson, a veteran of the first world war, as drum major. Watchers stood open-mouthed as the black-browed Wilson, with an equally black busby on his head came swinging down the street at the head of the line.

Most of the members of the band, too, were veterans of the first war, principally with British, Scottish or Canadian forces, and some of them, including Wilson, had been decorated for bravery.

So famous did the band become, that even when the flotilla at large was not assigned, its band was called on to add color to parades all over the area.

Too old, most of them, to get into the second war with the armed forces, the bandsmen did their hitches with the TR's and many of them were working in war plants the rest of the time, turning out war material. 

XIII 

Chief Magoon was promoted to the rank of lieutenant, junior grade, in the temporary reserve, as of 28 May, although it was more than a month before papers granting him the right to wear gold braid came through. 19

George V. Wightman, noted Indian class skipper, and an early member of the flotilla, was raised from the rating of boatswain's mate first class to the rank of lieutenant, junior grade, also. He then was taken intown to headquarters as vice-commodore of the auxiliary. 

One of the first big jobs the two men performed under gold braid was undertaken 4 July, 1943. Lt. Wightman telephone Lt. Magoon about 0800 that Sunday morning.

Headquarters wanted 17 men from Flotilla 1-412, including 10 who could handle sub-machine guns. They were to report at the Hotel Brunswick, port security headquarters, at 1300. 

Could it be done? 

It could. 

The flotilla was meeting that morning at Ingleside Park to drill for another Winthrop parade on the morrow. Lt. 

Magoon called for volunteers and almost immediately had to close the list. He had 18 men in nine minutes. 

Flotilla 1-412's detachment, under Chief Ward, joined other temporary reservists at the Brunswick. Scuttlebut rumors flew thick and fast. A parade in South Boston. A review for some gold braid. Guarding prisoners.

The latter proved to be correct. The duty officer lined up the TR's in a second floor corridor and told them they were going to be face to face with the enemy and that every man had to be alert, even though the enemy would be unarmed. A false move and the battle-experienced enemy could be in possession of arms that would kill.

The reservists were transported to Commonwealth Pier in trucks. There, they were told to load their pieces, tommy guns for the machine gunners and .38 revolvers for the others.

They then relieved regulars who had been on the job since 0600. At strategic spots on and around the pier, TR's paced slowly as groups of dark-tanned, light-haired Nazi Germans were sent down the gangplanks of a grim, grey transport, processed inside the shed and taken elsewhere. 

Nothing untold transpired, but it would have been interesting to know the reaction of the members of the superrace had they realized that their afternoon guards would return to jobs as bookkeepers, salesmen and shipfitters at the end of such a grim tour of military duty. As the war 20

progressed, the TR's were called out again and again for similar watches, because that first one proved successful.

Several weeks later, on a Saturday night at the yacht club, Lt. Wightman again faced Lt. Magoon. This time, it was at a testimonial dinner to the man who had fashioned 

Flotilla 1-412 into an efficient, on-the-ball unit.

Lt. Wightman related as much of the events of 4 July as he could at the time, under regulations, to illustrate the way Lt. Magoon could make his flotilla respond. It was a part of a presentation speech that ended with Lt. Wightman presenting Lt. Magoon a gold watch.

For once in his life, Lt. Magoon was practically speechless. 

XIV 

Delegation of authority to qualified subordinates is a secret that successful leaders have learned through experience and by example. Lt. Magoon decided early in August of 1943 that with Flotilla 1-412 expanding so rapidly, the ranking officers were getting away from the personal touch with individuals that they had enjoyed previously. 

Then, too, the keeping of records, the compiling of official logs and the work of mailing out schedules to nearly 400 was beginning to become at least an eight-hour daily job. 

Lt. Magoon met both problems with his customary zeal. 

Fourteen acting officers of the deck were appointed to supervise the work and on-the-job training of the men-two officers of the deck for each 12 hours of each half day.

These subordinate, part-time flotilla commanders were given a list of men due to report to them during a given week. It was up to the officers of the deck to pick competent skippers for the CGR 1020 and CG 38521, together with efficient motor mechanics and seamen. They were left to work out deck and radio watches, men for station duty, for galley duty and for boarding duty.

They were to see that the men received training in whatever specialties were offered, to keep records of the progress of the men of their command, to recommend for advancement in ratings and generally to act as liasion officers between the personnel and the top officers of the flotilla. 

The job began to click from the start. Men were encouraged to learn signaling, radio procedure and other more 21

interesting tasks, as well as to accept the less agreeable jobs like good Coast Guardsmen.

Meanwhile, the wives were given opportunity to take an active part in flotilla activities by enrolling as temporary SPARS. They were to do the filing, typing and mailing of flotilla matters at Lt. Magoon's now half-civilian, half- 

Coast Guard office, under the direction of his long-suffering secretary, Miss Lee Pentila.

No uniforms were issued at the outset, although there were vague promises from higher up that eventually these would be forthcoming. Nevertheless, the gals pitched into work. Among the first were Mrs. Herbert Permatteo, Mrs. 

Earl Ruddock, Mrs. R. Clark Wallace, Mrs. Eugene Proctor and Mrs. Ada Ross.

After a long wait, uniforms finally were issued and the TR SPARS joined the line of march in parades, setting a pace that made the male members straighten their shoulders, lift their chins and try to believe that all the cheering from the sidelines was not for the SPARS. 

XV 

Enrollment in the temporary reserve of the Coast Guard did not affect a man's draft's status, and as time went on, 110 members of the flotilla were honorably disenrolled to answer the call to the regular armed forces. Some did not wait to be called, but enlisted; and all served with distinction. 

While no actual credit was given for duty performed as a TR, the lessons learned on Boston harbor patrol stood the Navy and Coast Guard draftees and enlistees in good stead, for they quickly won promotions.

Eventually, the flotilla was represented in every branch. of the regular service. This included the Air Transport 

Command, and probably the flotilla's outstanding contribution to this branch was Freddie Lord, an early auxiliary member and a crack pilot of the Northeast Airlines. 

Freddie was commissioned a captain in the ATC, and although too modest ever to tell the whole story, even when security permitted, he took part in many an exciting mission. 

Despite himself, one story came out. In the mid-winter of 1943, Freddie brought his ship down in the Canadian wilds to bring food and re-assurance to the crew of another plane that had been forced down, far from civilization. 22 22

Later, the stranded crew members were flown to civilization in other smaller ships, and eventually, even the plane was flown out of the wilds.

That story broke through the press associations; otherwise, Freddie would have continued to drop in on meetings and even to make unofficial tours of patrol duty when he was on leave, without any of his mates ever knowing a thing. 

XVI 

Before the end of its second summer, the flotilla had increased its enrollment to 600, divided about equally between boat and beach duty. Between August and September,

Flotilla 1-412 was asked to absorb the beach section of 

Flotilla 414, Point of Pines.

Along in this period, Francis V. Ward, an early member and an indefatigable worker, received a deserved boost to gold braid, becoming an ensign (t) and drawing with it the additional duties of assistant division officer of Division 5A, serving under Lt. Magoon. 

Herb Permatteo, who had mastered more Coast Guard lore in six months than many regulars do in the same number of years, was selected for the job of personnel officer to succeed capable Gene Proctor, who was forced to drop most of his Coast Guard work because of civilian business pressure. Permatteo was promoted to chief boatswain's mate, the rate held by Proctor.

As the fall wore on and weather became rougher, the TR's of Flotilla 1-412 showed their mettle in battling the elements and drew repeated praise for their all around efficiency from Chief Bill Duggan. The flotilla lost a good friend when Lt. (jg) Vincent Nasca was transferred from the Lifeboat Station, for he had been interested in the educational program and always was ready to talk to the flotilla on subject of general interest.

The older members recalled the first boarding officer with whom they came in contact. He was Ensign Louis H. 

Kramer, who gave the TR's their first lessons in boarding procedure, and their first contact with gold braid.

Ensign Kramer taught his lessons well, and showed his appreciation of what these volunteers had done by writing an official letter of commendation to Capt. Frank M. Meals, 23

Captain of the Port of Boston, when he finally was transferred to sea duty.

Later, the flotilla came to know Lt. (jg) George Tracy, who was attached to the Lifeboat Station for more than a year as chief boarding officer. Lt. Tracy also became temporary-reserve minded as time went on, and his transfer, too, left a gap.

XVII 

The turn of 1944 brought many changes. The flotilla roster was close to its all-time peak of 626 enrolled members. 

This left room for increases in rate. The 14 officers of the deck on the boat section were advanced to chief's ratings. 

The shore, being a younger organization, did not gain these advances as quickly, but they came along in due time. 

Then suddenly, at noon 27 March 1944, the order came to secure the beach patrol barracks at Revere Beach. Manpower shortages and the course of the war made beach patrol a non-essential, in the opinion of headquarters. 

The people of Revere, particularly the Point of Pines section, greeted this news with an effort to circulate a petition, asking for retention of the beach patrol. They had to be dissuaded, since regulations are not changed by petition. 

In the year the beach patrol had been active, it had become an efficient unit under Chief Bickford, soon to be granted a temporary commission as an ensign, and later to increase the gold stripes.

The regular personnel at the beach had been reduced from 60 down to a maintenance force of 8, so well had the TR's done their job. 111 persons had been taken to hospitals, fires, breaks and lost children had been reported. 

And, although the patrolmen chuckled as they stepped over lovers on the sands during their tours of duty, they knew that by and large their presence had prevented more serious occurences.

In less than a month, the shore section was given a new assignment. This was to guard the East Boston docks of the waterfront, where valuable cargo and soldiers were being shipped out to the fighting fronts. Here again, longshoremen were fished out of the water, fires were quickly reported and the TR's continued to earn their salt. 

Headquarters were established on the second deck of the 

Jefferies Point Boys Club on Marginal Street, East Boston, 24

and it became the only Coast Guard Station in the world manned 100 per cent by TR's.

The shore section men, with a spirit that awed the boat section, overhauled and remodelled the East Boston station, until it was snug and shipshape; oftentimes neater than some of the equipment the more carefree boat section handled. 

The new assignment began 23 April 1944. 

XVIII 

The next red letter day in the flotilla's history was 7 May, 1944, when 400 members turned out for a review before Rear 

Admiral R. R. Waesche, commandant of the Coast Guard, on Boston Common. In all, 5000 TR's from the First Naval 

District were in the line of march, and nothing short of West Pointers could have put on a better show as they swung past the reviewing stand.

That review only slightly surpassed an event that had taken place a short time before, 22 April, at the First Corps of Cadets Armory, where a flotilla review and dance was held, with another 400 members in attendance. Here, Commodore W. N. Derby, district Coast Guard officer, reviewed the battalion and pronounced it excellent.

XIX 

Most of the spring at the Lifeboat Station had been spent in burning, scraping and painting the unit. It was a long, dull, uninteresting task, but eventually, like everything else, it was completed.

But the job had left its mark. The regulars still attached to the Lifeboat Station at that particular period were not the most enthusiastic in the world, and the TR's, trying to do their best, even though many of them had finished a full night's work in war plants before reporting for duty, felt a little imposed on as they saw how the regulars managed to disappear when the work was being done. There was some grumbling, and a few TR's managed to find excuses to disenroll, but as the good weather came on, most of the hard labor was forgotten. 

In the late spring, Flotilla 1-412 lost another old friend.

She was the fussy, sometimes asthmatic CGR 1020. The 

Coast Guard authorities began to consolidate the lines, and that included decommissioning the private yachts that had 25

been loaned to the government for the duration or any fraction thereof. 

It was decided that the TR's at the Lifeboat Station could handle the regular, 38-foot picketboats as capably as 

Regulars. In addition, these boats were faster and quicker on the tiller than private boats like the CGR 1020. 

So, the old girl was tied up at Constitution Wharf for several months, awaiting disposition of her case. Meanwhile, at least two men from every watch were detailed to stand by until the very minute she ceased to be government property.

Some of the newer men grumbled at this dull task, but those who had slept in her bunks, cooked meals over her stove and scrubbed paint, shined brass and piloted her through those early months merely looked at the new men with pitying scorn. They never could understand.

XX The summer saw another indoctrination school offered at Gloucester, and many of the members took advantage of it. 

This school had profited by some of the short-comings of the first one at Bourne, and boat men found they had not been overlooked. A 100-foot schooner was part of the school equipment, and a 24-hour cruise off Gloucester was part of the course.

But the first false rumors of peace in Europe were beginning to spread through the country, and these, coupled with the mark left by the burning of miles of paint in the spring, created the first uneasiness among the membership. Some began to wonder if the patrols were necessary now. 

So. Lt. Magoon talked with his junior officers and evolved a plan for showing the faithful watch standers that their work was appreciated. Certificates were prepared. Men who had served a minimum of 300 hours and six months of duty were to receive diplomas designating Awards of 

Achievement. 

Those with a year's service and 600 hours, were to receive Awards of Merit and those with two full year's service, or those who had served 1800 hours within 18 months' service were to receive Awards of Distinction.

These diplomas were suitably printed up with bronze, silver and gold colored seals in the background. 26

But the District also wanted to give the men some recognition, too. So metal wrist tags and citation certificates for men serving 600 hours or more also were provided. 

The first big night for presenting the awards was 27 October 1944 at the State Guard armory in Malden. This ceremony was repeated thereafter each month, as more and more men qualified.

This innovation served its purpose. Men began to check their records carefully and took pride in trying for the various awards. Disenrollments continued, of course, but the backbone men of the flotilla carried on, keeping the tradition alive.

There was, of course, some shuffling of assignments, as changes in civilian jobs and some actual physical weariness set in, but eventually the ranking officers became these:

One night at the start of cold weather in the fall of 1944, Commander Davis of the Harbor Entrance Control Post called the Lifeboat Station to say that the sonic devices on the hill at Deer Island were picking up the sound of hammering coming from the general direction of BLBS, and asked for a check on the cause.

Chief E. B. Holman, OD, turned out of his sack to investigate. He discovered he could hear a faint pounding, but reported that he could not determine the cause. Then, a few moments later, it dawned on him. One of the picket boats tied along side was moving gently up and down on the swell, bumping against a frozen fender on each rise. The crew, so accustomed to the sound, had forgotten to consider it a source of noise.

Sheepishly, Chief Holman called the Hill again. Commander Davis breathed a sign of relief, but uttered no word of condemnation. He understood.

Most crews ashore and afloat included groups of men who worked together on civilian jobs, but one of the most complete units was the Friday day crew at the Lifeboat Station, which boasted of six men from the Boston Herald-Traveler. 

They were Eddie Devin, night city editor; Fred Foye, school sports editor; Percy Harling, staff artist; Al Mello, Charley 

Leveroni and John Fenton, Staff reporters. Leveroni, the cook, became famous for his Italian meat sauce and spaghetti suppers, from which watch members, regulars and TR's alike, staggered away from the mess table with bulging belt line.

Sometimes, standing watch in the radio shack of the BLBS seemed like an awful bore. One man was assigned to listen for the radio telephone and shore telephone calls, and his watch mate to log everything that entered or left the submarine net off Deer Island and to handle lines whenever any boat came alongside.

But occasionally, all the long hours spent on routine watch proved their worth. 

One Friday, late in the day, a Navy plane crashed off

Provincetown on a routine flight. Two men in the plane escaped, took to their life rafts and set up their portable radio. 33333

Through one of those freaks of nature, the distress call could not be heard at HECP, but was picked up by the Friday night crew at BLBS, and the word was flashed to the Hill. Eventually, a patrol boat in the Provincetown area was contacted, and through word from the Hill, relayed through BLBS, the drifting Navy fliers were picked up safely.

Among the many boarding officers stationed at various times at BLBS was Earl Laird, chief warrant gunner and former Boston traffic policeman. A big, husky, red head,

Gunner Laird had a well-founded knowledge and understanding of small arms and a penchant for boarding every vessel in sight. 

One Wednesday night while on a boarding mission in the anchorage inside the net, he went aboard a ship that seemed to be anchored safely in the harbor and proceeded to the bridge to interview the captain.

At just about that moment, the ship hauled anchor and steamed out through the gate. In the darkness, he had boarded the wrong ship. The sea was too rough to have him taken off, even at the Outer Examination vessel, so Gunner Laird had an unscheduled stretch of sea duty as far as the entrance to the Cape Cod Canal.

From that night forth, around the mess deck, he became known as "Outbound Laird".

It was dark in the early evening of 19 March 1944. The radio watch on BLBS picked up the shore telephone and heard a tense voice from HECP announcing that a strange and unidentified object was in the main channel about 200 yards beyond the submarine net. There had been some submarine activity off the Atlantic coast within the month. 

Bravely, the crew of the CG 38520, a picket boat, set out to investigate.

They returned in a short time and fears at HECP were set at rest.

The object was a waterlogged Christmas tree, floating base upward out to sea.

One day in July 1943, however, a fishing vessel came through the net with an unexploded anti-submarine bomb aboard. The bomb had been dragged up with a load of fish, about 25 miles southeast of Boston. Gingerly, the bomb was handed into the gig from BLBS and taken back to the station. 

More sighs of relief.

It was a day typical of the nasty weather of the 1943-44 winter. A pulling boat from Deer Island light had capsized, and its lone occupant, R. C. White, 1 1/c of the regular reserve, was fortunate enough to be able to cling to the submarine net, although there was a heavy tide running out and the water was close to the freezing mark. 

Both picket boats and the gig from BLBS were sent to the rescue. The two larger boats could not approach too close because of the danger of going aground. The gig, however, was manoeuvered in close and big Chuck Schlaufman, weighing in the neighborhood of 250 pounds, wiggled his huge frame out on the pitching bow of the gig, clamped one of his fielder's glove-sized hands around young White's collar and virtually plucked him off the net like a snail from a rock.

The youngster had been granted a 48-hour pass in order to get married, so braved the stormy seas to keep that important engagement. After being thawed out, he made it, too.

It was something in the line of poetic justice, perhaps, that eight days later, Schlaufman, OD on Wednesday's day crew, was made chief, along with the other OD's. 

In every organization, there is always some comedy relief. 

Flotilla 1-412 had one of these individuals. For the sake of posterity, let him here be listed as The Brain. Unable for certain reasons, to get into the regular service, he enrolled in the TR's, and because he had no family to support, he volunteered to put in full time at BLBS.

His first week was quiet enough until Thursday, when that rollicking crew, under Chief Beattie and with prankster

The Brain dutifully turned the extinguisher upside down.

Later, Masselink handed The Brain a bucket and set him about "bailing out the well deck."

Twenty minutes later, Massie looked out and found The Brain dutifully scooping bucketfuls of water from the well deck and pouring them through a hatchway. "O.K. Mac," said Masselink, taking pity, "I guess the tide's too high; secure."

The spring of 1945 was one of recapitulation as rumors of the end of the war in Europe stirred up the scuttlebut that it wouldn't be long now" before the flotilla would be secured from active duty, in the harbor, at least. 66 The office figures showed that 1028 men had enrolled in 

Flotilla 1-412, and that an additional 190 had applied, but had fallen by the wayside for one reason or another. Meanwhile, 555 had been disenrolled for various reasons, including 110 who had gone into the armed forces and 15 who had been transferred to other flotillas. Three men had died, although none on duty.

So far as could be determined, in the period between October, 1942 and June, 1943, when the flotilla acted mostly as chauffeurs for regular boarding personnel at BLBS, boarding officers had been delivered aboard some 5000 vessels of various descriptions. 

From June 1943, when the flotilla took over most of the boarding, to 31 March 1945, some 17,000 vessels had been examined by the TR's. In that time, some 1700 radios had 38

been sealed and about 2000 armament reports had been taken.

The number of meals cooked in the BLBS galley by TR cooks, it is roughly estimated, would have fed half of Europe for a month. THE COMMANDANT OF THE UNITED STATES COAST GUARD 

WASHINGTON The Commandant of the United States Coast Guard takes pleasure in awarding the SECURITY SHIELD OF HONOR to the TEMPORARY RESERVE UNIT FLOTILLA 412, U.S.C.G. 

WINTHROP, MASSACHUSETTS 

for services as set forth in the following

CITATION: 

"For valued and important contributions to the 

Port Security program of the United States Coast Guard. The Temporary Reserve Unit, Flotilla 412, of 

Winthrop, Massachusetts, its individual members motivated by an unselfish desire to serve their country and protect their community, has not only contributed materially to the protection of the waterfront facilities and vessels in the Port of Boston, but has enabled the Coast Guard to release many full-time personnel for sea duty. The various responsibilities of this Temporary Reserve Unit consisted of boarding and examining vessels, sealing of radios for clearance, beach patrol, guard duty and fire watches performed at the Boston Lifeboat Station, the East Boston waterfront and Reserve Coast Guard Station. The members of this unit conscientiously and faithfully performed their duties frequently under adverse conditions and with many personal sacrifices. By its outstanding contributions and devotion to the Coast Guard, this 

Temporary Reserve Unit of Winthrop, Massachusetts has materially assisted and facilitated the effective prosecution of the war effort " prusuche 

R. R. WAESCHE 

Admiral, USCG
