Some writers of note claim that the English Setter originated in a Spaniel ancestry. To prove their claims, they quote early writers on the subject, of which the following excerpts will give you the foundation upon which their claims are rested: 

Stonehenge, writing on The Dogs of the British Islands, in 1867, says: As some difference of opinion appears to exist with regard to Setters, we have determined thoroughly to satisfy ourselves as to their origin and best form, and we have called all the best authorities to our assistance. We propose to place the result of our labors before the public, and to add our own conclusions.

There is no doubt that the sport of hawking was known and practiced by the ancient Britons, and that the Roman was totally ignorant of the science; but the invader at once came to the conclusion that the system might be improved, and introduced the Land Spaniel, if not the Water Dog, also, into this country. 

These dogs roused the game, and this was all that the hawker required of them in those early days; but in after years, as we shall see, dogs were required to point, or, in the language of the quaint old writer, "sodainely stop and fall down upon their bellies," and having so done, when within two or three yards, "then shall your Setter stick, and by no persuasion go further till yourself come in and use your pleasure."

At first, then, without doubt, the Spaniel was merely used as a springer for the hawk, which was subsequently neglected for the net; and the propensity of the dog to pause before making his dash at game was cultivated and cherished, by breeding and selection, until, at last, gratified by observing the action of the net, he yielded his natural impulse of springing at all, and set, or lay down, to permit the net to be drawn over him. After this, the hawker trained his Spaniel to set; then he cast off his hawks, which ascended in circles, and "waited on" until his master roused the quarry from its concealment, when he pounced upon it like a pistol shot.

When used either with hawks or for the net (especially in

The net enveloped a whole covey in its meshes, and few manors would allow of many coveys being taken in a day; whilst the disentangling the birds, and securing them, allowed time for the heavy dog to rest and regain his wind.

Richard Surflet, who wrote in the year 1600, gives us the following information. Writing of the Field or Land Spaniel, "of which sith before no author hath fully intreated," he describes him as "gentle, loving, and courteous to man, more than any other sort of dog whatsoever;" and as "loving to hunt the wing of any bird, especially partridge, pheasant, quails, rails, coots, and such like." He tells us we are "to choose him by his shape, beauty, metal, and cunning hunting; his shape being discerned in the good composition of his body, as when he hath a round, thick head, a short nose, a long, well-compast, and hairie eare, broad and syde lips, a cleere red eie, a thick neck, broad breast, short and well-knit joints, round feete, strong cleys (high dew-cley'd), good round ribs, a gaunt bellie, a short, broad backe, a thicke, bushie and long-haired taile, and all his bodie generally long and well-haired. His beautie is discerned in his colour, of which the motleys or piede are the best; whether they be blackand-white, red-and-white, or liver-hued-and-white; for, to be all of one colour, as all white, or all blacke, or all red, or all liverhued, without any other spot, is not so comely in the field, although the dogs, nothwithstanding, may be of excellent cunning. 

His mettall is discerned in his free and untired laboursome ranging, beating a field over and over, and not leaving a furrow untrodden, or one unsearched, where any haunt is likely to be hidden; and when he doth it, most coragiously and swiftly, with a wanton playing taile, and a busie labouring nose, neither desisting nor showing less delight in his labour at night than he did in the morning and his cunning hunting is discerned by his casting about heedfully and running into the wind of the prey he seeketh; by his stillnesse and quietnesse in hunting, without babbling or barking; but when he is upon an assured and certain haunt, by the manner of his ranging, and when he compasseth a whole field about at the first, and after lesneth and lesneth the circumference, till he have trodden every path, and brought the whole circuit to one point; and by his more tem-

perate and leisurely hunting, when he comes to the first scent of the game, sticking upon it, and pricking it out by degrees; not opening or questing by any means, but whimpering and whining to give his master a warning of what he scenteth, and to prepare himself and his hawke for the pleasure he seeketh; and when he is assured of his game, then to quest out loudly and freely." 

After describing Spaniels which "delight in plains or the open fields," and others more adapted for covert, he goes on to say: "There is another sort of Land Spannyels which are called 

Setters, and they differ nothing from the former, but in instruction and obedience, for these must neither hunt, range, nor retaine, more or less, than as the master appointeth, taking the whole limit of whatsoever they do from the eie or hand of their instructor. They must never quest at any time, what occasion soever may happen, but as being dogs without voices, so they must hunt close and mute. And when they come upon the haunt of that they hunt, they shall sodainely stop and fall down upon their bellies, and so leisurely creep by degrees to the game till they come within two or three yards thereof, or so neare they can not press nearer without danger of retrieving. Then shall your Setter stick, and by no persuasion go further till yourself come in and use your pleasure. Now the dogs which are to be made for this pleasure should be the most principall, best, and lustiest Spannyel you can get, both of good scent and good courage, yet young, and as little as may be made acquainted with much hunting.' 

"There is no doubt that the Setter is a Spaniel, brought by a variety of crosses (or rather, let us say, of careful selections) to the size and form in which we now find him. He is the most national of all our shooting dogs, and certainly has existed for four centuries. His form probably has improved." 

Stonehenge, in another part of his works, says that about the year 1555, a duke of Northumberl and trained a Sussex Spaniel to set birds for the net; and soon afterward the Setter was produced, either by selection or by crossing the Talbot 

Hound and Spaniel.

Another noted writer of his day was Laverack, who, writing on The Setter, stated that he was of the opinion that all Setters have more or less originally sprung from our various strains of Spaniels, and that he believed that most breeders of any note

"How the Setter," says this authority, "attained his sufficiency of point is difficult to account for, and I leave that question to wiser heads than mine to determine. The Setter is said and acknowledged, by authorities of long standing, to be of greater antiquity than the Pointer. This seems to be a logical conclusion, and if such men as Laverack could not fathom the depths of the mystery surrounding the origin of the Setter dog, we will doubtless be pardoned for our inability to do so. Therefore, we find it necessary, after reviewing what these authorities of a by-gone age concluded, to leave off as we began-to-wit, that there is nothing definitely known of the true origin of the English Setter, which, incidentally, is also true of all other breeds of dogs, excepting, of course, such breeds as have been originated within later years. 

We might quote from the writings of early authors on the possible origin of all the other old breeds of hunting dogs, but it would be an imposition upon your time and patience, for at the end we would have established nothing of a certainty except the fact that we were unable to draw aside the curtain of mystery which shrouds the ages.

THE SETTER THREE PRINCIPAL VARIETIES English: 

The English Setter comes in a variety of colors-black, black and white, black and tan, pure white, liver, liver and white, orange or red, and yellow and white spotted. A predominance of liver color is indicative of Pointer, or Water Spaniel blood predominating. Irish:

The Irish Setter is either pure red, or red and white, or yellow and white spotted. Pure red seems preferable to a majority of sportsmen. This dog's nose, lips, and palate, are invariably black. His coat is somewhat more wiry, and his frame more bony and muscular than the English Setter. He is the hardiest and most dauntless of the setter breed. He is apt to be somewhat unmanageable and headstrong, but if managed with a tight hand these faults can be kept down. He is full of pluck, he possesses rare speed and perseverance, and his dash renders him a favorite with many sportsmen who desire these characteristics in a dog. Russian:

The Russian Setter is a clearly distinct variety. This cannot be said of the Scotch and Welsh varieties, which only differ from the English as being inferior in purity of blood. The 

Irish dog is without doubt the original type of the Setter in Great Britain. The Russian Setter is taller than the English dog, and is very muscular and bony. The head is shorter and rounder than that of any other setter, and is covered with such a fleece of coarse woolly hair that it must be kept clipped away from the brows to facilitate the animal's vision.

recommend him to the sportsman, that the Setter does not possess. In the the first place, the extreme hardiness and swiftness of foot, natural to the Setter, enables him to get over much more ground than the Pointer, in the same space of time. Their feet also, being more hard and firm, are not so liable to become sore from contact with our frozen ground. The ball-pads being well protected by the Spaniel toe-tufts, are less likely to be wounded by the thorns and burs with which our woods are crowded during the winter season. His natural enthusiasm for hunting, coupled with his superior physical powers, enables him to stand much more work than the Pointer, and oftentimes he appears quite fresh upon a long-continued hunt, when the other will be found drooping and inattentive.

The long, thick fur of the Setter, enables him to wend his way through briary thickets without injury to himself, when a similar attempt on the part of a Pointer, would result in his ears, tail and body being lacerated and streaming with blood. 

On the other hand, the Pointer is superior to the Setter in retaining his acquired powers for hunting, and not being naturally enthusiastic in pursuit of game, he is more easily broken and kept in proper subjection. 

The Setter frequently requires a partial rebreaking at the commencement of the season, in his younger days, owing to the natural eagerness with which he resumes the sport. The necessity of this, however, diminishes with age, as the character and habits of the dog become more settled, and then we may take them into the field, with a perfect assurance of their behaving as well on the first hunt of the season, as the staunchest 

Pointer would.

The extreme caution, and mechanical powers of the Pointer in the field, is a barrier to his flushing the birds, as is often witnessed in the precipitate running of the Setter, who winds the game, and frequently overruns it, in his great anxiety to come up with it. But this occasional fault on the part of the Setter, may be counterbalanced by the larger quantity of game that he usually finds in a day's hunt, owing to his enthusiasm and swiftness of foot. Setters require much more water while hunting than the Pointer, owing to their thick covering of fur, encouraging a greater amount of insensible perspiration to fly off than the thin and short dress of the Pointer. better calculated to hunt in the coldest seasons than early in our falls, which are frequently quite dry and warm. 

A striking instance of this fact came under our own immediate observation this fall, when shooting in a range of country thinly settled, and uncommonly dry. The day being warm, and the birds scarce, the dogs suffered greatly from thirst, insomuch that a very fine Setter, of uncommon bottom, was forced to give up entirely, completely prostrated, foaming at the mouth in the most alarming manner, breathing heavily, and vomiting from time to time a thick frothy mucous.

His prostration of both muscular and nervous powers was so great, that he could neither smell nor take the slightest notice of a bird, although placed at his nose. He could barely manage to drag one leg after the other, stopping to rest every few moments, and we were fearful that we should be obliged to shoulder and carry him to a farm-house, a considerable distance off. 

However, he succeeded, with much difficulty, in reaching the well, where he greedily drank several pints of water, administered to him with caution.

He recovered almost immediately, gave me a look of thanks, and was off to the fields in a few moments, where he soon found a fine covey of birds.

The Pointer, his associate in the day's work, and a much less hardy dog, stood the hunt remarkably well, and seemed to suffer little or no inconvenience from the want of water. The 

Setter has natural claims upon the sportsman and man generally, in his affectionate disposition, and attachment, to his master, and the many winning manners he exhibits towards those by whom he is caressed.

The Pointer displays but little fondness for those by whom he is surrounded, and hunts equally well for a stranger as his master. *Although a principle, we have recommended the use of the Pointer in especial to the young disciple of the trigger, the first place, among shooting dogs, must be awarded to the Setter. In style and dash of ranging, in courage, and capacity of covering ground; in beauty of form, and grace of attitude; in variety of color, and elegance of clothing, no animal of his species will at all bear comparison with him.

MERITS OF THE POINTER 

The Pointer, historians of the dog family point out, is the offspring of the two ancient races known as sagaces, or intelligent, and pugnaces or bellicosi, or warlike, and many share the view that his portion of the latter blood is even greater than is generally supposed. The intelligence of the Pointer, except as indicated by his power to scent his game, is of a none too high order, and though docile and easily commanded, he is not generally sagacious, or affectionate. Neither his temper nor other qualities, except in the field, greatly recommend him; but for the steady pursuit of his game in the open plain, his caution in not over-running it, his capacity for enduring heat and thirst, and his retentive memory of what he has been taught, make him ideal for the pursuit of some species of animals and for some localities. The barrens of certain parts of our country, the New 

Jersey Pine-grounds, while there were Grouse on them, were well adapted to the display of his peculiar and characteristic qualities. The Grouse mountains of Pennsylvania, so rocky and so much beset with stubs that his bare feet, unless protected with buckskin boots, through which the claws protrude, did not allow him to bear the wear and tear of daily work. Some of the prairies of the West, which are dry and deficient of water, are well suited for him, and there, the Pointer is in good demand. 

He is, briefly speaking, an unusual dog, suited to unusual conditions. Like everything else in the dispensation of Mother Nature, the Pointer has his own particular place in the scheme of things, and fits admirably into the niche created for him.

HUNTING DOGS WE GO AFIELD! 

The crisp November air and the morning sun combine in exhilarating effect upon the soul of the outdoorsman. Our dogs strain at the leash with every fiber of their being. They know well enough that a day of sport is in store for them, and they are very impatient to get going.

At length we reach the hunting grounds, which is a natural set-up for Quail-stubble and uncut clover, a favorite rendezvous for Bob White.

We adjust our guns, the dogs are unleashed, and we are ready to begin the hunt.

The four dogs are expert at the game-two pointers-two setters-all bred in the purple and well trained. To the dogs these fields are familiar haunts-they have hunted them before. 

We know that we can depend upon our dogs to produce resultswhat, indeed, is a greater faith than that which a man has in his dog! Particularly when you have trained the dog yourself and know what he will do. 

Presently, our dogs make a stand! The moment is electric!

Sportzell, a setter of noble bloodlines, is on point-as steady and true as Gibraltar's well-known rock-Topsy, a pointer of high standing in the community, is backing. It is a picture fit for a painting. We sigh for a camera. It was ever thus-when the dogs make a stand like that which would look so magnificent in a frame hung on the wall of one's den, the camera is reposing in its accustomed place back home. 'Tis but the irony of fate! 

My hunting partner slowly approaches the dogs, commanding, "Steady!" And at the opportune moment, after having taken up advantageous shooting positions, the covey is flushed, and, exploding like a trench mortar, or a Russian bomb, they leave the earth with the whirr-r-r of an aeroplane propeller. 

Bang! bang! bang! speaks our repeating shotguns, and six of the flock fall to rise no more. 

Sportzell and Beauty break into a race to get the birds, which they retrieve tenderly tho' with evident eagerness. 

The remaining portion of the covey is seen to scatter, and we know that it would be of no avail to try to follow them. Birds of a scattered bevy, as you old-timers well know, are hard to find.

Quail, when they alight after they have been frightened, remain quiet and compress their feathers to the body, which withholds the scent, and so it is wise before attempting to hunt them to give the covey time to reassemble and get over their fright, so they won't lie so close to earth and withhold scent. 

And so endeth the morning's foray after the king of all game birds. We hie ourselves home for a noonday snack and, perhaps, another hunt in the afternoon. Ah, this is the life!

R. Thornton, who is Honorary President of the National Shorthaired Pointer Dog Club, and therefore speaks with authority: The breed is registered here in America under the name German Shorthair. This name in reality means, or could mean, any German breed having short hair. There are many different breeds in Germany that are dressed in short hair. The terminal name, Vortstehhunde, means standing or pointing. When combined with Deutsche Kurz-haar, Vortsehhunde gives the intelligent name, meaning German Shorthaired Pointer, or standinghound. They are also known generally as the Gebraushhund in 

Germany and Austria. The translation means many-use, or allpurpose dog, the latter term being applied to them because of their quick adaptability to all game. However, any really intelligent dog from several breeds may, with patience, be developed into a dual, or all-purpose dog. As a breed, they have no equal when it comes to readily adapting themselves to all varieties of game. All that is required is a chance, and the killing in their presence, whatever species you want to hunt. I have heard them criticised by those not well informed as being a

"jack of all trades and a master of none."

This criticism is unjust and made only by some one incapable of judging, or in other words, talking about something he knows nothing about. 

Without bias, prejudice, malice or any thought of remuneration, the following statements are made in all candor from actual experience in breeding and handling hundreds of these dogs. 

First of all, condemn no breed because you have seen one or two that were duds. I have heard experienced pointer and setter men say that only about forty percent of those breeds make good. I can honestly say that a much higher percentage of the 

German Shorthaired pointers will make good if given just half a chance. Do not expect a dog of any breed to develop if left shut up in the kennels and never taken out, any more than you would your bright child to read or write if never given a chance.

I have said many times that improper handling has spoiled more dogs than all the good ones ever produced. Most dogs are highly intelligent but one must understand dog psychology in order to be capable of handling a dog properly. Every look and every move your dog makes has a meaning. He is actually talking to you if you only knew enough to interpret it. I imported my first pair of German Shorthaired pointers in 1924, not with the idea of extensive breeding. This pair proved superior to our average hunting dogs, the best retrievers I had ever used, and handled all our native upland birds as near perfect as possible. I soon began taking them South on quail hunting expeditions, and found them to work equally well on quail, also took to squirrel, opossum, and perfect on woodcock. The demand for this breed grew so rapidly that I soon found myself an extensive breeder. Since 1927 I have had from eighteen to twenty brood matrons all the time. I have kept them busy, having raised hundreds of pups, and think I can safely say, without bragging, that I have raised more of this breed than any other man on earth. Dogs, guns, and fishing have been my hobby since early boyhood. I have been a keen student of dogs, and am proud of my understanding of man's most faithful friend. My experience with the German Shorthaired Pointer qualifies me to pass judgment on the breed with a recognized intelligence.

The German Shorthaired Pointer is a direct out-cross from the old German Pointer, a flat-coated, chestnut-brown dog, originating from the crossing of the Spanish pointer, with the Blood Hound. This dog was used for years in Germany as a dual purpose dog. As a retriever, on all feather game, on wild boar, deer, bear, and furbearing animals, possessing an excellent nose and worlds of courage. Field trials were unknown in those days, but with the advent of high speed came the demand for greater speed in their hunting companions. They then began crossing their old Ger man Pointer with the English Pointer. This cross carried over the sterling qualities of the original pointer but with the desired increase in range and speed. For range and speed they now compare favorably with our ordinary pointer and setter in our gun-dog class. I have had some of them of field trial caliber making cast as much as three-fourths of a mile. Range and speed of this character is too much for any man that hunts on foot, or in a fenced farming community, and is calculated to run any man to death. I have hunted them from saddle horse, day in and day out without a rest, and long days, and there seemed to be no limit to their endurance. One of them, I remember time and again, after a long hard day's hunt, when coming in to the car, would invariably double his range and speed, simply could not get enough hunting in a long day. 

This cross with the English Pointer produced the two-color scheme, brown and white. They may be solid brown but are never pure white. Always brown about the head and ears, and almost always liver spots over the body like the English Pointer. 

They may be any variation of the two colors, finely ticked known as either light or dark tiger, according to the amount of white or brown showing through the coat. Any color such as black or yellow is an absolute proof of impure breeding. The hair is, as a rule, more wiry than our English Pointer, and slightly longer, closely knit like a hair seal. Late in winter I measured the hair on my entire breeding stock. At that time it was found to be one-half inch long, a few specimens a little longer over the back and shoulders. Fuzzy or curly hair on ears or feathers also indicates impure breeding. At the time the hair measurements were taken, I measured and weighed my entire string of seventeen brood matrons and three stud dogs. The three stud dogs

averaged twenty-three inches at the shoulder; the seventeen matrons averaged twenty and one-half inches at the shoulder. 

Bitches averaged fifty and one-half pounds; the three stud dogs weighed, respectively, fifty-eight, sixty, and sixty-five pounds, all in good condition. These weights and heights will average less than the ordinary German Shorthaired Pointer. 

By selective breeding and proper feeding I have markedly reduced the size of this breed as they were too large and heavy to please the average hunter in the United States. Many of my first stock that I imported weighed eighty-five to ninety pounds, in fair condition, and were twenty-four inches or more at the shoulder. They are deep chested, well muscled with good feet, and can stand hard hunting over any sort of terrain. Differing in appetite from most of our hunting dogs, they always have an appetite, will eat most anything, and for this reason we watch our feeding carefully the first year. Feed three times a day for the first six months, then twice a day until one year of age. Older dogs are fed only at night, unless hunting them long days, at which time they are given a light feed morning and noon, and their regular rations at night. The pups are kept just slightly thin as too much feed will produce a large dog. This is an important point for those who like medium sized dogs. They are especially clean about the house and kennels, and small puppies resort to the kennel runs as soon as they can get out instead of soiling their enclosed pens.

The essential points in training or handling this breed differs very little from other sporting breeds. The most important detail is early handling, i. e., the first three or four months of the puppies life. The more human contact they have, the more handling and petting, without abuse, the better for the pup. 

By this you win their confidence and with ordinary care, this confidence will not be broken. My only difficulty with pups has been a lack of cooperation from hired help, just plain neglect of this one thing. When pups are fondled and cared for, a shy pup is almost unknown. This characteristic is carried over from the 

Blood Hound. One of England's oldest and largest breeders of 

Blood Hounds made this statement: "The most important thing in successful rearing of Blood Hounds is their early up-bringing."

They are peaceable and not aggressive fighters but should the occasion demand, they are capable of attending to their own affairs, are especially affectionate, fond of children, and a sensible guardian and watch-dog. The puppies will hunt and point at an early age if given a chance. I have had them pointing game at three months of age, at five months good shooting dogs. To do this they must be in the field almost daily from the time they are able to get about. They can be gun-shied the same as any dog, but are not especially susceptible to gun-shyness. We practice shooting a cap gun or blank pistol at feeding time, pound tin pans, and use the whistle. This accustoms them to noises of various kinds. 

The use of the whistle in a series of short blasts teaches them to come expecting to feed, and in the field they readily come in to the whistle when hearing this signal. One short blast, walking in the direction you want them to turn. The twenty-two is used about the kennels and on short runs about the ponds and river. 

In the field we use the .410. One shot only at a time and that while the pup is busily engaged in hunting. Watch his reaction and proceed accordingly. Kill an English Sparrow or some other marauder, let them sniff it, and then throw it out in cover suficiently heavy to make him use his nose. Not too far at first, as failure to find the bird may discourage him. Finding the object gives him courage and confidence. Gradually increase the length of throw and throw bird in a new place each time so as not to confuse with the old scent. As a rule they will retrieve it back. Caution should be used with them the same as any retriever, and not allow them or teach them to kill anything the first year or so, for so doing may make them hard-mouthed or

rough retrievers. Never throw them into the water. Start all puppies during the summer months when it is hot and the water warm, throwing a stick or some freshly killed bird or game where the pup can wade in and then a little farther where it will have to swim. Never crowd a pup into the cold or swift water the first year. By so doing he may develop a dislike for cold water before he knows what it is all about. Lessons should always be short, always discontinuing before the curiosity is worn off. Continuing too long may discourage your pupil.

In the field at first, let them absolutely alone, using the whistle only to call them in, and this not too frequently. Let them range where they will and do not try to limit their range. 

I had an owner come to me once wondering why this dog stayed at his heel all the time. He said that he would hunt for his boy but would not go out for him. I soon found out he thought his dog was going out too far and he kept calling him in. The dog, anxious to please, decided he did not want him to search for game, so he stayed at heel. I instructed him to not take the dog out unless the lad was with him and to let the boy do all the handling. In a few times out he had corrected his error and the dog developed into a perfect gun dog. It is a mistaken idea to believe you are teaching the dog. He knows more about hunting than you as he has his natural instincts besides a super-nose to guide him. The handler's only duty in the field is to curb faults when the proper time comes and do not attempt this until your dog is thoroughly bird-wise and loves the sport. If your pup ranges too far you can soon tame him down by hanging a thirty or forty foot rope on his collar. This extra load will soon pull out some of his energy. Stroking or handling while on point tends to staunch them. The more handling while on point, the better. If the dog attempts to break, grab his collar or tail or light rope tied to his collar and stop him abruptly, giving the command, "Whoa"! Talking to the dog while pointing only confuses him. The command, "Steady," and the great string of conversation you have heard the inexperienced handler "pull off" is more than useless and only tends to confuse the dog, and many times I have seen the young dog back off from his point and come in to see what his master wanted. Let them alone. The less conversation with any dog, and the shorter, the better. Never yell or pour out a string of commands. Teach

"Down," "Sit down," "Heel" -and that is about all you need to teach them. Do not whip or abuse these dogs if you expect to have success.

A well-known trainer of field trial and shooting dogs-our friend C. A. (Buck) Curtis, prepared the following article for us, which gives his opinions relative to the merits of the different breeds of shooting dogs and his suggestions for their proper handling:

There is so much to say regarding the training of dogs that it is a hard matter to decide where to begin. All dogs have a disposition of their own and to try and train on the basis of each and all the same would be foolish. One dog will be smart, easily handled and a litter brother or sister will be the most miserable, hard headed dog alive. One thrives on hard work and needs correcting continually the other will be ruined with one tenth the treatment necessary to make a dog of the tough fellow. 

All breeds likewise have a tendency to follow their own rules. The pointer as a rule is the easiest to develop of any of our different breeds of bird hunting dogs. They generally can be developed the first season, broken at a year and a half of age and will be all right through their career. (I am speaking of shooting dogs, the field trial dog must be developed according to his running ability and how it withstands the siege of training.) 

The breed as a rule is strong and has a world of bird sense tho' I am well aware that among them can be found as worthless dogs as any but that applies to any breed. 

The English Setter is next in the list and all the rest fall in from here. A man who wants an Irish Setter, German Pointer or any of these breeds can be prepared to spend quite a sum of money if he sends his dog to a trainer. I have yet to see one of these last named breeds, the English excluded, that I thought was at all comparable to a real good Pointer or English Setter. 

Beware of the $10 trainer as well as the $10 dog. You will get just what you pay for with any and the cheaper the price the cheaper the training your dog will receive. Take your dog to some good trainer and pay his price. He probably feeds good dog food and not rotten meat and corn meal. Dogs have to eat and that is no little expense. If he charges a good rate he has a reason. There is no money in the game and I have yet to hear of an ex-dog-trainer buying out the Northern Pacific Railway.

There are no two trainers in the country who train alike and yet many of them reach the same ultimate result. There is

no set method of training and every season there is something new to solve. A goodly proportion of common horse sense applied correctly is the best thing to do with any dog and don't try and break him over night. Take plenty of time but every time afield he should learn something. You need no clubs, tho' on some of the trainers a club would not be amiss. I generally use a light check cord on the shooting dogs; a good force collar is needed as there is always one or more who definitely does not and will not understand your language. The force collar on these fellows will be worth its weight in gold. Be very careful near the dog when on point or approaching game. This is where the bird shy dogs come from. A man rushes up to the dog when it is new to the pup-the pup wonders what the rush is and perhaps helps the handler to flush the birds. He is then as a rule brought back and punished or gets a flailing when caught. The next time he will flinch and run to one side and he is again punished, the next time he will be worse, etc.

It is so useless, this spoiling dogs, a bit of patience and careful handling is the thing. Go carefully to him on point, at least until after he is broken or nearly so, talk to him as you would a baby, stroke him along the back and gently push him forward, all the while talking low and soft to him. You will find he will resist the shove, they like to flush the birds themselves but don't want a bit of help. Do this a few times and you will have him holding his points. It will take a considerable length of time to get him to this stage but it is all necessary if you wish a dog broken as he should be. 

If you haven't the time or patience send him to a trainer and you will be miles ahead of trying to do the job yourself. If you have had no experience buy one of the many books on the market and follow the directions given. There are many short cuts from the rules given but it takes experience to recognize them. A good trainer can probably accomplish in a week what will take you a month to teach him. They know what to do, it is their business to know, and they will see things that an amateur never would recognize.

BREEDING, TRAINING AND CARE OF THE HUNTING DOG 

Mr. Vinton W. Mason uses the following methods in breeding, training, and caring for Pointers and Setters, the two breeds in which he is primarily interested. By reason of his long experience and national reputation in the handling of dogs, he speaks authoritatively upon the subject: 

I expect every bird hunter wants the best in dog, gun or ammunition. Of the latter I shall make no mention but will give a brief resume of my experience in breeding and developing 

Pointers and Setters. 

In June, 1891, I bred my first litter of bird dogs (Pointers).

Since that time I have bred thousands and during that long period, forty-seven years, I have had but one thought in mind to improve the breeds in which I am interested; namely, Pointers and Setters. Breeding bird dogs is purely a hobby with me and my only one. I have spent much time, thought and money to produce the best and if I can offer any helpful suggestions it will be a pleasure.

BREEDING 

Select for your foundation the best blood possible; dogs that are well known and have made records. This will be much to your advantage if you contemplate selling puppies and no less if you intend to develop them. A few simple rules which spell success. During pregnancy the bitch should be exercised regularly and special attention should be given to food. As soon as the bitch comes in season she should be wormed thoroughly.

Do not worm after she is bred. As soon as the bitch shows in whelp feed morning and night either raw or cooked meat with other food at night. Ten days before whelping give one large teaspoonful calcium phosphate with night feeding and continue until puppies are weaned. About the same time give cod liver oil (biologically tested) with night feed, one teaspoonful and increase to one dessert spoonful. For 24 hours after whelping feed nothing but milk. Thereafter milk should be fed regularly morning and night. I do not feed any canned or prepared foods in my kennel. I make my own food.

FEEDING AND CARE OF PUPPIES 

At the age of three weeks start feeding luke warm milk.

HUNTING DOGS 

Wet the tongue with milk and they will soon start licking the milk. At the age of four weeks get a package of corn flakes, borrow your wife's kneading board and rolling pin and reduce the flakes to powder. Soften with warm water and to each puppy give one teaspoonful to start with and increase. Feed puppies separately and give the same amount; this will insure an even lot of puppies. I expect more puppies die from worm infestation than any other cause. Therefore watch puppies closely. If the stomach shows bloated after feeding, trouble is brewing and worm immediately. If worms are present worm again in a week and thereafter every 30 days until puppies are one year old. 

The ordinary worm medicine should not be given puppies at that age. I have a formula for very young puppies and with the best results at the age of four months I use Vermifuge. Proper food and proper care will prevent rickets and will grow puppies with plenty of bone and muscle. At the age of five weeks I start with night feeding-lean, raw hamburger put through the cutter twice-just a taste to start with and increase daily. Change of menu should be made from time to time but milk should be given regularly. A clean place to sleep and plenty of room for exercise is important. Soft ground is preferable to hard. By starting early feeding (at the age of three weeks) you will have no weaning trouble and at the same time relieve the strain on the bitch.

FIELD DEVELOPMENT 

As soon as my puppies will follow me, two to three months of age, they are given daily field exercise. This develops muscle and acquaints them with cover. But if training is not given until they are a year old, let them develop their natural hunting instinct. Early prompting to me means much although some trainers disagree with me, but from experience I can say that ninety per cent of early hunting puppies make the best bird dogs.

Early prompting may be started in this manner: Get a pigeon, a ten-foot pole and a cord and you have the necessary equipment. 

Attach the cord to one end of the pole, the other to the pigeon. 

I use an inner tube (automobile), cut a section say three or four inches long; cut two holes for the feet and slip the pigeon into the tube, attach the cord to the tube on top. Move the pigeon along on the ground and if necessary lift it into the air but

don't let the puppies get hold of this bird. At the age of five or six months I start my puppies on young guinea hens or quail, both of which I keep for that purpose. I prefer young guineas; when about three months old they will take wing and fly ten to forty paces; wonderful experience for young dogs. On liberated birds I use a 20-foot cord on the puppies. When the point is established make them hold the point for a reasonable length of time; this insures staunchness. Never pull a puppy away from a point, always lift bodily and carry a reasonable distance. In flushing the bird, if possible, do not let the puppy see the direction of flight. Give the puppy due credit for his work and under no circumstances strike or punish a puppy. His confidence in you spells success. Early training will take away the class and style which we so much desire and which adds much pleasure to a day's shooting. Boldness and timidness precludes me from laying down any fast rule for field training. In conclusion let me say that cleanliness is next to Godliness in a kennel and patience is a virtue. 

Clean your yards morning and night, change bedding once a week, spray sleeping boxes with flit. Flies and flit do not mix. 

Dip or wash dogs in warm weather; use a weak fresh water at all times.

GENERAL RULES USED FOR WORKING TRAINED BIRD DOGS 

To make a dog charge or lie down-Give the dog the command, “CHARGE” or “LIE DOWN” and as you do so, raise the right hand up as though you intended slapping the dog on the top of the head. This is generally the way they are made to lie down or charge, especially if they have been broken under the force system. If he does not lie down and you wish to force him to do so, then take hold of his collar with the left hand and with the thumb of the right hand over his left kidney and the fingers of the right hand over the right kidney-then quick and hard pinch together and push down on the dogs back-this will break him down to the ground. When you begin to pinch and push down-be sure to give the command "CHARGE-DOWN." A few of these treatments as above described, if properly given, will make them all go down when told to CHARGE or DOWN.

To have the dog come to you when called-Simply say to him with his name, "come here" or "here Jack" or whatever you call the dog. If you are hunting with the dog and he is ranging and going through weeds, brush, or any thick cover so he himself makes so much noise he cannot hear your commands or the whistle-THEN-as all dogs' nature is to work back and forth across in front of you-YOU stop, stand still and watch for the dog and when the dog sees you turn your back to him and he will soon come in towards you. As soon as he is close enough to hear your commands call him to you and give such commands as you desire him to have.

How pups and dogs are made gun shy-Never under any circumstances turn a shot gun loose over a bird dog pup close up until you have tested the pup or the young dog to see if he shows signs of being afraid of the noise of the gun-pups are not born gun shy but are made so by the man who fires the first shot from a shot gun close over them. THE AMERICAN WATER SPANIEL 

Editor's Note: My good friend, Mr. Driscoll O. Scanlan, who is President of the American Water Spaniel Club, is perhaps the foremost breeder and authority in America on this newly recognized breed, and we are glad to present to our readers the following article from him with reference to this interesting breed of dogs: 

"AMERICA'S OWN" 

This medium sized brown curly Water Spaniel is progressing rapidly in North America. The American Water Spaniel is a very old breed, in fact it is claimed that Columbus brought them to America on his first trip and left a pair with the Indians, and the bloodlines of today trace directly back to this pair. Many of the old-timers can recall this wonderful Spaniel as they were numerous thirty-five years ago. They were then the all around shooting dog and the merry trick dog and companion of many school children and their parents.

However, for some unknown reason this breed was never recognized by any registering office until the United Kennel Club, Inc., recognized them several years ago because of their purity, their natural hunting instinct and because they were one of the greatest retrieving bloodlines known to man. Since the 

U. K. C. recognized them they have become very prominent throughout the country and many kennels have been estaolished. It may at first sound tame indeed to the average sportsman, the possibilities of this American Water Spaniel as a shooting dog, as one has but to look at a single issue of most any sporting paper to note the universal popularity of the English Pointer and English Setter. While conceding the nobility of these great bloodlines, I hope it will not give offense to invite attention to this most wonderful American Water Spaniel. Like the Indian a true American breed. "AMERICA'S OWN.”

This delightful worker, a "natural busy body," is now universally recognized and those of us who know and have worked with this companion feel that for all around usefulness, there are none his equal. This dog as a retriever is well-nigh perfect by nature. It will watch its handler with intelligent understanding, going like a flash at a signal to "Bring 'em in." These dogs will watch the hunter drop four or five birds, then hunt swiftly and merrily until every one is retrieved. Rabbits, chickens, grouse, quail, pheasants, ducks, all are brought in with unfailing

dispatch and tenderest care. I have known of an American 

Water Spaniel retrieving an egg without cracking the shellthough I must say that I could not recommend such an object for general use in the training of retrievers. This breed naturally swims almost like a seal, and few are the water-fowl that escape them. Their tail acts as a rudder and is a great aid in swimming and steering especially in a turbulent stream or where there is a current.

I do not want to devote all of this space to the retrieving qualities of the American Water Spaniel and would like to say a word particularly as to the general qualities of them as an all around shooting dog. The nose of this breed is faultless. They work the thicket, rough ground, or covert, depending on body smell for game locations. The tail, though "ALWAYS IN MO- 

TION," plays an unmistakable signal when game is scented.

The enthusiasm and thoroughness of their work are a joy and an inspiration to the hunter. The American Water Spaniels have a real, great recognizable desire to please their masters, consequently, they are easily taught. They quickly learn to drop to shot and wing, though sometimes their enthusiasm causes them to be over anxious. They do not point game, but spring it. 

They soon learn to work near the hunter, thirty yards or nearer, and try to work toward the gun. 

The American Water Spaniel is of deep chocolate color. 

The body hair is of short curls. The legs are comparatively short-haired. The tail is well covered. It does not have the “shave” nor “rat" tail, nor the "tuft" or "top-knot" and therefore is outstandingly different from the Irish Water Spaniel which is very different in size and shape also. The American 

Water Spaniel's forehead is covered with comparatively short hair. The head should be broad and well developed and the nose rather short and square. The ears are covered with short curly hair coming within an inch of the end of the nose. 

The average man desires a dog of somewhat all around proportions. The business man, the clerk, the lawyer, the doctor, or the farmer, cannot use a dog all of the time, thus keeping it under his own direction. In fact, it is only at intervals that the dog is hunting. In the interim between hunts, the wife and children must have some supervision of the dog. Here is a real point in favor of the American Water Spaniel. They love every mem-

ber of the family, and really try to fit agreeably into their environment. They are dependable watch dogs, giving prompt alarm at strange persons or stock. FOXHOUNDS 

The foxhunter is inherently democratic. He must be so, for when foxhunters assemble for the chase royal, all caste and social distinction for the time being is thrown aside, and each man is judged by his fellows solely upon his merits as a sportsman.

Thus, the so-called "foxhunting fraternity," by which term foxhunters like best to be known, is in reality nothing less than the brotherhood of universal fellowship brought about by the pursuit of the most democratic of all sports-foxhunting. 

The southern portion of the United States lays claim to the credit for introducing this grand sport into America. It is their proud boast that for a long, long time it was strictly a southern sport, having been introduced into this country from England about the year 1650, when the English colonists brought foxhounds to Virginia and Maryland. In England, a pack of foxhounds, consisting of from twenty-five to forty pairs, is maintained either by the neighborhood or else by rich individuals. 

In America, as a usual thing, each hunter owns his own pack of hounds.

Before the Civil War foxhunting in the South was much more popular than it is today, but this does not mean that the chase royal is still without numerous champions. Indeed, there are many more hunters now than then, due to increase in population, as well as to the World War, which taught men to live the simple outdoor life and gave them the incentive to seek their sports in the out-of-doors. Out of many of these boys of yesteryear came the sportsmen of today. Prior to the Civil War, the hounds in use were the old black and tan descendants of the ancient Talbot hounds, whose pedigreed record may be traced back into the dimmed pages of history for more than two thousand years. These hounds had the facility to track a cold trail with perfect ease, and, possessing enormous muzzles, they bellowed forth a voluminous cry when in pursuit of their quarry. 

Again, they possessed remarkable endurance and staying qualities, two requisites which must be present in the make-up of the ideal foxhound. These dogs, we are told, were accustomed to chases of great length after the fleet gray fox, a fox found com-

monly throughout the South. In Maryland, the English foxhound was crossed with the Irish stag hound, producing hounds of greater speed and more excellent quality than those in use up to that time. This cross was made because of the difficult conditions in that State, a dog being desired that would make a catch in quicker time and not require six or eight hours as did the Talbot descendants. Thus the first step in the improvement of the American foxhound took place, and subsequent crosses and improvements have produced the greatest foxhound the world has ever known.

In the year 1738, the English colonists became dissatisfied with the common gray fox, so they imported the red fox from the mother country-England-liberating it along the shores of Chesapeake Bay. The red fox has a habit of making great circles when being pursued, while the gray fox seeks to elude capture by dodges and doubles.

Now there were plenty of gray foxes in Kentucky then as now, and McQuerry knew a gray fox track when he saw one. But he was puzzled that morning as he trudged through a foot or more of snow, accompanied by his faithful sheep dog. For plainly stenciled in the snow were the tracks of a fox, yet not a fox. 

Yes, there is a slight difference in the tracks of a red and a gray fox. So McQuerry and his dog set out to run down this strange animal, and followed the trail to a hollow log. McQuerry stationed the dog at one end of the log while he rammed a stick through the other end, and out jumped as fine a specimen of red fox as ever one could hope to see. McQuerry was paralyzed for the moment, but not so the dog, which made a leap and landed on the strange animal, crushing it into the snow. 

Scampered Over From Virginia 

McQuerry rescued the fox before it had been torn to pieces by the sheep dog and carried it to the home of his friend, John W. Walker, an extensive landowner and famous foxhunter, whose name has come down through the ages as one of the originators of the celebrated Walker breed of foxhounds. 

This red fox had strayed a long piece out of his bailiwick, and had scampered over the mountains from Virginia, where these red runners had been known for generations, but there's no way of telling what made him want to emigrate.

Before the advent of the English colonists, legend has it, there were no red foxes in America. But those Cavaliers, even in the early days, had to have their English sport; so, along with their horses and hounds, they brought over a few red fox pairs from home and liberated them in the tidewater region of Virginia, where they multiplied and spread up and down the Atlantic Coast. But the mountains checked their ramblings toward the West.

That was a great night for McQuerry and Walker, who lost no time in organizing a hunt for that very night. They called in their hunting organizations with their hounds and liberated the red stranger, giving him a good start. The chase began. But it was a disappointing affair, because when that big sheep dog had pounced on the red fox it was injured; and it didn't take the hounds long to run the creature down.

Up to that time Kentucky hounds were just plain varmint dogs, descendants of hounds brought West by the pioneers to help track down deer. There was nothing artistocratic about their lineage, although they had their good points and served a useful purpose.

It wasn't long after Marth came to Madison County that she became a mother. In the meantime old Rifler was crossed on the native hounds. One of Marth's offspring was a beautiful gyp, but she suffered a foot injury and was unable to hunt. 

However, she became famous as the "Mash Foot Bitch," the dam of many litters sired by native hounds. 

That was the beginning of the Walker breed of hounds, although for many years it was known as the Walker-Maupin breed, taking the names of two old foxhunting cronies and famous figures in the sport, Gen. George Washington Maupin of 

Madison County and John W. Walker of Garrard. General

Maupin was a colorful figure who went down in foxhunting tradition as “Uncle Wash," a wealthy planter as well as a trader in Negro slaves. He possessed a tract of more than 1,000 acres in 

Lincoln County which he used as a private hunting preserve. 

The Maupin and Walker families still are foxhunters and breeders of blooded foxhounds. Especially are the Walkers still active in the breeding industry, always striving to improve the breed that bears their name. From the first John W. Walker, who was born in 1802 and died in 1885, down to little Ed Walker, Jr., of Paint Lick, whose father is secretary of the Kentucky State Foxhunters' Association, there have been five generations of that family identified with Walker hounds.

The stud book of the Walker family, in the possession of Woods Walker in Garrard County, dates back to 1867, and

. the first entry is a hound named Spotted Top, whose sire was couchman and whose dam was Aggie. You can almost hear the ghostly baying of those long-dead hounds as you sit on the cool front porch of Woods Walker's fine old Colonial mansion and pore over the pages of that yellowed volume. 

While the Walkers and the Maupins were developing the Walker breed in Central Kentucky, another breed, known as the Triggs, was being developed in Southern Kentucky by Col. 

Hayden C. Trigg of Glasgow, in the sixties. Colonel Trigg was one of the most celebrated foxhunters of his time. When he was 

18 he was stricken with tuberculosis, and it was a foregone conclusion that he had not long to live. But he took up foxhunting, remained out in the open during all kinds of weather, defied the doctors and got well. 

Trigg Hounds Have Bit of Brogue 

Colonel Trigg originated the breed that bears his name, and is just as popular among many sportsmen as the Walkers, by crossing descendants of Imported Rifler on a breed that came from Georgia known as Birdsongs, or Julys. He also mixed in some Irish hounds with them, possibly to get more brogue in their voices. 

If you go to a Kentucky or National foxhound field trial you will hear the hunters talk about Walkers, Triggs, Birdsongs and 

Julys. They also might speak always slurringly, of "potlickers." 

Now those Birdsongs and Julys are the same thing. The name 

Birdsong is taken from George L. F. Birdsong of Thomaston,

Ga., who, in 1861, developed a breed of hounds on a cross by a famous hound from Maryland named July. 

But a "potlicker," poor soul, is a canine social outcast and is not eligible for field trials. Don't breathe this to a soul, but he can't trace his ancestry back to any of the real foundation stock. Just another varmint dog masquerading as a foxhound.

HUNTING DOGS BREEDING AND CARE OF PUPPIES 

Following are the methods employed by my good friend Stanley Blake, a Kentucky dog breeder of many years' experience, who knows as much about dogs as any man in America: 

First: Select the very best bitches you have; never breed a timid or gun-shy bitch; never breed a bitch the first time she comes in season, and be sure she has had actual field work for whatever game she is adapted; also be sure the dog you breed to is as well bred as the bitch, and he is an actual field worker of the kind of work he is adapted for. If possible he should not be less than three years old-so much the better if he is five or even six-then you will not be bothered with puny, shy or unhealthy puppies. While in whelp feed your bitch plenty of good nutritious food, give her daily exercise; if possible, work her on game until the day she is due to whelp. This, through hereditary nature, will make the puppies natural workers. Select a good, clean, quiet place, well ventilated for her to whelp in, and allow no other dogs or strangers to disturb her for at least three weeks. 

Never feed grown dogs and puppies together; start to feed puppies at three weeks old, buttermilk twice a day, morning and evening; this will clean their stomachs of worms. Leave the mother with them until they are at least five weeks old; then, for her own health, as well as the puppies, allow her to stay with them for one hour a day for one more week, then feed twice a day, buttermilk or beef broth with bread crumbs (corn bread best), little strips of raw lean meat is good twice a week but very little until they are past four months old, at which time they will get their second teeth and can masticate more substantial food and will grow strong. Avoid sweet stuff, fresh milk or meat, as it has a tendency to produce worms. 

Feeding, Kennel Care and Local Disease Treatment 

Be sure and see that your kennels are well ventilated; if possible have running water, at any rate a goodly supply of fresh water at all times. Disinfect thoroughly at least once a week. 

If nature does not provide it, have a good artificial shade and a perfectly dry kennel in damp or cool weather. Feed grown dogs not more than once a day, corn bread and table scraps as a mixed diet. Best of all, corn meal and middlings with well-cooked

meat and soup baked to a pone or mush. Dogs will eat this substance readily and it keeps them in perfect health. Exercise or work on game, if possible, at least once a day. To prevent fleas, and for mange and for all forms of skin diseases use one part creolene to 100 parts of water, and dip dog, head and all. 

For cankered ear, saturated solution of boracic acid. Massage or rub thoroughly. For distemper, allow the dog his liberty and give him all the raw eggs and fresh milk he will eat. Give him a good dose of castor oil about twice a week.

Correcting Faults of a Dog 

If your dog is too friendly with strangers take a friend with you and instruct him to step on the front feet of your dog every time he comes near him; this will soon break him of his friendly habit and he will fairly snarl at a stranger's approach. If he puts his front paws on you in friendly affection, press your foot on his hind toes enough to cause him some pain, and he will soon learn he must not muddy up one's clothes. In breaking a dog from running rabbits, or other objectionable faults, never kick or cuff him on the head; this often ruins good dogs by causing canker heads or ears, or an inward hemorrhage. Never call him to you and whip him-he will think you are whipping him for minding you, but go to him, throw a stick to attract his attention, and catch him around the nose, taking both jaws in your hand; this will keep him from snapping at you or howling, and will produce the desired effect a great deal better than a cuff or a kick; take a good strong switch, and thrash him soundly and plenty.

Handling a Dog in a Strange Country 

On receiving the dog take him out of the crate as soon as possible, give him water-not too much at once, but a little every few minutes until he is satisfied; give him a tablespoonful of castor oil, exercise him, be kind to him, and let him know that you are his friend and not going to hurt him, as he is often half scared to death in transit, for he don't know what it all means. 

He misses his kennel mates, his master's voice and his home surroundings. Do not have a fixed sentiment in your mind that you do not like him or that he is not going to make good, for he can read the aura in your expression that is not visible to a human. Don't think because he makes up with you that he is

No matter how well he does the first few times out he will improve each time, as a month or even longer is none too long for a good sensible dog to become reconciled to his new master's home and surroundings. Feed him a little meat, corn bread or table scraps, a mixed diet, and gain his confidence by kind treatment, and he will do anything for you, and reward your patience with his best efforts.

THE COON HOUND 

In a certain sense, and a very important one, at least to that big army of outdoorsmen who love to hear their dogs give tongue on a wily old coon's trail, the coon hound is a distinct breed, for it will chase nothing but coon. Provided, of course, the dog has been properly trained. Of course, a coon hound may chase an o'possum, but this little fellow is so much like the coon, we judge the scent is almost identical. And some coon hounds will chase any kind of varmint, but they are usually called varmint dogs. But a coon hound proper will chase nothing but coon or o'possum, and we are speaking only of small game, for here again there is an exception. A coon hound will chase a bear, be he ever so well trained on coon, for, again, the scent is identical, or practically so.

There is a wide divergence of opinion as to what kind of dog makes the best coon dog. Some folks like an ordinary hound trained on coon, others prefer the old-fashioned long-eared, deep-throated Southern dog for the job, while still others like a mixed pack-part still-trailers, such as the Airedale terrier. 

As is the case in fox chasing, the methods of hunting coons vary in different sections of the country. In the North, most hunters hunt the coon by day and with a shotgun, with which the coon is shot from the tree the minute the dogs tree it. In the South, the usual procedure is to coon hunt by night, the coon being a nocturnal animal, with a good pack of hounds, or hounds and airedales mixed, and of course with a good lantern and an axe as necessary parts of the equipment. The lantern is used to "shine" the coon's eyes. The light will attract his attention and when he turns his face toward the hunter his eyes will appear in the darkness like balls of fire. This is a positive method of finding out promptly if the coon is in the tree, which has already been indicated by the dogs baying at its base. The axe, of course, is used to hew the tree down if all other means fail to dislodge the coon. It is, incidentally, probably used too frequently on other people's property to enhance the popularity of coon hunters.

Sometimes, rather than cut it down, one of the more daring hunters will climb the tree, and quite infrequently he comes

HUNTING DOGS 

back down faster than he went up, for when a coon is cornered he can do all sorts of straight and fancy clawing and biting. 

And when at last the coon is ejected by one means or another, the waiting dogs find in him a worthy adversary as soon as he hits the ground, even though he be, as he usually is, far outnumbered.

The fox is famed in song and story for his cunning, but let us not forget that his ringtailed majesty the coon is noted for his sagacity likewise, and many are the tricks he plays upon an unsuspecting pack of coon hounds.

He will double back on his trail like a veteran fox, he will travel through water to throw the dogs off the track, and if at at long last he is brought to bay with all avenues of escape cut off, even though he be greatly outnumbered, the dogs will find in him a worthy antagonist.

We salute that hardy band of good fellows known as coon hunters, and hats off to this gallant little animal we call the 'coon, and may his tribe increase!

RABBIT HOUNDS 

The ideal rabbit dog is a foxhound trained on rabbits. Thus you get a dog speedy enough to bring the rabbit around to you (they nearly always run in a circle when pursued by hounds) and this enables you to get a good shot at the fleet-footed target. 

Not only do you get speed in this kind of dog, but you get a dog with a head full of sense, which, by heredity, the average foxhound possesses.

Some like the Airedale terrier, trained for this work with more or less success, but for us, we'll take a foxhound trained on this game every time, unless, of course, you offer us a good, well trained, beagle hound, which are also dandy for rabbit chasing. And while some prefer one breed for rabbit dogs and some another, the main thing to consider is the individual dog and his individual excellence, regardless of breed. There are good, bad, and indifferent dogs in all breeds, and here again is where expert handling shows up to real advantage. Everything else being equal, the foxhound would probably give more all around satisfaction, but very often you will find an individual beagle, or possibly even an individual airedale terrier, which would give better satisfaction than some foxhounds. It all reverts to how the dogs are trained, given a fairly good dog to start with.

The rabbit is one of our principal game animals, probably more people enjoying this form of sport than all other kinds of hunting combined. This is due to the universal distribution of the rabbit and the fact that its meat forms a very important part of the nation's food supply, further to the fact that there is real enjoyment in the sport of rabbit hunting because Br'er Rabbit furnishes the hunter with a target which, when in motion, takes a quick eye and a fast trigger finger to hit.

The rabbit readily adapts himself to local conditions, hence has been able to survive even though hard hunted each year. 

Wherever natural shelter is absent in the form of a thin growth of vegetation or wherever brushy bushes are lacking, the rabbit usually digs himself in and lives in burrows in the ground. 

Sometimes they utilize deserted badger or Prairie-dog dens, but frequently they dig for themselves under rocks and other ob-

jects. Ofttimes they also make their home in the crevices of rock walls and rock fences. In fact, upon occasion they will make their nests most anywhere a likely spot may be found.

Again, that old time-honored adage applies, Necessity is the mother of invention. Increased cultivation of farm lands is the most serious stumbling-block to their rapid increase, but, in spite of all this, they somehow manage to hold their own. In fact, in quite a few localities they have become so numerous as to be termed a nuisance by a certain class of folks, who, while wellmeaning, think more in terms of dollars and cents than in terms of wild life and its propagation on their lands. These misguided individuals lose sight of the fact that, while the rabbit may destroy a small amount of garden stuff, the average annual supply of rabbit meat amounts to many thousands of dollars, or even millions, while they do, comparatively speaking, only small damage. Certainly they do not deserve utter extermination, as some would have.

The cottontail forms a target worthy the quick eye of a trained gunner, as it bobs up and down, leap by leap, and moving swift as a fleeting shadow. As a game animal the cottontail furnishes some of the most enjoyable and interesting sport to be had by American hunters. The zigzag, headlong rush of a rabbit for the nearest cover expresses the quick energy with which this animal is endowed, exciting a pleasant thrill in the onlooker. Rabbits are also the victim of trappers in large numbers annually, a vocation indulged in by many persons who do not stop to consider that such methods often prolongs the suffering of the victims, but here again enters the human, or should we say inhuman, equation. It boils down to the fact that while some don't believe in trapping, others do, and there you have it.

THE MERRY BEAGLE HOUND 

Specially prepared for us by ERWIN A. Kopp, of Masterly Beagles. 

What is greater sport than to own and run a brace or pack of merry little Beagles, defined in the standard as the "miniature foxhound," and to hie away to the edge of any hamlet to a likely rabbit patch, for isn't the lowly rabbit found in sufficient numbers almost anywhere and everywhere to furnish us sportsmen, who really love hound work, with the sport of kings; to hear that music of all music which only the little beagle can make while trailing and driving its quarry, the wily rabbit, who is doing his uncanny utmost to throw off the pack, dodging from bush to bush, doubling back on his trail, and cutting figure eights to evade that fiery pack of furious little demons? Isn't that harmony? Isn't it living the life of a Riley to sit on a stump or rail fence and let those merry little Beagle hounds furnish us with such sport, enjoying the work they are doing as much as they enjoy running their rabbit? You who have tasted of this supreme ecstasy, you alone know the real living and the thrill of the greatest of sports, the hounds to the chase! The old proverb, Once a beagler, always a beagler, still sticks! 

I am interested primarily in helping the novice, for well I know from experience that any dog breeder's path is far from smooth, and that many setbacks are in store for any beginner aspiring to become a successful breeder. For that reason, I suggest that the prospective breeder begin in a small way. Don't buy the cheapest dog you can get. Purchase one or two of the youngest puppies available from the choicest stock of a reliable breeder.

Contrary to general belief, you do not need a good old hound to train pups. When your puppy is from four to six months old, you can begin taking him afield where you know there are rabbits. Move around slowly through the brush and let your puppy investigate for himself. Then watch for a rabbit in his form or nest. When you see one, get your pup, hold him up in your arm, and grasp the skin under his neck to keep his muzzle pointed towards the rabbit so that he will be sure to see the quarry when it emerges. Then walk up toward it slowly and cautiously. Usually one can approach to within a few feet

HUNTING DOGS 

of the rabbit. As soon as Mr. Bunny makes his exit, the pup will undoubtedly signify that he has seen him by lifting his ears, coming to attention, or making a start. Set him down quickly and let him take after Mr. Rabbit on a sight chase. Follow the pup for a short way the first few times. When the rabbit is out of sight, the pup may or may not put his nose down and look for him the first time. However, he surely will the second or third time you try him. A few lessons like this, and he will get busy and commence to look for the rabbit in that likely looking cover you found for him his last trip afield. When you notice that his hunting instinct is developing, in other words, when you know that he is looking for something and has become interested in game, move around slowly through the brush and let him investigate for himself, thereby encouraging him to search for and jump his own game. Keep watching the rabbit, however, so that if he loses the trail you can help him find it again. 

Once he finds out that there are rabbits squatting around in any likely patch, he will get busy the minute you hit the brush. After that, it is only a matter of taking him afield often enough to keep his interest in game alive. Of course, his breeding and the way in which you go about training him will have much bearing on the outcome.

The above training can also be applied to an older, inexperienced or untrained dog, but it is much easier to train him while he is still a pup, as he adjusts himself to things more quickly and also learns to hunt more easily while he is still a youngster. 

To get your puppy accustomed to shooting, carry a .22 rifle with you on these trips afield. Try to shoot a sparrow or gopher or anything that is game. If your training occurs during the open season and you can shoot a rabbit, so much the better. 

Shoot only once, or at least no more than necessary to make the kill. Let your pup see the game drop if possible, and let him go and get it if he so desires. Pet him a little and encourage him in low tones. Then let him play with the game, maul it, or, better still, open it and let him lick the blood and feed him the liver and the heart if he will eat them. (This undoubtedly is contrary to the belief of many gunners who pride themselves on the fact that their hound won't touch or eat a rabbit. However, that can very easily be taught your pup later.) Never,

under any condition, shoot just to make noise or to see how your pup is going to react to it. Shoot only at game, and don't proceed too fast with these lessons. One or two lessons the first time is sufficient. After he learns to like the .22 rifle and knows what it's for, take a .410 bore shotgun and go through the same lessons carefully, and thence to the 20 gauge and so on until your regular bore gun is reached. I advocate that these lessons be commenced in the spring or summer whenever possible, for, in that way, by the time you have your pup broken on rabbits in the fall, you also have him fully broken to gun. 

I know from contact with the fancy over a period of years that many nervous dogs (by the way, keep as far away from that kind as possible) and a few not nervous ones are spoiled every season by trainers who are either ignorant about how to train their dog or who are over-enthusiastic and who attempt to cram too much into a pup at one time, or by the other fellow who buys his pup in the spring, keeps him chained up all summer, and then, when the hunting season opens, grabs his pup and his gun and takes with him a gang of his buddies and proceeds to shoot rabbits over the pup, who does not know what that ungodly noise and blasting is all about. Naturally, he cringes, crawls away and hides, or follows his master's heels, wondering what is in store for him next, and is thus possibly spoiled for gunning for all time.

The gunshy dog, which is usually the dog that has been spoiled in one of the above manners, can be cured if handled correctly and with patience, but space will not permit me to go into that problem in this volume. 

Running fits, or even convulsions in some forms, have caused trouble and worry for many novices. To remedy this, first of all be sure your dog is free of worms. I would advise you to use several different kinds of wormers to get every kind of worm. 

We use two kinds-one which gets the roundworms and hookworms, and one which gets the tapeworms and roundworms. 

After you have your dog free of worms, don't worm him oftener than once every sixty or ninety days. Do not worm bitches heavy in whelp nor the hunting or field trial dog in running condition. A worming at that important time may throw him off form. To assure against future running fits, keep all starchy foods away from the dog for a while. Put him on a strictly raw

hamburger diet of one good meal per day, and the fits will disappear as mist before the morning sun. Gradually, you can get him off the meat diet and onto a balanced food diet again, but don't ever omit the meat entirely. 

Most novices, and especially city dog owners, feed their beagles altogether too much. Feed only as much as a dog will clean up readily. Don't let any food stand in front of the dog. 

Our puppies do best on two meals per day, our matured dogs, not nursing pups, are fed only once a day, and a matron, nursing her litter, is fed twice a day. Our whole pack is fed a nationally advertised food which is sold in three forms-puppy meal, matured meal, and pellets. 

See that your matron has a dry, warm, clean box of suitable size for her coming litter. The bottom must be clean, and, if of wood, must be of very smooth lumber. A paper or cardboard bottom is fine, as it can be removed when soiled and a clean one substituted. The bitch will usually scratch all bedding to the sides of the box, to prevent her lying upon one of her litter. 

Thus, the puppies must rub their bellies along the floor constantly in getting to their mother, causing an irritation of the navel, which is often disastrous. Watch the pups' navels the first several weeks, and, if they become infected, move the pups to a clean nest box with a smooth bottom and apply dry boric acid powder, iodine or both to navels daily until healed. 

I do not approve of an entire meat diet, especially for the breeder who has broods and raises pups. A meat diet causes the puppies to grow too large and the bitch gets too fat, so the heavy meat feeder always has more than his share of whelping troubles. 

Feed your bitch only one good meal per day while in whelp, consisting of some balanced, tried and proven, commercial food or all kinds of table scraps if you must, but never put her on a meat diet or you are surely heading for trouble. However, if your bitches have free range on a farm during their entire period, you might get by with a minimum amount of whelping trouble on an all meat diet, providing you don't feed too heavy, as your bitch will pick up many of the vitamins of which her all meat diet is deficient. Exercise is also an important factor while your bitch is carrying pups.

The hardest work for the dog breeder, and the least remunerative, is the working and conditioning of field trial

hounds. I will not go into detail along that line, but will let it suffice to say that for a field trial dog you will need, first of all, a crack, faultless, hound. In other words, he must be a topnotcher in every sense of the word. Then you will have to work him all summer and fall to give him all the experience possible and make a real rabbit-wise hound out of him. If he is five or six years old, so much the better. He will undoubtedly have all his powers fully developed by that time. The trials are a great thing and have helped greatly in putting the beagle where he ranks today as one of the leading sporting dogs in the world!

HUNTING DOGS KENNEL MANAGEMENT* 

The first point in successful kennel management is the kennel itself. The fence, of course, must be so constructed, either of pickets, boards, or wire netting reinforced by suitable material, built sufficiently high to render escape of the dogs impossible. 

The space within the kennel should be as commodious as conditions will permit, so as to allow room for them to romp and play, which exercise will be conducive to health. The enclosed ground should be gravelled, or sanded. The use of tanbark for a ground covering is also desirable in many cases. 

Along the side or end of the kennel a shed should be erected, closed at the back and ends, with a shingle roof, at least ten feet deep, which will afford complete shelter from rain, snow, and the hot sunshine. The shed, built open in front, should be floored with plank, moderately inclined, so as to allow any moisture to drain off. The shed should open toward the south. Along the back of the shed there should be arranged a row of ordinary dog-houses of pine boarding, one for each dog.

These houses should have no bottoms, in order that they may be removed, and the boards scoured beneath from time to time. 

These should not be built less than three feet in length, by two in breadth, and two feet high at the eaves, by three at the ridge. 

The aperture should be only large enough to permit the entrance and exit of the dog. The inside should be whitewashed, and the very best bed that can be devised is fine pine shavings, being soft and warm, and the turpentine in the shavings will act to prevent the presence of vermin.

If you live in town and do not have the space for such commodious quarters for your dogs as outlined above, or if you only have one or two dogs, movable dog-houses, such as described, placed on a plank platform, and having a post annexed, to which the dog may be chained, will answer your purpose, with the provision that you exercise the dog at regular intervals.

The kennel, whether large or small, whether for many dogs, or one or more, cannot be kept too clean. 

If, as in most cases, it is not practicable to have a channel of running water through the kennel, each dog should be provided at all times with his own large earthen pan of water, which

should be kept continually fresh. Frank Forester also suggests that at all seasons there should be in each pan of water a piece of bar brimstone, especially in summer. This acts as an alterative and gentle purgative, which keeps the bowels in good condition, cools the blood, and preserves the softness of the coat. 

It is a good plan to feed all the dogs at once, from one long common trough, into which the food has been poured, and the dogs then called out one by one, by name, from their houses, to which they should previously be compelled to retire, and none permitted to approach the feed trough until invited. It is well to stand over the dogs while they are feeding, with a switch, and at the first growl or snarl the offender should be administered a sharp cut. If any individual dog has a marked tendency to act the glutton, it may be necessary to feed him separately in order to insure the others getting their proportionate shares and in order, moreover, to preserve the peace of the dog colony. 

Separate feeding should, however, be resorted to only as a last means of promoting harmony. In many cases, a gluttonous dog can be controlled by the spoken word, calling him off quietly but firmly and ordered to his kennel. When he has missed out on a meal or two, he will, in most cases, show a new-found respect for his companions.

THE PROPER FOOD FOR DOGS 

The age old question, "What is the best food for dogs?" is one which will permit of argument, but the writer holds that inasmuch as the dog is neither wholly carniverous nor wholly herbivorous, his diet should consist of both vegetable and animal matter. 

Mr. Blaine, in his work on Canine Pathology, states that the dog is so constituted as to be able to take in vegetable matter as well as animal matter and formed to receive nourishment from either. He is furnished with sharp cutting teeth for tearing flesh, and he has also tolerably broad surfaces on other of his teeth, capable of grinding farinaceous substances. His stomach and intestines likewise hold a middle place between those of the carnivorous and herbivorous tribes of animals. 

Says Mr. Blaine: "He (the dog) is by nature predacious, and intended to live on other animals; the stronger he hunts in troops, the weaker he conquers singly. Yet still it is clear that his

HUNTING DOGS 

organs fit him, when necessary, for receiving nutriment from vegetable matter also, and we likewise see that he voluntarily seeks it, probably as a necessary mixture, to prevent that tendency to putridity, which too great a quantity of animal food begets. It is a received opinion among many sportsmen, that flesh-feeding injures the scent; but it cannot do it naturally; for the fox, one of the caninae, which is known to be by choice wholly carnivorous, principally lives by the exquisite sensibility of his olfactory organs. If the eating of flesh really has such an effect on sporting dogs, it can only do so, when it has been taken in such quantities as to vitiate the secretions of the body; and in this way the pituitary mucous secretion of the nasal sinuses may themselves become somewhat tainted. "A mixture of animal and vegetable substances is therefore the most proper general food for dogs, and that which best agrees with the analogies of their nature; but the proportions of each are best determined by the exertions of the body. "I do not," Mr. Blaine continues, "lay stress upon the argument drawn from the natural state of the caninae, or from the habits of the fox, since it is too notorious to require comment, that animals in a high state of cultivation not only acquire new wants as well as new habits, but lose many of their natural qualities and instincts. And I am thoroughly convinced, that the habitual feeding of dogs on flesh has a tendency to injure their powers of scenting; and I believe the reason why it does so, is precisely because it does vitiate the secretions of the body generally, and therefore does affect the nasal sinuses." 

Forester observed that he had many times noticed that dogs fed largely, even on perfectly fresh animal food, particularly in a raw state, became intolerably offensive in odor, as the writer has noticed in his dogs; and when this is the case it is certain that the secretions of the body are vitiated, and probable, moreover, that the health of the animal is in some way affected likewise. 

Flesh is beyond doubt essential to dogs. It is evident to us that, during the off seasons of the year when there is no field work to be done, except exercise, flesh may be given without detriment, and with real advantage. Raw meat gives a dog both strength and ferocity. The latter is so closely allied to endurance, and what is commonly called pluck, that the one quality can hardly be encouraged apart from the others.

It is okeh, then, to feed your dogs on flesh, while conditioning them. Even raw horse-flesh is used for that purpose by my friend, Stanley Blake, and many other breeders. It is well, however, to put a sufficiency of sulphur in their drinking water to keep their bodies open.

There are a number of prepared dog foods on the market, which contain meat and cereal, some of them very excellent foods and high in nutritional values, while some others that are found on the market are low in nutritional values and should not be used. You are urged to give close attention to the label, with particular attention to food content, found on prepared dog foods. By comparing various labels, you will be able to discern without difficulty those foods which will give the most nutriment to your dogs and which, therefore, are the most economical for you to buy. DIET FOR PUPPIES 

When a diet that the puppy enjoys and thrives on is once established, do not experiment except to add a little variety. 

To do this, as he grows, make the milk richer and stronger by a raw egg, crumbled toast of white, or whole wheat bread, shredded wheat, cornflakes, rice crispies, boiled rice or barley or rice pudding.

If milk is vomited, rest the stomach by missing a meal. Adding a raw egg or a crushed graham cracker will settle the stomach and overcome the condition. 

Meat and chicken broths or skim milk alone are poor nourishment for a growing puppy. They should be thickened with either cooked rice or barley. Rice pudding makes a fine puppy food, and sufficient may be cooked for three days' ration and kept refrigerated. It is wise to teach the puppy to eat chopped up raw greens or grass. Bitch's milk is twice as rich in butter fat as cow's milk, and when using cow's milk one-third to one-half teaspoonful of butter should be added to 8 ounces or one teacupful of warm milk. FEEDING NOTES FOR GROWN DOGS 

There are no hard and set rules governing the quantity of food to give a grown dog. It is a matter of watchfulness on the part of the owner in not giving too much. Better always have your dog a little hungry than to have him overfed. 

Meat soups make an excellent food and add variety to the diet. Stale bread is another means of altering soupy food. Wellboiled rice can be mixed in soup or gravy. 

Do not feed chicken leg bones or meat bones with sharp and irregular points. A smooth knuckle or a rib bone is excellent for your dog and he should have one every week or oftener. 

This induces a dog to exercise his salivary glands and teeth. It is an aid to proper digestion, sound teeth and gums. 

Even for active outdoor dogs, one big meal daily is enough. 

When exercise is not given or the dog is confined indoors, one meal a day is ample, preferably in the evening.

POISONS AND ANTIDOTES 

Dogs are most frequently poisoned by Strychnine, either by intention or accidentally. Many chocolate coated laxative tablets for human use contain Strychnine (1/60 grain). The bottle is often upset or a single tablet drops on the floor.

Poisons, less frequent, appear in the order named: Phosphorus (Rat and Roach), Barium Carbonate, Arsenic (Rat 

Poison), Bichloride of Mercury, Lead in paint, and Various Poison Compounds, used in household insect sprays.

Ground glass is not a poison for dogs. This has been proven by the U. S. Chemical Warfare Service. 

First Aid Treatment 

In any suspected poisoning, vomit the animal by placing from one-half to a tablespoonful of plain table salt on the back of the tongue, and wash down with two or three ounces of water,

32 or all the water the animal will drink. 

Mustard water may be used, giving one teaspoonful of mustard in one-third of a teacup of very warm water. Repeat in ten minutes if dog does not vomit. 

After vomiting has ceased, give either laudanum or paregoric in one-half teaspoonful to one tablespoonful doses according to the pain and size of the animal. 

To repair the damage which poisons may cause the stomach and intestines, give either of the following: Milk, raw eggs, large doses of melted butter, lard, Nujol or Mineral Oil, according to the size of the dog, using from one to four tablespoonfuls or more.

THE NEW PUPPY* 

Before bringing the new puppy into your home, make preparations for its arrival. It should be brought into your household as quietly as possible. He is seeing strange faces; hearing strange voices; being handled by strange hands. Let those hands be gentle; those voices kind, as the first twenty-four hours with your puppy are very important.

TRAINING Patience in training is essential and should only be undertaken by one person.

To be a credit to himself, to you, and to the neighborhooda dog should know four things. When you have taught him the first command thoroughly, it is easy to teach him to respond to many other commands.

To come when called: After you and your pup have become thoroughly acquainted, put a training leash on him, or attach a string about 5 or 6 feet long to his collar. Play with him with the other end of the leash, then call softly, "Here, Jack," pulling the leash or cord very gently. When he gets the idea, say "Good 

Boy," or whatever his name is, or "Smart Boy, Jack". His delight is to please! You have only to show him.

When, with the aid of the leash, he has learned this, try him without the leash. The object of all leash-training is to teach the puppy to do the same thing without the leash. 

To lie down: Firmly press hind quarters down, and gently pull the front legs forward, saying softly "Lie down, Jack," and keep repeating this until the puppy gets the idea. Then praise. 

To heel: A dog pulling on the leash is an indication of lack of training. Take the puppy's leash up short enough so that only the tips of the front feet touch the floor, street or ground. 

Hold a stick in front of the chest to bar his progress, as you say "Heel, Jack", until he gets the idea. When thoroughly taught

SIGHTING TELESCOPES 

My friend, W. R. Weaver, supplies the following information in regard to the use of sighting telescopes in rifle shooting: 

The use of sighting telescopes in rifle shooting, both for target and game, has increased more during the past five years than for any other period since the first scope sight was made. 

Naturally price has had a great deal to do with this increased popularity of the rifle scopes, but not all. Manufacturers found that they could produce an efficient scope, within the pocket book reach of all men who shoot, but price alone will not sell a commodity.

The real increase in the use of the telescope sight has come from the desire of the rifleman for a more efficient sight. When the merits of the scope sight are considered it is small wonder that it is now universally accepted. The chief advantages, since the scope has become so popular, are common knowledge. Magnification is one factor but this is secondary to several others. 

With a good quality scope extremely fast shooting is possible because the eye can be off center an eighth inch or more whereas with metallic sights the eye must be in the exact sighting line. 

Then too, with the scope there is only one sight, not two, to align. This sight as well as the target is perfectly clear cut and in focus. Blurring of the sights is one of the greatest disadvantages of metallic sights. The light gathering qualities of a good scope promote accurate aim in poor light where iron sights would be useless. 

You can well recall the evolution of the iron sights. First we had the old-fashion open sights, or as some refer to them, the V sight. They were used for a century and believed to be good, and then someone invented the peep sight, and it was found to be better.

Many hunters were slow to adopt this newer sight, and even today most guns are factory equipped with open sights. That is because of the popular demand that's almost a heritage. Very few hunters ever shot a peep sight first. They received their earliest training with open sights, and accepted them as efficient enough for all practical purposes.

Many gun shooters today still cling to this theory, despite

HUNTING DOGS 

the thousands of columns that have been written by gun experts, expounding the advantages of the peep sight. 

From all indications, however, the glass sight has not suffered the long wait before its general acceptance as had the peep sight. Present day gun users more readily appreciate the advantage of increased sighting power. 

Probably the first to feel the keen necessity of increased power in their sighting were those whose eyes were beginning to dim with years. Then came the hunter, especially those along the eastern seaboard, who liked to shoot woodchucks. These cunning little fellows seemed to be aware of the distance over which a man could successfully sight a gun with the old iron sights, and many misses were recorded. 

Then came rifles with more speed, capable of shooting fast bullets with a flat trajectory. Men used field glasses with which to locate the game and then took long and deliberate aim with iron sights. Finally the advantages of using the glass on the riflc instead of in the hands were realized and immediately this long range shooting became more popular-and more successful. 

When men could hit the target at a longer range, arms and ammunition manufacturers began to make guns and cartridges that would do the work. Scope makers began to increase their production, too, and with the increased volume they were able to reduce the prices so that the average scope wouldn't cost more than the gun on which it was mounted. 

Then came scopes in even a lower price range than the guns, and today one can find wonderful scopes for any caliber rifle that will cost but a small portion of the investment in the gun. 

As a result you will find more scopes in general use, both in target and field shooting today than even the fondest dreamers a few years ago would have believed possible. 

Today the man who has a battery of guns without having at least two or three scope equipped is almost as much behind the shooting times as the man who has no guns at all and incidentally, he is missing half the sport and satisfaction of rifle shooting.

HISTORY OF LEFEVER GUNS 

Among names famous in the gunmakers' art, not one is more resplendent with brilliant success than that of the late Dan Lefever, affectionately known to thousands of sportsmen as Uncle 

Dan Lefever.

Dan Lefever learned his trade as a gunsmith in Rochester, N. Y. That was about 80 years ago. In 1861, when the Civil War broke out, this great gunsmith had a little shop in Canandiagua, N. Y., and there he made superior rifles for some of the sharpshooters in the Northern army. A few years later he moved to 

Auburn, N. Y., where he did high class gunsmithing, also making a few shotguns on special order, all hand work. Occasionally he converted a muzzle loader into a high class breech loader and that is a mighty hard thing to do. The Lefever was the first double breech loading hammerless gun made in America. 

This original breech loading Lefever hammerless was cocked by a side lever, which the shooter pushed down directly after firing the gun in order to cock it so it could be loaded and fired again. A little later a change was made so that when the gun was opened in order to put the shells in the barrels, the opening of the gun cocked both hammers so the side lever cocking mechanism was dispensed with.

Patents on the first Lefever hammerless were issued in 1872. 

From Auburn Uncle Dan Lefever moved to Syracuse, where he went into the extensive manufacture of shotguns bearing the name Lefever and for many years he superintended the building of his famous line of guns.

A few years before his death Uncle Dan Lefever sold his interests in the Lefever Arms Co. of Syracuse and moved to Ohio, where he started another factory under the name of D. M. 

Lefever & Son. The Ohio plant was hardly going smoothly when

THE OUTBOARD MOTOR 

The importance of an outboard motor to the huntsman or fisherman is well recognized by members of these two clans. 

We recently purchased an Evinrude twin-cylinder Sportswin and find it an indispensable part of a sportsman's equipment. 

The principal feature we like about this motor is that it can be used on so many types of boats. It is designed and recommended, as are all other Evinrude models, for use in both fresh and salt water. Special Parkerizing rust-proofing process is applied to all propeller and drive shafts, and other exposed steel parts.

It is equipped with check valve carburetor, which is ideal for slower and medium speeds. On the models designed for higher speeds, rotary valves are used. 

It is of the weedless type, a necessary feature for getting about among lily pads, etc. It can be throttled down to 12 miles per hour and has a speed range up to 9 miles per hour. 

Ideal for fishing, duck hunting, etc. 

Evinrude motors are noted for easy, instant starting, 100% hooded power, 100% underwater silencing, 100% co-pilot equipped, and 100% centrifugal pump cooling.

We are delighted with our Sportswin and recommend this model particularly for duck shooting and fishing.
