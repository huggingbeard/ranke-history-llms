What joy in Prague!. A dream centuries old became a reality. The Czechs finally threw off the hated yoke of the Habsburgs and took the future into their own hands. 

Three hundred years before, the Czechs lost their independence in the unsuccessful battle of the White Mountain, and for three hundred years they fought, often desperately, to regain their freedom.

Since 1526 the Habsburgers acted independently as 

German Emperors and as Kings of Bohemia. The Bohemian crown included Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia; this, even today being the concept of historical countries. The independence of these Bohemian states was first lost in 1620 at the defeat on the White Mountain, and eventually became the provinces of the Habsburg empire. What the Czechs originally demanded was the restoration of their independence within the monarchy. The Czech historian Palacky uttered the famous phrase, "If Austria does not exist, it must be created." This statement is only understandable

in its relationship to the Frankfort parliamentary election in 1848. The Germans of Austria were determined to carry out these elections in the "National Convention of Nations belonging to the German Federation," while the Czechs refused such representation, since they feared, it endangered their nationalistic existence. Conflicts between Germans and Czechs were constantly reoccuring, the question of a peaceful relationship between these two peoples arose in history from time to time, and always remained unsolved. 

A pronounced antagonism against the dynasty and Germanism developed during the reign of Franz Josef I. This cleft developed unhindered until the World War. Up to this period, however, the Czech leaders never considered the possibility of a freedom as expressed through the establishment of an independent state. When Masaryk left Austria in 1915, he certainly considered the possibility of independence, but it was not until his activities on the outside that this thought took concrete form. The article by - 

Benes, who followed him soon after, entitled "Destroy

Austria-Hungary," was the first expression of revolutionary activity abroad. In post-war years, the question of which played the greater part in the creation of the Czechoslovak state was often discussed among the Czechs themselves, namely, the work abroad or politics at home. It appears without doubt that the establishment of the state in its present form is almost entirely the work of Masaryk and Benes. The groundwork was well prepared by the politicians at home, and the entire population, bitterly aroused against the government in Vienna, but it is safe to say that until 1918 no one at home gave serious thought to the establishment of a free and independent state. Thus if a Czechoslovakia state exists today in this form, it is due to the enlightened teaching and propaganda work of

these two men. Without their efforts there would be no 

Czechoslovakia today. This should not belittle the meritorious work of the leaders at home, but merely serve, in concrete form, that the state was created on the basis for which the combined statesmanship of Masaryk and Benes provided the plans. This work is of greater value since the ethnographic conditions of Austria-Hungary were little known abroad, and the leaders of Western democracies thus came to understand the demands of the Czechs. At any rate, in this imperfect knowledge of the situation, lay the germs of the struggle within the Czechoslovakian Republic, and with it, the crisis today. 

The new republic originated in struggle, struggle loosened the old bonds, struggle determined the boundaries, struggle with internal and external enemies, struggle against the Germans, Hungarians and Poles. Germans, Hungarians and Poles became citizens of the new state,-but Germans, Hungarians and Poles were also neighbors of the new republic. The Sudeten Germans, named after the Sudeten 

Mountains in these countries, consisting of the German Bohemians, Moravians and Silesians, were hit with the greatest force by the collapse of the monarchy. Their entire political and economic position would be demolished by one blow, their future become undetermined. Only one question was clear-a Federation of the German provinces was not to be considered in a republic which was to be formed. Only industry remained exclusive, because it hoped for better business conditions in a victorious state, as the new Czechoslovakian state considered itself. For the rest it must be said, that an "Anschluss" to Germany was not sought by either side. As the Austro-Hungarian disaster became obvious, before the proclamation of independence of the Czechoslovakian State, in accordance with the

"self determination theory" originated by President Wilson, the German-Austrian Convention laid claim to the control of the entire German-speaking Austrian provincesespecially that of the Sudeten-Germans. This demand was arrived at in agreement with the countries concerned. Thus arose four provinces from the district of Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia, which were under the control of a new German- Austrian state. This structure was not a serious reality either geographically or economically and only served as an expression of animosity to the Czechs. Contrary to this, the Czechs took the stand that their state was to be created within its historical boundaries, and that no other incorporation was to be countenanced. They made reference to the transactions which were carried on during the war, with the Allies, in which they had been assured the desired boundaries. Through the basic differences of the two points of view, an agreement could not be arrived at. 

The Czechs occupied the German provinces withont any serious opposition. The Sudeten-German politicians did, however, seek to gain German military aid, which had originally been promised to them, but due to the uncertain conditions of the German state itself, and in consideration of the terms of the Peace Treaty, this had to be abandoned. 

In early January 1919, the French, English and Italian governments sanctioned the right of the government of Czechoslovakia to occupy the territory within the historical boundaries of the Bohemian crown. Thus the destiny of the German territory was sealed. Notwithstanding this, the Sudeten-Germans did not consider their cause a lost one, and pressed their rights as before. Thus bloody conflict ensued, between the population and the military occupants, results which overshadowed the communal life of both peoples. The Sudeten-Germans themselves and 10

the Austro-German government sought to convince the Peace Conference of the error of its decision-in vain, however, for on September 10, 1919, the Peace Treaty, which gave Czechoslovakia its legal boundaries, was signed. This decision was attained because of the impressive presentation of the Czechoslovakian delegation at the Peace Conference, which convinced the chief delegates of the Western Powers anew, of the necessity of absorbing German Territory. The Czechs argued that a loss of this territory, that is the territory of German colonization, robbed them of their natural defences and would thus leave them open to a later German attack. Only a natural line of defense -the surrounding border mountains-were, from a military point of view, a weapon for supporting the independence of the state. The reference to the industries in the border territory, without which the new republic did not believe it could exist, served as further argument. Finally the historic moment for the ancient political, cultural and economic unity arrived. The Sudeten-German territory never belonged to Germany. On the contrary, parts of present-day Prussia were previously part of the lands belonging to the Bohemian crown. Therefore it is entirely erroneous to say, that the Sudeten-Germans want to return to their native land. It was the well meaning right of the 

Czech leaders-the chief delegate was Dr. Benes-to demand as large a part of redistributed Europe as possible. Another question remains, however-whether the others were not duty bound to examine the facts in a neutral manner, especially, since neither Clemenceau, Lloyd George or Wilson knew the subject matter well enough to be able to decide satisfactorily. That a situation was provoked, in 1919, which strengthened the enmity between the Czechs and the Germans, was to be attributed to this lack of knowledge-if 11

one is to overlook the psychological side of the victors—and, at that time, gave cause for a later conflict. However, it must be said that no solution could have been given which would have satisfied the Germans within a Czech state. 

While the German question presented a more or less internal problem, the determination of the new frontier in Slovakia brought the first foreign conflict for Czechoslovakia-with Hungary. The inhabitants of the most northern part of what formerly was Hungary,-the Slovaksgave their allegiance to the newly formed Czechoslovak state through the Treaty of St. Martin of October 30, 1918. 

Even then, voices were heard at the council which resented such a union. They feared the influence which a union of 

Slovakia with the historic countries, of a more secure economic standing, would have on their own economic life. Fundamentally, however, the Slovakian parties took the stand for a unified state. Immediately after the agreement at 

St. Martin the Czech troops began to occupy Slovakia. The 

Hungarians to whom this territory formerly belonged, met this occupation with armed force, so that the Czech army, which numbered only a few hundred, had to withdraw. The negotiations for the purpose of compromise—until the decision by the Peace Conference,-between the Hungarian

Government and Dr. Hodza, plenipotentiary of the Government at Prague-resulted unsatisfactorily. The Hungarian troops evacuated Slovakia, under orders from the Allies who also determined the present boundaries. Still the 

Hungarians sought to repossess the land. In May, 1919, under the Communistic regime of Bela Kun, the Hungarian soldiers took over the greater part of the territory. So once again the Peace Conference stepped in, and demanded, according to the established boundaries, that the Hungarian Government cease their invasion and evacuate the land. 12

Thus was the territory definitely awarded to Czechoslovakia and the Peace Treaty of Trianon on June 24, 1920, which concerned already existing facts, legalized. Also, the territory of Carpathia Russia once belonging to Hungary, east of Slovakia, was awarded to Czechoslovakia as an autonomy.

Particular difficulties accompanied the establishment of a frontier on the Polish border. The object of conflict formed the Teschen territory, the eastern part of which was formerly Austrian Silesia. The Poles occupied this territory, which resulted in military steps being taken by Czechoslovakia. Here, also, the two factions could not agree, and an arbitration was expected. Although one was achieved in 1920, border incidents occurred until the end of 1923, at which time the Hague Court of Arbitration established a final borderline.

It is easy to understand that the conflicts that the young republic had with parts of its own people and with its neighboring states left many thorns. When Austria, Hungary and Poland made demands, they were not territorial demands as such, but rather, in every case, concerned territory in which natives of these countries lived. 

It was certainly painful for the Germans and Hungarians to have peace terms dictated to them, after having waited four and one half years to exert their own volition against their opponents. For the Germans in Czechoslovakia, the psychological moment was almost their entire concern, since nothing, either geographically or ethnographically, was changed. It is certainly understandable that such a territorial loss disconcerted the Hungarians; still many such difficulties could have been mitigated through a less stubborn attitude on the part of the Hungarian Governments. 13

THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE REPUBLIC 

The young republic faced a number of problems, whose solution and mastery made apparent the skill of not only its leading men, but also of the entire Czech people. Although mistakes occurred which later-as in the present situation endangered the existence of the state, its accomplishments cannot be belittled. No matter how the situation is viewed, it must be recognized from an unbiased attitude that Czechoslovakia remained the only state in middle and eastern Europe spared from the political and economic confusion which caused the suffering in other lands; it always maintained a feeling of consolidation. This does not, however, exclude the fact that Czechoslovakia also suffered an economic depression that hit the world in general. But perhaps, just such abnormal circumstances, especially in a new state, are the criterion of its inner strength and the spirit of its countrymen. Everyone who knows the situation must agree that in the twenty years of its existence, the state has accomplished constructive work in many fields, which served the welfare of all its inhabitants.

IN POLITICS 

If one follows the work of the foreign revolution, its alliance with the ideals of the Western Democracy, it is only too clear that this contact was maintained even after the peace settlement-in fact strengthened. Thus, if not 14

an actual hatred, at least a strong aversion to all that was 

German and Hungarian played an active part, explainable by a war psychosis and by a recollection of pre-war days. 

Who could blame the Czechs for harboring a bitterness against the Germans? Many actions which did not serve the interests of the state must be excused for this reason. 

The treatment of the Czechs by the Germans in former Austria was not yet forgotten. The Czech people, their language, their culture, in fact, all that was Czech was made to suffer. To be seen frequently with a Czech was sufficient to be expelled from "better German Society."

Foreign policy consisted of two main problems. First, the safety of Slovakia against all time attempts of Hungary to regain this territory, which it now, as before, still claims. Hungary never concealed the fact that it signed the 

Peace Treaty under pressure, and only awaited the desired opportunity to regain its lost property. This also concerned the territory assigned to Roumania and Jugoslavia ; in this case, however, the strained relationship was eventually lessened, yet an expressed enmity between Hungary and Czechoslovakia continued, which only awaited a favorable moment to break out in open warfare.

The second important question of Czechoslovakian foreign policy was the prevention of a union between Germany and Austria, and the restoration of the Habsburgs. 

The union was definitely prohibited in the Peace Treaty, and no attempt was made in the early post-war years to join these two countries. But it was generally known that in Austria large circles flirted with the possibility of such a union. The oft seemingly hopeless economic situation of 

Austria was offered as a pretext for such plans, with reference to the fact that an independennt Austro-German state would be an economic absurdity. But actually the 15

thought of a greater German Empire was the driving force. 

In this question the politic of Czechoslovakia sought ȧid from their old ally, France. Not only did they have the same ideals, but also the same "enemies." Thus they arrived at a pact for mutual aid, which was framed in the Locarno Treaty in 1925. The Germans in the body politic resented this tie from the beginning. Not only from animosity toward France, but in the knowledge that any tie of the Czech Republic with France was directed against 

Germany. Intentional attack was not expected, but German suppression was understood in the concept "Maintaining conditions as created through the Peace Treaty." In this connection the occupation of the Ruhr still remained a lively memory.

The attitude of the government toward Germany was one of formal neighborliness during post-war years. It was always a reverberation of Berlin-Paris translations. If these became more strained, as during the time of the Ruhr occupation, the German-Czech relation clouded. 

Undoubtedly the unsettled political and economic conditions in Germany had bearing on the seemingly friendly relations of both sides-and in fact these relations were peaceful. The native Germans however, could speak more openly, and did so at every opportunity. They could not agree with any foregn policy which arose against Germany and which wounded their deepest feelings. While, however, before Hitler came into power this situation took an undetermined form for this very reason, since the Sudeten Germans did not agree with the tolerant German foreign policy, since 1933, they became much more radical in their views, they demanded a foreign policy in agreement with 

Germany, a demand although seemingly understandable, from the standpoint of Czechoslovakia not feasable. How- 16

ever, it must be taken into consideration that almost onequarter of the whole country was sympathetic toward Germany, that the native Germans actually belonged to the same people, and finally, Germany, the largest neighboring country, would eventually strengthen itself. It would be difficult to judge today whether a closer policy with Germany would have made the Sudeten-Germans more sympathetic toward the Czechoslovakian state-in fact it is doubtful. But certainly a foreign policy without regard to 

Germany caused much bitterness among the Germans in Czechoslovakia, and it hindered a better relationship with Germany.

The attitude toward Austria was friendly, only troubled for a short time by the expected customs union, between Germany and Austria in 1931, which was sharply attacked by Czechoslovakia in conjunction with the Great Powers. 

The government saw in the proposed agreement a forerunner to an "Anschluss" to which it, under no circumstances, wanted to give its consent. Although the plan met with general opposition and had to be abandoned, the thought of an annexation with Germany, although not new, was again formed, and intensified itself from year to year. 

It must be pointed out here, that this plan was energetically defended by the Social Democrats of Austria. When the social democracy demanded an independent Austria after 1933, it meant merely a united front against the National Socialist regime in Germany, namely, Fascism. They were not opposed to a union with the German people, but were opposed only to a union with the present government. 

This differentiation must be made. The official relationship between Austria and the Czechoslovakian Republic remained, until the German annexation of Austria, a very good one, especially toward the end when it was particular- 17

ly strengthened by a common interest-namely, the prevention of an annexation.

The ambitions of Hungary to regain its lost territory was causing peril, as was expected from the beginning of 

Czechoslovakian independence. Czechoslovakian diplomacy sought again to arrive at a better understanding, without avail, since Hungary, before settling other problems, wanted the territorial problem settled. Thus came the restoration attempts of King Karl in 1921, which met with stubborn objections from the Czechoslovakian government, who took measures, one of which was mobilization; thus these attempts failed. All this naturally had its reaction. Hungary remained an enemy of the Czechoslovakian Republic to this day. In order to avert danger from this side, Czechoslovakia, Jugoslavia and Roumania formed a defensive alliance known as the Little Entente-with the expressed clause that the three partners would aid each other in the event of an attack by Hungary. This policy in its very spirit caused further strain between the two countries, and also agitated the some 800,000 Hungarians within Czechoslovakia. But here again it must be rightly said that the 

Czech foreign policy from the point of view of insuring the safety of its borders, was properly handled, and especially in this case where actual danger threatened from the ever present Hungarian ambition for revision. The revision, of the Trianon Peace Treaty, to reestablish the pre-war Hungarian territory is on the program of every Hungarian government. The Hungarian population within 

Czechoslovakia did not appear aggressive in any manner, but there exists little doubt that at a propitious moment they might voice their feelings.

The relationships with Poland also remained unsolved. 

In the case of Germany and Hungary it concerned certain- 18

ly a deeper cause, whereas with Poland it could be considered an opportune unfriendliness. It would be easy to appease if Poland did not take unfair advantage of the strained relationship existing, at that time, between Germany and the Republic of Czechoslovakia. Both states signed conciliatory agreements after 1923, and Dr. Benes in reference to these agreements spoke even of an "eternal friendship." In actual fact there were no weighty differences between the two countries. Neither the extent of the territory of which Poland demanded control, nor the size of the minority living in Czechoslovakia, cira 90,000, could cause a serious misunderstanding. On the contrary, one can say with right and reason, that both countries faced a like danger in middle Europe, and therefore had to set up a similar front for defense. Nevertheless, Poland takes her stand in opposition to Czechoslovakia. Its policy since its establishment of friendly relations with Germany in 1934 leaves no doubt as to its aim. It does not indicate, however, that in the event of war it would be found in the enemy ranks. 

Nevertheless, its position greatly weakens the international standing of Czechoslovakia.

The situation with U.S.S.R. is of importance. Although a close bond existed between the Czech and Russian politicians before the war, the relationship between the two governments remained cold over a period of years, due to the existing type of government in Russia, and the fear that communistic activities would eventually gain the upper hand in Czechoslovakia. The Czechs, however, had never forgotten their ties with Slavic Russia, and felt themselves naturally drawn toward it. It was therefore understandable when the Czechoslovakian state, after having already established commercial contact in 1922, finally in 1934 recognized the Soviet Union as official, and normal diplomatic 19

relationships were begun. This development was accelerated by the National Socialistic government in power in Germany since 1933, whose open policy against the Soviet Government and the growing threat against Czechoslovakia was the cause for the Czechoslovakian-Russian Alliance signed in 1935. That this particular alliance should be the object of strongest attack by the Sudeten Germans is made clear by the agreement of the German population with the then ruling German government. Germany also saw in this an unfriendly move, especially so since it was to be considered as the completion of the recently signed Russo-French Treaty.

The relationship with Italy is still to be mentioned. 

In early post-war years, the two states were very friendly toward each other, since the memory of their common fight against Austria was still fresh. Even after Mussolini came into power in 1922 there was no change in these happy relations. But these altered as Italy began to broaden its political and economic sphere of influence, in Central Europe, and to use Hungary, so to speak, as a springboard. The 

Italian foreign policy openly supported the Hungarian plans for revision, and thus consciously placed itself in a counterposition to Czechoslovakia. Italian friction with France and Jugoslavia did not help this situation. Ultimately,

Czechoslovakia agreed to the sanctions against Italy of the other powers, because of its war with Abyssinia, a circumstance which only aggravated the situation. The relationship was also obviously influenced by the Rome-Berlin axis; at the same time the fact must not be overlooked that Italy viewed Germany's aims for expansion at the cost of 

Czechoslovakia with grave distrust. 20

IN ECONOMICS 

The great necessity of solving its economic and financial problems faced the young republic. The measure of 

Dr. Rasin, first Czechoslovakian Minister of Finance, must be considered ingenious. As it became more obvious that 

Austria was heading for inflation, Rasin decided in 1919 to "stamp" all banknotes of the Austro-Hungarian bank and to exclude the unstamped notes from circulation. So a national currency was created-namely, the Czechoslovakian crown. It is to be attributed to this measure that the 

Czechs escaped the financial chaos that the other countries later suffered.

The question of repaying the war loans raised a special point. The peace treaty of St. Germain relieved successive states of the former monarchy of this burden. The weight of this fell especially heavy on the Germans, who, following their patriotic duty to the limit of their capabilities, gave their help to the Austrian state. The Czechs from the viewpoint that they must give no financial aid to their enemies, as they still considered the old Habsburg state, gave only as much as pressure from above demanded, so they were much less injured than any other part of the population, due to their agreement not to honor the war loans. This action, which can be explained by the Entente from their standpoint as victors, taken by Czechoslovakia, worked against its own citizens, and created one of the greatest reasons why the situation between the Germans and the Czechs could not be improved. In this particular case the small man learned that the government was unfriendly toward him. Thus later, it is true, a measure was found for part payment, but for the majority of the Germans the problem of the war loans still remains unsolved today. 21

The basic manner of the partition of the large territorial possessions, after the establishment of the state, caused much bad feeling. Not the fact as such, incited the spirits in the German camp, but the circumstance, that this measure was a nationalistic act causing harm to the Germans. The large landowners were for the most part German and Hungarian. The land reform was to satisfy the hunger for land of the small peasant. It had without doubt, moral right. The lands of the Czechs were confiscated after the battle on the White Mountain and given to the loyal members of the royal nobility. This land reform was therefore, enacted in the name of righting the wrongs that the 

Czechs sustained three hundred years earlier, it appeared, in reality, a measure in decreasing the economic power of the German and Hungarian citizens of the state. 

The dissolution of the customs and monetary unity of the old monarchy had particular difficulties for Czechoslovakia. The initial problem raised the question of industry. About 80% of the most important Austro-Hungarian industrial factories remained in territory of the present Czechoslovakia, as against 25% of the population. It is easy to see what such a disproportion, which arose practically overnight, meant. Here a new and unbiased policy should have given aid. At the beginning, this policy, still under the war psychosis, which did not permit of a contact with its former customers, suffered. Corresponding to the general policy, they had to seek western orientation for economic outlet. Although the Peace Treaty recommended an economic alliance between Czechoslovakia, Austria and Hungary for the first five years, a certain shortsighted, too exact nationalism frustrated these plans. However, soon it appeared that an economic outlet was not to be found among the western allies, and treaties with the neigh- 22

boring states were signed, which, however, eased the situation only in part. The industrial situation remained serious. The large territorial outlet, the Austro-Hungarian monarchy, was irrevocably lost for the most part. 

In addition to all the difficulties caused by the hyper-v trophy of industry, the policy of the Agricultural Party arose, which was of the opinion that the importation of agricultural products be more and more limited; since the neighboring countries, especially Hungary, Jugoslavia and Roumania were primarily agricultural countries, this meant only one thing-the closing of their nearest market. For example, on the insistence of the Farm Party the commercial treaty with Hungary was cancelled in 1929. There was immediate cessation of textile export from Czechoslovakia to Hungary, a large output, and a sudden definite unemployment appeared. In this attitude the Czech and German farmers were in agreement, and this resulted in Germans appearing in the Cabinet of the Goverment for the first time. It was, also, the agricultural protection which hindered commercial activities within the countries of the Little Entente, although here political and psychological hypotheses were given. On the other hand, such a policy-especially, with such an uncertain international situation-resulted in the creation of entirely new industries within the successive states themselves, and thus was Czechoslovakian manufacture lost for all times. In this disagreement between agriculture and industry, Czechs and Germans took sides against Czechs and Germans. The agricultural element was scarcely aided by this, since through growing unemployment customers for agricultural products were lacking. 23

THE GERMAN PROBLEM 

In studying the national structure of Czechoslovakia, it becomes immediately apparent that it is a miniature of former Austro-Hungary, with similar problems. Here, as there, different nationalities lived and were ruled by one of these different nationalities. If the German element controlled the fate of Austria, so in the Czechosolvakian republic, as the name indicates, the Slavic element holds the reins. 

Czechs, Slovaks Germans, Hungarians, Poles and Ruthenians live in this state, formed by peace treaties. The 

Czechs thus formed a state in a manner to which they had been opposed before 1918, and to which they had denied every right of existence. This naturally does not mean that such a structure could not learn from the failures of history, and for its part, create better conditions for people of different nationalities to live together in friendly relationship. It is, however, a historical irony that just this particular people, who had so strongly resented a monarchy made up of different nationalities, had to set up just such a type of state. This chart shows that the Czechs are in the minority against the other nationalities in the republic, yet in the official circles only a Czechoslovakian nation is known. But actually these are two different nationalistic groups, although closely related in language. There is no more a 

Czechoslovakian language than there is a Czechoslovakian nation. Thus the Czechoslovakian concept, especially in the sense in which it is used, is a myth. Actually, two official languages exist, Czech in the historical lands, and Slovak in those of Slovakia. In addtion to this, there is Russian in the Carpathian region. The desire for autonomy, which had been promised to the Slovaks during the war in the Pittsburgh Treaty, makes it known that this part of the population wanted its own government and its own cultural life.

An obstacle in the path of a prosperous life together for the inhabitants of the states, was caused by the establishment of the Constitution. Immediately after the founding of the state, a revolutionary national convention was named which made necessary laws for the early days. 

The crowning of their achievement lay in the drawing up of the constitution. But this National Convention consisted of only Czechs and Slovaks. This means that one-third of the entire population had no voice in the work of laying the foundation for the state. Not until the completion of. and because of the Constitution, was a general election called, which made representation from the other nationalities possible. It was inevitable that in the course of the years, their demands would be refused according to the clauses in the constitution. But just for this reason, the 

Czechs wanted to complete the constitution without interference from the others. Particularly the Germans saw in this, a measure which was only to serve to lessen their 25

rights. Thus the oft repeated call for a change in the Constitution. But especially in this respect every Czechoslovakian government was stubborn, as it wished to avoid setting a precedent.

Even if the Slovakians are included in the Czech group. hardly two thirds of the entire population are accounted for. In such a census situation, it is difficult to speak of the other populations as minorities. This is true in large measure about the Sudeten Germans. This part of the population inhabits the border territory of Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia, neighboring Germany and what was Austria in enclosed territory. The plains made up of German speaking territory measure about 26,000 kilometres, that is, slightly smaller than Belgium-the Sudeten German population is almost as large as Denmark and larger than Norway, the single Baltic states and many other independent states. Not only because of their numbers do they play a part, but also their economic and cultural position is important. That they are a part of a large nation must not be overlooked, any more than the circumstance, that three and a half million Germans were considered a minority, when two and a half million Slovaks acted as part of the ruling state. There is no analogy on earth, and therefore it is difficult to understand a minority concept. 

Similarly, but not in such a crass manner, the situation existed with the Hungarian inhabitants. They also live in an enclosed territory in southern Slovakia. The antipathy, however, does not exist toward the Slovakians, but again, against the Czechs. The protection in this case by Hungary is naturally not so powerful as that of 

Germany, so that no direct attack threatened from this side. It has, however, already been mentioned, that the 26

Czech Hungarians, at a propitious moment, would step out of their reserve.

The Poles of Czechoslovakia found themselves in open conflict with the government. At any rate the situation was the expression of the unfriendly feeling of the Polish government toward the Czechoslovakian government, because the Polish minority in particular, enjoyed far greater rights, under Czechoslovakia, than, for example, it did under Germany. Especially, from an economic point of view, this national group were in better circumstances than the average Pole living in Poland.

A somewhat unclear policy was carried on by the Slovakians. The great part of the Slovakian population remained under the influence of the recently deceased Father Hlinka, for many years the leader of the Slovakian Peoples Party. He was the unrelenting defender of autonomy and the elimination of the Czech element in Slovakia. In this, his fight against the Prague government, he employed the same tactics as that of Konrad Henlein's party-that is the Sudeten-German party, which knew, naturally, how to take advantage of these confederates. What the actual intention of a leader of the Slovakian party would be, it would be hard to say. It was looked upon with suspicion when strings resulting from a case of high treason proceedings against a Slovakian professor some years ago, led to Budapest. It was more suspicious, however, that the foremost

Slovakian leaders sympathized with the man who had been heavily sentenced to prison. It is thought that a Slovakian autonomy could also be successful in a Hungarian state, but the Slovakians had the least reason for complaint. It might be that the political situation was not a desirable one, but nowhere was more accomplished by the Czechs than in Slovakia. Rightly, the taxpayers, of the historic lands. 27

-- complained that the large portion of the payments went to Slovakia, while it was needed in their own territory. Before the war, under Hungarian rule there were scarcely two hundred Slovakian public schools; now there are more than three thousand. Similar expansion is seen in all educational programs. Also great progress was made in other fields a great construction program-which can only be judged by those who knew the conditions before the war. 

Even so, the position of Slovakia is not such that the Czechs could look to them for any great aid. On the contrary, the opponents censure the Czechs, due to the Slovakians and their aversion to Czech supremacy. But it cannot be counted upon that the Slovakians, in a moment of danger to the state, would take up arms against it.

To the myth of a Czechoslovakian nation, there is added another one that of a national state. Every measure and every law-in short, the entire force of government is based on this concept, and with this understanding the state is controlled. The constitution that was to insure the predominating party of the Czechs, was built on this fiction. 

The form of government is the democratic republic, yet an exaggerated Chauvenism, which held the reigns in its hands after the war, did not permit the true democracy to arise. Innumerable laws were effected which seemed only to be created against the Germans and Hungarians. First of all, the language law. It degraded the inhabitants of the state who were not Czechs to citizens of the second class, and was particularly irritating to the Germans. As for example, Prague as the capital of the state, was also the capital of the Germans. Not only were the main German institutions located there, but also German schools, theaters and thousands of German residents and since due to travel, German visitors were most numerous. In spite of this, di- 28

rections in German were not permitted to be displayed. In every other language, placards giving directions could appear. On the other hand, in the German area, directions had to appear in both languages, and the Czech was to be placed first, and these localities had been previously pure German, and even now have only a few percent Czechs! In order to dissipate German influence, Czech officials were placed in German territory. So it happened again and again that only Czechs clerked. Though truly in almost every case they understood German, the German inhabit-. ants looked upon them as strangers and usurpers, especially since German officials and employees had been dismissed. This occurred in railroad, post office, political and financial offices. This replacement of Czech officials in 

German territory gave rise to the necessity for creating Czech schools, and was looked upon by the Germans as telling proof of government Czechization.

This language law was certainly an expression of resentment of the Czechs against whatever was German, especially as it made its appearance in the early post-war years. 

Unfortunately, it must be said that this language problem does not exist only amongst the Czechs; it is today the greatest disgrace in Europe. Economic jealousy alone could not aggravate such an unhealthy atmosphere as did the language conflict.

As we have already seen, the Czechs attempted, whenever possible, to strengthen their own power. The war loan problem, the bestowal of state commissions, the subvention of Czech banks served toward this aim. It would be entirely erroneous, however, for this thesis to imply that the Sudeten German territory was intentionally destroyed. In this the Czechs are at fault in very small part. The Czechoslovakian economic situation suffered as a result, as did the 29

rest of the world. It is generally known that industry was especially affected, but a large part of Czechoslovakian industry, such as glass and textiles, were to be found in border territory, particularly occupied by Germans. It is understandable, in accordance with the structure of the crisis, that the industrial centers of the Czechoslovakian republic, especially those in German territory, were hard hit. Overexpansion of industry and the many finance measures of other countries placed the majority of manufacturers in new difficulty. The industries had to reorganize again and again in the last twenty years, and a large number of them were not in a position to withstand constantly recurring crises, and ultimately had to dissolve. It is to be. noted that in no other case did the Germans and Czechs find themselves so in accord as in the effort, if not direct-to ruin the industry. The coalition of Czech and German farmers in the throttling of agricultural imports could have no greater results than that of increasing unemployment. 

That in this, German industries and German people were affected, did not deter the German farmers from acting jointly with the Czechs when it concerned possible larger gains, a hope which did not materialize. The only beneficiaries were the Czech farmers and later the Czech industries, which succeeded in creating new modern factories on the ruins of the German-in Czech territory. 

France played a condemnable part in its relationship with the commerce of Czechoslovakia. Although a friendly political relationship existed between the two countries, France did not contribute any aid to the economic difficulties of Czechoslovakian business. Trade negotiations never led to any satisfactory result and the agents for 

Czechoslovakia always came back empty-handed. Every handkerchief exported to France had to be bar- 30

gained over! The French trade policy had brought Jugoslavia, for instance into the German camp, and under such continuing conditions it seemed apparent that the Czechs would have to revise their policy. A more active supporting policy of Czechoslovakian commerce would have been more valuable than the present mobilization and protestation, in giving aid, if the Czechoslovakian republic was to be attacked. A helpful trade policy would never have permitted the crisis today to become so pronounced and would have urged a more evolutionary tendency. These facts were known in Czech circles as well as in German. The foreign policy is to be determined by the necessities of the momentary economic situation. But naturally the economic situation influenced the inner political life. 

The situation is here clearly shown since it is necessary in the judgment of the whole complex question. The Czechoslovakian government itself said repeatedly, that mistakes were evident and should be corrected without further ado. 

Without doubt the pacification of the case in the interests of the Czechs and also of the whole state was necessary and it is doubtless that now the Czech people think differently than they did immediately after the war in the excitement of victory. The Czechs are really a peace loving, industrious people and in the depths of their very spirit incline toward democracy. If certain privileges were limited, the reasons did not lie so much in the attitude of the government at Prague, as in the international situation in general and in the conditions of Central-Europe in particular. In no case, during the last twenty years, did either oppression or terror exist. All the nationalities had certainly more freedom than even the German people enjoy today. Nevertheless, people especially those in the west err, if they assume that freedom, as it is understood in the western demo- 31

cracies, is such a valuable possession to the average German. 

National propaganda is right in so far as it says that the democratic way of thinking is foreign to the population. The 

German is accustomed to being ruled by a strong handand the majority of the Sudeten-Germans, in becoming members of the German Reich will not suffer under the existing limitations of freedom. The Austrians will perhaps be an exception to this, and will probably be a more difficult problem for the present regime to handle. Large groups of the Czech population already were cognizant of the fact that the concept of a national state had to be abandoned, that only a change of the constitution could bring about justice for the peoples of the state. It is obvious that the Czechs and the Germans living together in the same territory for centuries had influenced each other's culture and the knowledge that in spite of the repeated friction, Bohemia belonged with the most economically developed countries of Europe. One cannot speak of the "pure" blood of the population, neither of the Germans or any of the others, since Czechs and Germans have intermarried during the course of the centuries; it is safe to say that about 70% of the inhabitants have "mixed” blood. Henlein, the leader of the Sudeten-Germans himself, has Czech blood on his mother's side. It is often the case that there are leaders with pure Czech names-and whose fathers speak no word of German. The absurdity of this lies in the fact that just such people are the greatest enemies of the Czechs.

If the problem of the German population is treated with more detail than the problem of the other groups who were also not Czech, it is because the German question is a vital one for the Czechslovak state. Certainly difficulties existed with the Magyars, Poles and even the sec- 32

ond state group the Slovakians-that are not to be underrated. These are however so involved with the German problem as to be necessarily solved along with it. 

The Germans of the Czechoslovakian republic are however difficult to handle. They are an industrious, unassuming people, somewhat obdurate as one generally finds among mountain folk. In culture and politics they succumb to the Czechs. Not now alone but also in the time of Austria the Czechs showed their political superiority. 

They display no interest in intellectual matters and only sporadically produced men of some importance. 

But even these in comparison with Germans in Germany or Austria were not above the average. One can quietly assume that the Sudeten Germans are the least valuable group amongst all the German people. This knowledge is not new and Germans in the Reich and in Austria were very little drawn to the Sudeten Germans, and fundamentally no more so today. In politics they belong to the most radical element. Hitler's race theory had its forerunner set in the examples in former Austria by German- 

Bohemian representatives-Wolff, Schoenerer. One can hardly realize sufficiently how much harm was done by these two representatives to the monarchy. Too much is not said when one states that the intolerant policy of the Sudeten Germans, which showed itself primarly toward the Czechs caused in great proportion the destruction of the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. It is true that the Germans in historical lands from the beginning opposed their membership in the new state energetically. It remains without argument that they rightly considered themselves injured by the attitude of the Prague government. Also, the psychological attitude that they were formerly a ruling nation with all privileges and were now to be "ruled" is not to be 33

overlooked. The Czechs could certainly by means of a wise policy have won over the working element especially in the industrial centers. For this they had too little foresight and the growing unemployment served toward agitation against the state. Yet everything could have been righted with the proper spirit. But today no one can speak of good will on the part of the Sudeten German. In this lack of proper groundwork mutual understanding was frustrated. 

It would seem unnecessary to seek further explanations. 

In the intellectual makeup of the Sudeten German there is no room for thoughtful consideration. It is not even a fanatic belief in a German empire but mere mob psychology which causes the people thoughtlessly to follow slogans and catchwords. If the Sudeten German had to accept the many restrictions which the food supply make necessary in Germany, propaganda would probably have spoken of four million starving Sudeten Germans! But, when a twenty-year old boy visits Germany and writes about the pleasure found in marching with the boys and girls even though the stomach grumbles, the enthusiasm at home knows no bounds. With the knowledge of the situation it must be douted thhat the Sudeten leaders were serious in their desire for autonomy. They want no relief within the 

Czechoslovakian state, they now want their "Revanche" for the lost World War, they want without doubt German annexation. This "Revanche" thought-revenge on the

Czechs is perhaps the only driving motive. Naturally it became more possible for them because of the national socialist program of unifying all Germans into one state. And thus it is clear that a national socialist government must follow such pressure.

Germany itself is more disposed to an incorporation today than it was previously. Even Bismark averted such 34

an annexation. Even later in the time of the Kaiser and during the existence of the Weimar republic this problem was not discussed. Certainly post-war Germany in spite of its ⚫ formal neighborliness was not particularly warm toward its Southern neighbor. Although no particularly serious conflict ensued Germany's attitude remained reserved. In this however, the psychological moment played a part. The

Germans considered the Czechs as "traitors," since during the war they had gone over to the enemy. The work of 

Masaryk and Benes on the continent is not forgotten, their union with the arch-enemy, France and its ideology which was always considered as against Germanism. That the relationship of the Germans in the republic toward the Czechs also played a part is obvious, although too much weight must not be put on this.

Only the methods changed in Germany's policy toward the Czechoslovak state since Hitler's inauguration. The aims remained the same and it is perhaps only recently seen that the old aspirations of Germany could be accomplished through radical means. The "Drang nach Osten" i. e., "Drive toward the East" existed under Wilhelm II as well as under Streseman and is driven forward with greater force today. Hungarian wheat, Rumanian oil, the Ukraine, the old dream of "Berlin-Bagdad" is as much alive today as ever; the aspirations for expansion know no bounds. (One should also not underestimate in this connection, the present conflict between the United States and Mexico. This obviously carries the mark of "made in Germany.") In the achievement of this goal Czechoslovakia naturally plays a great part. Not that 3½ million Sudeten Germans must be free but the Czechoslovakian republic must disappear from the midpoint of the line Paris-Prague-Moscow. At any rate it must be so weakened that it could not offer serious 35

opposition. Therefore the cry for self-determination, since this does not mean anything else according to previous statements but that not only the Germans would desert them but also the Hungarians and the Poles. It is certainly not said that the Czechoslovakians were fortunate in the existing allied system and that perhaps a closer political and economic alliance with Germany would be of greater advantage. On the other hand Czech public opinion and the fear that with time they would be swallowed by Germany prohibited this. Today the Sudetens only serve as an excuse for approaching the goal in the east. The Gerwithin the Czechoslovakian republic however, imagine, as is characteristic of them, that their fate is the seed of the problem and they flatter themselves, that it is the main point of world diplomacy. Not a small part of them understand the true situation. mans Whether this problem is to be settled by peace or by war still remains to be seen. One feels however, that this is of secondary importance. In the further consequence of 

German policy a great disturbance is unavoidable. Let it be assumed that the Sudeten German problem is settled peaceably. The next problem might be Danzig, then Memel, Poland, Switzerland, Alsace, Southern Tyrol, then even Hungary and Roumania. Not two opposing ideologies will find themselves in conflict but rather Germany through economic and political reasons will find itself against the entire world even fascistic Italy, whose interests in middle and southern Europe are under growing pressure. If this bastion, Czechoslovakia, falls, the way to the east is as good as clear. It is of no special importance in this connection, whether the remaining Czechs create an active state. This point must be ceded in any event, though it concerns a fertile territory and a population whose tenacity and industry 36

would hold their diminished state in order. Its development must be considered in the light of the latent danger of attack by an overpowering and aggressive neighbor. No guarantees or treaties are of any use in such a case. In 

March 1938, as the first troops crossed the German-Austrian border, the official German government stated that the integrity of the Czechoslovakian state would be absolutely guaranteed. The German-Czechoslovakian arbitration pact was definitely assured continuance. These guarantees have the same fate as the German Austrian treaty of 1936 which guaranteed Austria's independence. 

The Czechoslovakian crisis has opened the eyes of the whole world, even of those who did not wish them opened. 

If failures and errors are to be corrected by the measures which we now see, then it can only presuppose the end of civilization. 37
