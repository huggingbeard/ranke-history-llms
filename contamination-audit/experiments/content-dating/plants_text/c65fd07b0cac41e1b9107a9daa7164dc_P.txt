The aluminum fabrication industry is extremely heterogeneous in terms of production, marketing, size, technical skills required, profitability, and growth. Most companies in this industry, however, have had a substantial and profitable growth in recent years.
While a wide variety of different diversification opportunities exist within this field for New England textile manufacturers, production of aluminum extrusions and the manufacture of aluminum building products are indicative of the potential for growth and profits which characterize many segments of this industry.
Acquisition of an existing, profitable company fabricating aluminum is recommended if problems of obtaining a supply of aluminum can be satisfactorily resolved.
2. The Instrument Industry As a diversification opportunity for textile manufacturers, the instrument industry offers many advantages. The nature of the product, the growth of the industry, the needs of many New England instrument firms, the potential for integrating resources, and the availability of labor and technical ability all provide a favorable environment for diversification.
The industry has been growing at a high steady rate. In addition, the underlying factors causing this growth assure a steadily expanding market for instruments of various types. The rate of growth of the instrument industry will probably remain at its present relatively high rate at least until 1958, with some possibility of a decreased growth rate after that time.
Certain areas within the industry will continue to grow at above-average rates and provide good opportunities for diversification.
Conversely, the management problems of the instrument industry are very different from those of textile firms. As a result, it appears more attractive for a textile company to buy or merge with a going concern rather than attempt to expand from within.
3. The Plastics Industry The plastics industry may be of particular interest to the mediumsize textile firm where the amount of capital available for investment is a limiting factor. There are diversification opportunities within the plastics industry which can require a relatively modest initial cost.
A diversification effort into plastics has the potential of partially offsetting many problems which a New England textile firm now faces in its present market. The present and continued rapid growth in plastics offers a New England textile firm an opportunity for expansion not possible in the present static or declining market for textiles. The stability of the demand for plastics, due to its wide market base, may help to alleviate the problems associated with the wide cyclical ups and downs in the demand for textiles. New England is expected to continue to enjoy a prominent position in the plastics industry, since there are segments of the industry not faced with such geographical disadvantages as are faced by the textile industry.
Diversification opportunities in the plastics industry must be evaluated in terms of the specific situation. In general, certain segments of the plastics processing and fabricating industries appear to best satisfy the requirements for textile firm diversification. Specifically, high pressure laminators and processors of resins, requiring specialized knowledge and techniques, appear to be particularly attractive.
Acquisition of a going concern provides the most effective method of entry into the plastics industry by a textile firm. Experienced technical personnel and an established market position may be most easily obtained in this manner. Furthermore, acquisition of an established plastics company will enable a textile firm to contribute its strengths most effectively to the operation.
4. The Electronics Industry The electronics industry has, in a relatively short period of time, developed its present volume of approximately $9 billion and is expected to attain sales of nearly $13 billion by 1960. The industry is comprised of the following five product groups, which are listed in diminishing order of suitability for the textile industry: Components, Commercial Products, Household Products, Military Equipment and Receiving Tubes.
In segments of the industry where products have become relatively standardized, large manufacturers maintain dominant positions because of high capital requirements and efficiencies realized from mass production. On the other hand, smaller companies can enjoy profitable growth when engaged in the production of highly specialized items, attaining competitive stature through product development and application engineering.
Component manufacturing provides attractive diversification opportunities in view of its growth potential, the relative low entry cost, and the minimal requirements for research and development. The manufacture of hardware items, especially those associated with the trend toward miniaturization, is considered the most suitable diversification possibility for textile companies. Similarly, the manufacture of printed circuits should also provide occasions for remarkable growth during the next 10 years.
The field of commercial products should be closely examined for acquisition possibilities, particularly in specialized areas, such as electronic controls and data processing equipment, where small companies can avoid direct competition with major concerns.
5. Other Possibilities for Diversification The production of prefabricated homes, abrasives, sporting goods, and non-woven fabrics offers other diversification opportunities which would be appropriate for some textile firms. Further, there are a number of industrial activities carried on in New England which, under the proper circumstances might become appropriate areas for the textile industry to explore.

LO INTRODUCTION The problems of the New England textile industry have been isolated, discussed, and analyzed by many highly competent individuals and groups. The problems require no amplification in this report. A simple restatement seems warranted here only to give perspective to the report.
PROBLEMS OF THE NEW ENGLAND TEXTILE INDUSTRY The textile industry is notably unstable. Historically, its growth pattern has been typified by acute depressions followed by upswings, such as the one caused by the Korean War. These upswings require large industry capacity to meet market demands. As a result, periods of low activity find the industry with much overcapacity.
In seeking to retain a share of declining markets during slack periods, manufacturers face strong price competition. In fact, price is often the focal point upon which market share depends. In recent years price competition has been aggravated further by the re-entry into world markets of foreign manufacturers who were inactive during the war.
The two principal elements in the cost of textiles are labor and raw material. Little can be done to reduce material costs. Consequently, manufacturers facing strong price competition tend to seek lowcost labor areas, and in other ways reduce labor costs. Lower-cost southern labor is the principal reason why the past 55 years have witnessed a shift in industry concentration from New England to the southern states.
Effect on Textile Manufacturers Reduced profit margins for prolonged periods have followed from the slow growth and the instability of the industry. Because of this reduction in profits, management has been restricted in its ability to borrow money for new plant and equipment to replace obsolete facilities.
This, in turn, contributes to the manufacturer's problem of remaining competitive.

In many cases the losses have been so severe that liquidations have resulted. In other cases these losses have forced management to undertake the costly and risky strategem of relocating in regions where labor costs are lower. Manufacturers who remain in New England often have been forced to reduce the scale of their operations.
Those firms which remain in operation in New England find themselves in one of three positions. First, there are the firms which face ultimate liquidation because of their inability to retain markets for their output at prices which cover costs. Second, there are the manufacturers who have continued to operate at a healthy level, increasing their share of the market. These firms appear to be in the minority.
Third, there are the companies which, though subject to extreme competition, are able to continue operations at a reduced profit level. These firms constitute the majority in the New England textile industry.
Effect on the Community The instability and slow growth of the textile industry have meant an economic loss to the New England community. Management teams capable of providing employment have been dissolved. Capable leadership needed in the New England community has been dissipated.
Other businesses have lost textile firms as customers for their goods and services. Reduced textile operations by firms remaining in New England have been responsible for labor cutbacks, periodic unemployment, and reduced spending. The loss of taxes, because of reduced textile operations, has restricted the ability of New England communities to spend in the public interest.
All members of the community stand to gain if a solution to the textile industry's problems can be found. Stability and growth in this industry will benefit the community. The industry's problems are the community's problems.
ALTERNATIVES TO NONTEXTILE DIVERSIFICATION What action can be taken by the textile industry to stabilize business, introduce growth opportunities, and help firms facing ultimate liquidation to survive? This is management's problem. As with most problems of management, alternative courses of action present themselves. Management's success depends upon recognizing the alternatives and weighing them in arriving at a workable plan.

Diversification into nontextile lines, with which this report is concerned, is but one of several possible ways in which textile manufacturers may approach the problem of increasing profits and improving the growth potential of their operations. This report is designed to aid textile management in weighing the advantages of this approach.

For example, through a cooperative advertising and marketing program, manufacturers might create for New England woolens a demand which could persuade consumers to pay premium prices for quality output. New marketing techniques would provide at best only a partial solution to the problem of attaining stability and growth in textile operations. Price competition may largely offset or negate improved marketing methods. Shrinking markets for certain types of textiles would partially offset other gains.
In some situations, modified and improved materials handling equipment, improved plant layouts, new mills, new textile machinery, or similar improvements might be most productive in reducing costs and permitting more competitive pricing of New England output. Since costsaving equipment is equally available to competitors in the South and elsewhere, some industry members hold the opinion that a New England manufacturer would not obtain long-term benefits from plant improvement.
The merging of small mills which have lost their principal customers may provide an opportunity for consolidation under one roof, with a resulting reduction in costs and greater assurance of market demands for goods produced. The problems here are manifold, and it is outside the scope of this report to discuss them. It should be pointed out, however, that such a move presents the danger of merely compounding the problems of the merging companies which are already experiencing difficulty in competing successfully. The consolidation of two marginal operations does not in itself necessarily produce a successful one.

Diversification into other lines of textile production might open new markets or provide a firmer grasp on present ones. On the other hand, to venture further into an industry which is the source of the problem, might magnify existing difficulties.
Plant relocation in the South is another course which might strengthen a firm's market position. Tax concessions by southern communities and important lower labor costs might result in lower prices for products and make them competitive. However, the recent enactment of the one-dollar minimum wage law considerably reduces the wage differential advantage enjoyed by southern mills.
Relocation has other disadvantages. The cost of moving machinery and equipment, hiring and training new workers, and providing severance pay for present workers is high. There are other costs which cannot be measured in dollars and cents: losing a faithful and loyal work force; rendering a work force jobless; and possibly breaking up a community. Further, liquidations are not unknown in the southern states.
The chances of survival in the South must be carefully examined. In many cases relocation must be rejected as too costly and impractical.
The recent change in the minimum wage law and the rapid industrialization of the South work to minimize the wage differential between northern and southern areas. Since such changes might in time resolve the problems of some manufacturers, a "do-nothing" policy at this time is sometimes advocated. It must be pointed out, however, that these changes would not offset problems of slow growth and those caused by fluctuating demand. Nor would a "do-nothing" policy assist those manufacturers who are faced with declining markets caused by the relocation or liquidation of customers within the industry, or who are threatened by low cost imports from foreign manufacturers.

PART I DIVERSIFICATION: A PROBLEM-SOLVING TOOL FOR THE TEXTILE INDUSTRY DIVERSIFICATION AS A COURSE OF ACTION Diversification into new fields of activity is a business alternative which can be used to offset the effects of declining markets and fluctuations in demand, and to increase the rate of growth of a company.
For firms facing declining or vanishing markets, entrance into a new field provides an opportunity to become established in a growth industry. For firms facing fluctuations in demand, broadening the product base stabilizes operations by reducing the impact of seasonal fluctuations or a depression in any one field. For firms in fields where profit margins are low, a more secure financial position can be gained by expanding operations to include activities in fields where profit margins are high.
SUCCESSFUL USE OF DIVERSIFICATION An excellent illustration of diversification used to these ends is furnished by the Elgin Watch Company of Elgin, Illinois, which has engaged in the manufacture of timepieces for almost 90 years. Recently the company has been faced with aggressively marketed imported watch products made by foreign companies enjoying a 65 percent wage rate advantage. Moreover, the watch market fluctuates widely: a 10 percent change in disposable income results in a 20 percent change in the demand for watches. In addition, the watch industry's postwar pre-tax return on net worth has generally been in the neighborhood of 10 to 15 percent, while for all manufacturing the figure is approximately 25 percent. Following a thorough study of industries, the Elgin Company settled upon electronic components manufacture as a suitable area of diversification, on the basis.
of growth prospects, net worth return (approximate average 50 percent), and type of manufacturing skills required. It implemented diversification in this area by the purchase of three going concerns. In the first half of 1954, while sales of domestic watches continued to decline, Elgin reported a substantial increase in dollar volume of business.
SPECIAL HURDLES FACED BY TEXTILE FIRMS While diversification has proved a successful tool for solving the very problems that plague the textile industry, diversification has appeared to be a more daring and radical step for a typical textile firm than for firms in other industries. There is some reason for this belief: --men who have devoted their lives to the manufacture of textiles often have had neither the time nor the occasion to learn other types of businesses; --management staffs have typically been trimmed to the bone as cost-reduction measures in times of severe price competition; --in many instances excess mill capacity has been disposed of to cut costs; textiles; --textile machinery can be used only for the manufacture of --although a textile firm may have prestige and name acceptance in textiles, these assets may be of limited use in persuading greater sales in fields where the name is unknown.
Too little attention has been paid to the fact that these handicaps can be overcome. Nor has there seemed to be adequate appreciation of the wide variety of resources available within the industry which can contribute to the success of a diversification program.
If diversification requires of textile management specialized knowledge in another field, particularly in areas of marketing and research, this "know-how" can be acquired through the purchase or acquisition of a firm with its management team intact or through merger with such a firm. New skills can be learned. Specialized personnel can be hired.
The inability of a textile firm to use its production facilities does not present a stumbling block to diversification. Production equipment and space can be obtained through acquisition of a going concern or through merger; plant space can be built; machinery can be purchased or leased.
TEXTILE RESOURCES WHICH CAN BE USED IN DIVERSIFICATION Textile firms have many resources which can be applied to a diverse operation, and which can help the new enterprise attain its maximum growth potential. Opportunities exist for exploitation of textile management, organizational, and locational resources. All will not be available in every case. The extent of resource transfer will depend on the specific situation. The following paragraphs are merely suggestive of the wealth of applicable resources which each firm will discover when it examines itself.

Management Resources "We do the same things here (as we did in textiles) but call them by different names. In these words a former textile plant superintendent who had become a production manager in the metalworking industry described an experience common to most men in business and industry who transfer to other fields.
Textile manufacturers have a general conceptual understanding of manufacturing processes which put raw material in at one end and take finished goods out at the other. Cost systems, materials handling, labor relations, plant layouts, warehousing, and inventory control are just a few of the areas of production which are common to all sections of American industry.
A textile management team which has brought its firm successfully past the obstacles which historically have challenged this industry, may be counted on for seasoned business judgment. This talent is indispensable for success in nontextile diversification.
An established textile firm can contribute strong financial management ability to operations in other fields. Financial experience, vital in business planning, can be applied in new-venture financing and merger negotiations. Sound accounting practices, developed by many textile firms in the face of strong price competition, can be implemented in cost-control systems to insure maximum efficiency in the new operation.
In a price-competitive market, many textile firms have developed cost-cutting procedures through improved materials handling methods. The ability to set up economical production flow schemes may well be applied in other fields.
Textile firms, through their established credit lines, may be able to supply the financial requirements of promising firms which, by virtue of their rapid expansion, are underfinanced and unable to get the working capital they need to take advantage of market opportunities.
Such a move does not imply the investment of risk capital. Rather it suggests that textile firms can take advantage of the fact that some companies would prefer to meet their financial needs through merger arrangements with a firm with an established line of credit.

METHODS OF DIVERSIFYING A textile company can enter into nontextile diversification: by the purchase or acquisition of a going concern; by a merger; by subcontracting for other manufacturers; or by entrepreneurship.
The advantages and limitations of these alternatives are discussed below. It is not possible to recommend one over another. The selection of a method should be based on an appraisal of the individual company's situation and the opportunity under consideration.
PURCHASE OR ACQUISITION OF A GOING CONCERN Entrance into a nontextile field can be accomplished through the acquisition or purchase of a going concern with its management team intact.
Acquisition through the purchase of the assets of a small but promising concern or a controlling interest in a larger firm can be accomplished by a firm with a limited capital position. This concern can bring with it technical and production know-how, an accepted product line, and an established distribution system. It can also bring growth potential in its particular field. A textile company may furnish capital and/or plant space for expansion to a growing firm. It may offer other resources, such as a New England location which favors exploitation of local markets.
The recent diversification of Crompton Knowles Loom Works into the chemical field emphasizes the flexibility which the purchase of a going concern can give a diversifying company. This firm, world-famous for its textile machinery, is a prominent member of the machine tool industry. The purchase of the Althouse Chemical and Dye Company has broadened the firm's product base into the growing chemical field. The acquisition of the Wrap King Corporation has permitted this firm's entry into the promising packaging field. The purchase of the Carl N. Beetle Plastic Corporation has complemented Crompton Knowles Reinforced Laminates Division established in September, 1954, by bringing with it an outstanding reputation in the plastics molding field. This program further stabilizes the firm and provides a basis for renewed growth. New skills and technical competence have been added to this firm's fund of talent in the form of new personnel and equipment. These factors can provide a sound basis for future expansions in related fields.

MERGER A merger can provide the same mutual benefits and opportunities for transfer of textile resources as can the purchase of a going concern.
Merger may be a desirable alternative if textile management elects to diversify by joining with a relatively large and established firm in another field. In this case the tax advantages and readily available capital for expansion which the textile firm can offer, may be the most important consideration. It is conceivable that a merger could be formed in which textile operations would play the minor role in the final organization. This role may not be undesirable in itself if the consolidation permits the textile operation to retain its position in the industry and offers promise of greater profits for the newly formed concern.
SUBCONTRACTING Establishing facilities to handle subcontracts for manufacturers in nontextile fields may be attractive for some textile firms. For example, a machine shop might form the basis for a bigger operation in the metalworking industry. It must be recognized, however, that subcontracts are often assigned on the basis of competence in a particular field, competence which a textile manufacturer might not possess. In this case it would be desirable to establish associations with manufacturers who could contribute equipment and a nucleus of technical assistance to the textile firm. Furthermore, machines can be purchased or leased and competent personnel hired. The prime contractor might want to take advantage of low labor costs or the proximity of a New England mill to local markets, or he might want to overcome a shortage of production capacity at his plant.
While a subcontractor may be subject to the whims of the contractor's fortunes, he may gain valuable experience in a new field with only a moderate investment. Such experience may be valuable to a firm that is "feeling its way" into nontextiles. It might also be attractive as a short-term venture to resolve temporary problems. Work of this sort might be found in all industries from food to atomic energy.
The Rock of Ages Granite Company, one of the oldest and most renowned monument manufacturers in the United States, diversified into the electronics industry via the subcontracting method. The company established itself as a subcontractor during the war by doing work on proximity fuses. In 1945 it moved into the manufacture of electronic components on a subcontracting basis for a large electronics manufacturer. The company trained available labor in this work. Technical assistance was provided by the prime contractor.

Management's primary concern in this case was for the local community, which was almost entirely dependent upon the granite industry.
The recognition by management of its twofold responsibility to the community and to the owners of the company, resulted in substantial benefits to both.
Present operations are nearly double their 1945 size and support a labor force of over 700 people. Going concern values have been transferred from quarrying to electronics to an extent unforeseeable when the new operations were begun. Administrative requirements have been handled successfully from the policy-making level of management down through line supervisory personnel. The staff departments were able to transfer their functions to the new operation almost in their entirety.

It is entirely possible that sound ideas in the form of inventions can be used as the basis for nontextile manufacturing. Ideas for modifying or improving existing products or the recognition of an unfilled need in a market, may point to worthwhile ventures for the diversifying firm. Separate production and marketing facilities can be set up within the company to capitalize on these opportunities. ESTABLISHING A DIVERSIFICATION PROGRAM A detailed program for action is essential for successful diversification. The individual company's problems must be objectively and realistically assessed. Diversification as a solution to these problems, must be analyzed in the light of the specific objectives of the firm and the diversifier's particular situation. It should be undertaken only after a careful audit of the firm's resources which can be brought to bear on the new program.
DEFINITION OF OBJECTIVES A clear-cut statement of the firm's objectives in launching a diversification program should be the first step in establishing such a program. This will require a careful analysis of the company's situation. A textile firm may be faced with any or all of the problems associated with the industry. These should be stated specifically in terms of the company concerned, and then translated into a set of objectives.
Objectives should be well defined. A broad objective, such as "increasing profits, while undoubtedly a real and a common one, is too broad to be helpful in determining the direction a program is to take. On the other hand, an objective such as "raising return on investment to 6 percent" provides a more definite goal.

AUDIT OF COMPANY'S RESOURCES There are many resources within a textile firm which can be applied to a diverse operation. Some of these have been suggested above. All will not be applicable in every case, and the extent of resource transfer will depend on the specific situation.
A complete audit should be made of the resources the textile firm can bring to bear on a diversification opportunity. Greater emphasis should be placed on intangible resources such as management attitudes and abilities because tangible ones may be more readily acquired. The resources available may determine the form diversification should take, and a clear picture of these resources will assist the firm in evaluating various diversification opportunities.
ESTABLISHING CRITERIA Establishing criteria for new operations begins with the question: "What kind of product or service will best serve the company's objectives and make use of its particular resources?" Criteria set forth the requirements the diversification opportunities should meet. In essence, the establishment of criteria is the setting forth of specifications for diversification. It is the blueprint for action and provides a pattern for the evaluation of opportunities.
CONDUCTING A PRODUCT SEARCH After criteria are established, the product search may begin.
A successful search often depends upon opening up channels of information to many fields so that the applicability of a range of opportunities may be evaluated. A product search may be directed by someone within the firm. Or it may be accomplished with the assistance of private firms that specialize in this type of work. However, textile management could benefit most by engaging the services of a research or consulting organization specializing in diversification. Through these organizations time can be saved and expert guidance received.

ALUMINUM FABRICATION INDUSTRY Industry Growth The growth of aluminum production between 1930 and 1954 has been phenomenal, compared with the growth of U. S. industry, as is indicated in Exhibit I.
Major reasons for this cutstanding growth lie in the characteristics of the metal itself. Its basic properties include lightness, corrosion resistance, high thermal and electrical conductivity, durability, and attractive appearance. Alloying adds high strength without significantly impairing either the ease of working with it, or the comparative economies which result in its application in many products. Relatively stable prices for aluminum over the period have contrasted with rising prices of some of its competitive metals, thereby opening new markets. Additional impetus was given to increased usage of aluminum by shortages of copper and the savings in fabricating cost by die-casting and extruding aluminum. In fact, despite considerable expansion of productive capacity since the beginning of World War II, suppliers have not been able to satisfy market demand at existing prices.
Aluminum Supply At the present time, the industry is experiencing one of several aluminum shortages since World War II. Reliable industry sources estimate, however, that supplies will be eased by 1958, by which time the major suppliers will have completed expansion of their primary production facilities. At the present time, some companies are having difficulty obtaining enough aluminum for their production, while other companies have been able to maintain capacity operations and even contemplate expansion in the near future. It does appear, however, that many companies have delayed action on planned expansion programs until the supply situation improves.
Industry Structure and Markets Aluminum is produced in two stages from its raw material, bauxite.
Alumina is first produced from bauxite in a separation process, and then reduced (in separate plants and in different locations) into aluminum pig, ingot, and billet. There are three American producers of aluminum: Aluminum Company of America (Alcoa), Kaiser Aluminum and Chemical Company, and Reynolds Metals Company. Aluminum Limited of Canada (Alcan) also serves the United States market. All four of these major producers own raw material sources, and fabricate various forms of aluminum. They also sell primary aluminum to independent fabricators, and compete with them in certain types of fabricated products. Exhibit II indicates the major kinds of aluminum fabrication, the form of aluminum which is generally used, and the chief products made from these fabrications. It should be emphasized that this Exhibit does not attempt an exhaustive listing of the variety of fabrication processes in current day usage or cannot do justice to the infinite applications of aluminum in everyday life, but is included rather to give those unfamiliar with this industry some idea of a variety of the more common operations which the term "aluminum fabrication" includes.
Diversification Opportunities In discussing an industry such as aluminum fabrication, it is important to remember that aluminum is a material, not a product. In the semifabrication stage, certain standard or custom aluminum shapes or forms are fabricated and sold to diverse markets for different end uses. Some of these shapes or forms are fabricated further, and some are used as delivered, generally as a component part of an end product. Aluminum, however, is often fabricated by metalworking establishments, such as a general foundry, for whom aluminum is but one of many metals used. In other instances, aluminum components, which are a part of a manufacturer's end product, are produced by the manufacturer as a part of his regular manufacturing operation.

While it is impossible to describe each in detail, the sections which follow on aluminum extrusions and aluminum building products may give some indication of the potential which characterizes many segments of the industry.
Aluminum Extrusions The aluminum extrusion industry is an example of an attractive investment area within the aluminum field. It is a fast-growing, profitable, industry, serving diverse and expanding markets. Extruding aluminum does not require highly skilled labor, since extruding is chiefly a mechanical process.
Personnel with needed technical know-how are believed to be readily available.
It is recommended for consideration by the New England textile industry.
The Extrusion Process. The extrusion process is similar to pressing toothpaste from a tube. In aluminum, a preheated billet (a cylindrical form usually from 4 to 5-1/4 inches in diameter) is placed in the container of a hydraulic press and, under extreme pressure, is forced, in a plastic state, through the orifice of a steel die. The form of the extrusion is determined by the shape of the die orifice.
Revere Copper and Brass, Incorporated, one of the largest extruders, says regarding aluminum extrusions: "In this, as in every process, there are naturally some limitations.
But the possibilities of the extrusion process are among the broadest in the entire technology of metals. Aluminum extrusions can be designed for the frame of a picture or the frame of a highway bus, for delicate mouldings or sturdy hand rails, for complex window mullions or simple weatherstrip, for children's toys or a bomber's landing gear, for bridge members or precision instruments."* Aluminum Extruded Products. Revere Copper and Brass Incorporated, 1952, P. 4.

Extrusions are sometimes an end product of themselves, as in the case of aluminum trim for counters, while in aluminum furniture or windows, the extrusions are the material with which an end product is fabricated.
Industry Structure in the United States. Extrusions are produced by the three domestic primary aluminum suppliers, as well as by Alcan. In addition, there are about 93 independent extruders who purchase aluminum ingot or billet from the four suppliers. Among the independents with large-scale facilities are Revere Copper and Brass, Incorporated, Bohn Aluminum and Brass Corp., Harvey Machine Co., and Bridgeport Brass Company. These major independent firms can extrude aluminum for almost any use, since they have very large presses and extensive heat treating facilities. These companies distribute extrusions on a national basis through their own sales offices and through distributors. Companies of this size are competing with the aluminum suppliers to a greater extent than the smaller extruders.
The smaller independent extruders very often use part of their production for an end product which they further fabricate and market themselves.
In addition, they do custom extrusions for others.
Due to the outstanding growth, as well as the size of the smaller independent extruders, it is in this area that the most attractive opportunities lie for New England textile companies. To date, these operations are reported to have been quite profitable.
Industry Structure in New England. In addition to the companies mentioned above which distribute extrusions nationally, there were two independent extruders in New England in 1950: Himmel Bros. Co., New Haven, Conn., and Northeast Aluminum, Inc., formerly of Roxbury, now moving to Lawrence, Mass. Since 1950, two more extrusion operations have been established in Massachusetts; Trim Alloys, Inc., in South Boston, and Universal Converting Corp., in New Bedford. Each of these markets, in addition to extrusions, its own product: Himmel, store fronts; Northeast, store fronts through its subsidiary, National Store Front Co.; Trim Alloys, metal trim and "U-Do-It" items; and Universal Converting, furniture. While the number of independent extruders in New England has doubled in this period, it is believed that New England could support greater extrusion capacity on the basis of its industrial and population density.

Generally speaking, newcomers in the extrusion industry are companies which have formerly purchased aluminum extrusions for fabrication into their own product. When the volume of extrusions used reaches a fair proportion of the output of an extrusion press, integration backwards has been justified in many instances (1) by reduced costs, through elimination of the profits on the extrusions, and (2) by assurance of an adequate supply of extrusions for the manufacture of their end product. At the outset of such a company's entrance into the extrusion industry, its own requirements typically do not fully utilize its extrusion capacity. For this reason, the company sells custom extrusions to other users. Due to the competitive nature of the custom extrusion business, there is constant pressure on this newcomer to broaden his own product line to the point where he can use all his own output. In many instances, products added to broaden a line have been new products, resulting in expansion of the industry, rather than in an increase of competition. Therefore, there is, in effect, an inherent stimulant to growth which characterizes the industry.
While increases in the demand for aluminum extrusions are certain, expansion of facilities will depend in part on increased availability of aluminum billet to independent extruders. At the present time it appears that the supply situation will be eased by 1958, when additional primary capacity will have been completed.
Competition. To date, independent extruders have been able to compete very well with the large extrusion suppliers, because they can produce small-section, low-poundage jobs more efficiently than the large suppliers, and provide better service to this part of the market. This has been true despite an eight percent price cut on extrusions in 1953, and two cuts since that time.

Industry Expansion and Opportunities for Entry. Expansion in this industry will probably be of three kinds: (1) existing extruders will expand their production capacity, (2) present users of extrusions, with sufficient volume, will integrate backwards by adding an extrusion press to their operations, and (3) a newcomer to aluminum, probably with plans for a product made from extrusions, will establish extrusion facilities. Expansion means need for new capital, which, in turn, means an opportunity for a New England textile firm to gain a foothold in this growing and profitable industry.
In addition to captial, it appears that, in some instances, textile companies may be able to offer excess plant space as an additional contribution to a combined venture. One of New England's four independent extruders, Northeast Aluminum, Inc., has recently relocated in an old textile plant in Lawrence, Mass. Ideally, an aluminum extrusion operation should be housed in a one-level building with adequate entrances and exits. Textile companies with available space of the kind Northeast Aluminum found in Lawrence would gain, in addition to a good return on their investment, a chance to spread their overhead costs.
There are three possible methods of entry into the extrusion industry.
They are presented here in order of ease, but not necessarily in order of greatest profit potential. The easiest method of entry for a textile company would be through affiliation with an existing extruder, who needs capital for expansion. A company now extruding aluminum is thoroughly familiar with the purchase of billet, the extrusion process itself, as well as the marketing of extrusions, thereby minimizing the investment risks involved. Further, such a company would probably have an established position with a supplier. An arrangement whereby a textile firm could offer capital, and possibly plant space, to an extruder, in return for an equity interest in the business, would appear mutually beneficial to both companies. Management in the extrusion company would presumably be left intact, although the textile company's management might well make helpful contributions in terms of over-all business policy.

Somewhat more difficult on the surface would be affiliation with an extrusion user wishing to integrate backwards through addition of extrusion facilities. In general, it might be expected that greater risk would be involved in investing in a company which is expanding into new fields. In addition, supply problems would be maximum for a newcomer. It should be emphasized, however, that the desirability of such an arrangement would depend wholly on the specific character of the company using aluminum extrusions, and of the textile company concerned. Such a situation would probably offer an attractive profit potential, without necessarily involving greater investment risk. In any event, a textile company attracted by the aluminum extrusion industry should not overlook this means of entry, if a source of aluminum supply can be established.
The third possibility for entry into this industry would be for a textile company either to offer financial support to, or establish by itself, an aluminum extruding company. This kind of entry should be considered only if a marketable product, made from extrusions, is feasible as part of the new company's operations. This way of entry would offer the maximum number of problems, but should not be disregarded completely. The main criterion for evaluating such an opportunity would be the market potential for the proposed company's product. Again, problems of supply would have to be resolved before the operation could be justified. Operating management and technical know-how can be acquired, and marketing problems would not be too difficult.
Such an opportunity might lie in the highly mechanized, very efficient production of aluminum primary windows, as opposed to storm windows.
It has been indicated that real potential lies in an operation such as this. At the present time, the bulk of aluminum window production is manual. Cost reductions possible with a highly mechanized production line should result in gaining a fair share of this very price-conscious market. While consumers in the Northeast have, relative to other parts of the country, resisted acceptance of aluminum primary windows, some believe that the market is now ready to support such a large-scale operation.
The costs of buying a modern extrusion press and necessary corollary equipment, plus housing this equipment in a suitable building, have been estimated at approximately $250,000.
Aluminum Building Products Spectacular growth of the extruded aluminum window and door industry has tended to conceal that the building industry actually uses more aluminum in other areas.
These areas comprise a very wide range of products, running from aluminum roofing and siding to such aluminum hardware as door locks, sash hardware, and hangers. Estimated 1954 consumption of aluminum in the window and door industry was 310 million pounds, of which about 220 million pounds was extrusions. The balance is largely made up from roll-formed windows and doors, which are similar to extruded doors and windows but are produced from roll-formed aluminum strip.
In addition to this, the estimated consumption of aluminum in other building uses was nearly 500 million pounds in 1954.
Growth Potential. Few other uses of sheet aluminum have so great a growth potential. The East Coast market, stretching from Connecticut to Virginia, would be available to a New England producer.
In New England, the use of aluminum for roofing and siding has not been very widespread, although its excellent insulating values should be of considerable interest in an area where summers are hot and winters are cold.
While such roofing and siding are mainly a matter of comfort in the case of residential building, they are a source of considerable savings where heating costs are high in commercial building, and are actually a source of profit in the protection of livestock in farm buildings. The somewhat lower rate of new building in New England is not necessarily a deterrent to manufacturing considering entering this field, since most of these items can be applied several times to a house after it has been built. In most cases, aluminum construction of this type enjoys a very favorable position in Federal home improvement loans.
The chief characteristic of the best opportunities in this area is ingenuity in product idea and design. In recent years at least three types of interlocking aluminum shingles have been marketed, in addition to the more conventional type of strip or sheet roofing. Even in the conventional type, such ingenious developments as "Roll-It-On" have contributed substantial cost savings in the application of aluminum roofing. In the "Roll-It-On" process, aluminum is taken to the building site and rolled to the desired simple shapes by small portable rolling mills on trailers.
In this area, the type of equipment needed and the skills of the labor force are not as important as in others, such as the aluminum die casting industry. In general, the equipment consists of small roll-forming machines, or sheet metal bending brakes. In the case of hardware, more complicated equipment is needed, more particularly spinning lathes and operators, drawing and punch presses, casting equipment, and machine shop equipment. However, this is an area where a manufacturer of building hardware currently utilizing brass, copper, or steel might take advantage of the growth in the competitive material, if it were possible to alter his equipment slightly to take it. The most important asset in this field would be managerial and marketing ability, and good product design. Where these are present, the investment opportunity is a very attractive one. It gives the investor a chance to get his products started and overcome various production difficulties. Then he may enjoy the industry's growth while latecomers are still struggling with design and production problems.

Mechanical Measuring and Controlling Instruments Among the wide variety of instruments produced in New England for indicating, recording, measuring. and controlling are: dial pressure gauges, thermostats and thermocouples, flow meters and controllers, liquid-level gauges, pyrometers, tachometers, fluid-proportioning devices, accelerometers, Vernier height gauges, and traffic counters. In addition, apparatus is made for testing the physical properties of hardness, tension, elasticity, and torsion. Approximately 60 companies are active in this field, manufacturing a wide range of old and new products. Although there is increasing reliance upon electronic devices for measurement and control, mechanical and electrical instruments still provide the most economical and reliable methods of control in many cases. Leading companies in the field of mechanical recording and controlling in New England include the Foxboro Company and the Bristol Company.
Scientific Instruments New England has producers of transits, levels, seismographs, gyroscopes, high vacuum research devices. and various other scientific research instruments. The development of electronic instrumentation has been especially noteworthy in New England.
Optical Instruments and Lenses A few New England producers make a variety of optical lenses and prisms, fused quartz for optical purposes, magnifiers, spectroscopes, astronomical optical instruments, and other precision optical devices. Leaders in the field of spectrometry are Baird Associates, Inc., and Perkin-Elmer Corporation. The Polaroid Corporation makes a range of light polarizing materials.
The largest single factor in the optical field in New England is the American Optical Company which produces a variety of optical goods.

Entrance into the Field The best method for a textile firm to enter the instrument industry appears to be the acquisition of, or merger with, a going concern already established in the field. There are several reasons why attempting to develop an organization from within does not appear attractive for a textile company.
First, the operating problems of an instrument company are very different from those of a textile company. The great emphasis upon research and development of new items, the markets and selling techniques, and the types of production processes in the instrument industry, present problems very different from those found in textiles. The scientific and engineering skills and abilities necessary in instruments would also be strange to a textile company.
Acquisition or merger provides the textile firm with an operating unit which has already shown its ability to compete in the field. In the case of many New England firms, this ability would very likely be reflected in research and development talent and success, rather than success in mass production of instruments. Sources or potential sources of supply of raw materials will be known, markets and potential markets for products will have been tapped to some extent, an intact management and technical team familiar with the administrative and technological problems of the industry would be acquired, as well as the advantage of a relatively established name in some particular field.
The acquisition method of entering the instrument industry has been used extensively by companies which have already diversified into the field, and also by large instrument companies which have expanded their coverage of the instrument market. Speed of entry and acquisition of talent and potentially successful ideas and products appear to be the basic reasons for acquisition, since many of the large instrument firms had been spending large amounts of money on research within their own companies.

Going concern values, which could most easily be transferred to the instrument company, would be: capital, credit rating, general financial strength, and certain administrative functions, such as accounting services.
Further transfer of resources might take the form of more complete integration by utilizing excess textile plant floor space and labor for instrument manufacture. Initially, it appears sound to utilize textile space and labor for instrument assembly operations, rather than fabrication. In this manner, the textile firm would not be forced to invest heavily in fabricating machinery which it does not normally have. Also, there would be a larger and easier direct transfer of textile workers' skills to the semi-skill requirements of assembly operations, as compared to the relatively highly skilled requirements for many of the fabricating operations.
Recommendations Mechanical Measuring and Control Instruments. The Mechanical Measuring Instrument category of devices (Code Number 3821) has shown a relatively high rate of growth since World War II and appears to offer one of the best diversification opportunities for textile companies. Within this field, industrial controls present the greatest opportunities because of their relatively high value and comparatively high volume, together with industry's increasing awareness of the advantages of automatic control of operations.
Industrial controls would include instruments for measuring, indicating, recording, and controlling the many variables of various processes as discussed in more detail below.
Household instruments, such as thermometers, barometers, and rain gauges, do not appear to have sufficient growth or profit potential for a New England manufacturer, because of their relatively low value and domination of the field by several large producers. The market for physical property testing apparatus such as hardness, tension, compression, and torsion machines appears to be of too specialized a nature, as well as being dominated by companies now in the field, to be recommended for diversification.
Control Systems. One of the trends in the Mechanical Measuring category of instruments is the centralization of recording and control instruments on one or two graphic control panels. The process industries, such as oil refining, paper, rubber, textile, and chemical industries, have widely adopted such systems. Although these control panels and systems are almost always custom-made for each individual application, many of the individual instruments which make up a control system are normally standard measuring and controlling instruments. Many are purchased by the panel manufacturer. Manufacture of such control systems would appear to be a good diversification opportunity for a textile company. Two of the main requirements are: a force of sales and application engineers, with the ability to discover and promote use of such systems; and engineers with the ability to adapt standard instruments into a control system.
Basic Measuring Instruments. In the mechanical measuring and control area, the market for dial gauges, pressure gauges, flow meters, thermometers, etc., appears to be well covered by present large suppliers.
Little opportunity exists for a new or small company, producing essentially the same type of devices now being supplied by the industry for this purpose.
There will likely always be a market, however, for any industrial measuring instrument which can provide more accurate data in a more convenient form. An example of a recent innovation in a basic measuring instrument is a new magnetic flow meter developed by a leading industrial controls manufacturer. Innovations in other basic measuring instruments could reasonably provide a new or smaller firm an entrance into this market area.
Analysis and Control Instruments. Greater potential can be seen for instruments which analyze and then automatically send a signal to correct any abnormal condition. This type of instrument is the essence of automatic control, and will be used increasingly in industrial control applications.
An example would be a concentration analyzer, which would detect the presence of a larger concentration of one fluid in a mix, and correct the situation by sending a signal to an automatically-controlled mixing valve.
THE PLASTICS INDUSTRY Growth of Plastics Industry The plastics industry is generally considered to be among the fastest growing segments of our industrial economy. Consumption of plastic materials in 1954 was 350 percent over the 1944 volume. A graphical portrayal of the industry's growth, in terms of synthetic resin production, is shown in Exhibit VIII. The unique characteristics of the plastics materials themselves are perhaps the most important factors underlying the growth of the industry. These qualities have enabled plastics to compete successfully in various fields. For example, many cabinets and enclosures can be molded in plastics at low cost while other materials require more expensive methods of fabrication. Plastics have replaced other materials in many applications where corrosion is a factor. The wide range of available colors and types of finish has gained wide acceptance for plastic materials in the decorative field. In some instances plastic gears have replaced metal ones, because no lubrication is required.
4. The plastics industry possesses good market stability.
Plastic products in some form are sold to practically all segments of industry, through all types of marketing channels. These are both consumer items and industrial component parts. They may be proprietary or subcontracted products. The possession of an additional product line with good market stability should be particularly attractive to a textile firm which is faced with rather extreme variations in market demand and production levels.
Industry Structure The plastics industry in the United States is made up of approximately 5,000 companies. The industry may be divided into three general categories: the plastics material manufacturer, who produces the basic plastic resin or compound; the processor, who converts plastic into solid shape; and the fabricator and finisher, who further machines, forms, or decorates the plastic. F. Goodrich Company. Most of these companies derive a minor part of their income from plastics operations. Included in this general category are a number of dealers and distributors who rework thermoplastic scrap and handle a wide range of auxiliary materials.
Processors The companies classified as processors may form the resin materials into the finished product, or merely perform an intermediate operation before passing the semi-finished items to the fabricators or finishers. The processors fall primarily into the small-business category, and it is estimated that over one-half of these companies have been formed since World War II. There are about 80 firms of this type. The coater makes use of calendering, spreadcoating, and dipping to coat fabric and paper with resins.
Fabricators The remaining companies in the industry are fabricators and finishers of various types. The great majority of these companies are quite small; the average plant has fewer than 30 employees. These companies work primarily with plastic sheets, rods, tubes and other special shapes and make such products as luggage, rainwear, upholstery material and jewelry. The materials worked on by the fabricator and finisher are generally obtained from a plastics processor. There are approximately 1500 fabricators working on rigid plastics. This type of operation is quite similar in many respects to a small-part, metalworking or machine shop.
Finishers Another 1500 companies are finishers engaged in producing finished products from plastic film and sheeting. A number of printers and embossers, who produce designs and patterns on the surface of material are included in this category.

New England's Position in the Industry The Northeast has always held an important position in the plastics industry. While all types of basic plastic materials are not manufactured in New England, eight plants in this area produce about 15 to 20 percent of the total United States output. The New Englandmiddle Atlantic States area accounts for nearly 50 percent of the total plastic processing and fabricating. About 35 percent of the industry is located in the Midwest, while an additional 10 percent is located on the West Coast. The average New England firm is somewhat larger than the nationwide average. The relative importance of New England and its individual states, in terms of molding machines, is shown in Exhibit XIII. The average value of products shipped per New England firm slightly exceeds that of firms in other geographical concentrations of the industry. The large proportion of New England processors and fabricators, producing proprietary items, may be the underlying reason for this higher value.

Plastics processing and fabricating are important parts of an industry which is expected to continue an exceptionally rapid rate of growth.
Plastics processing and fabricating are expected to at least maintain their respective positions in the industry.
The problem of cyclical ups and downs faced by the textile industry may be partially answered by a plastics processing or fabricating operation, since most plastic products enjoy unusual market stability. Plastic resin, a raw material with unique and diverse characteristics, has gained wide market acceptance, and plastic products are sold to a broad range of consumer and industrial markets. The success of plastics, therefore, is not tied to one segment of the economy.
The entry cost can be relatively low. The establishment or acquisition of a plastics processing company by a textile firm would involve comparatively small risk. For example, it is estimated that initial investment in equipment for a small general-purpose molding plant would probably be about $200,000. A plant of this size is larger than most present-day molding firms, since over half of these companies show net worths of less than $100,000. Equipment investment for the production of sheet and laminated plastics is somewhat higher than for a general purpose molding plant.
Operation of a plastics processing or fabricating company offers an opportunity for offsetting the declining profit margins faced by many New England textile firms. It has not been possible to conduct an extensive investigation of the profitability of the plastics processing or fabricating industry.
It has been determined, however, that a number of plastics firms have net profits before taxes of 20 percent or more, based on net worth.
This would be a particularly desirable situation for those textile firms which can take advantage of a tax loss carry-back. However, profit margins of this magnitude are not the general rule. Increasing competition has narrowed the profit margin considerably among the custom molders and extruders.

Many opportunities exist for the transfer of going concern values from a textile firm to a company engaged in plastics fabricating or processing. The fixed costs of many staff administrative functions, such as accounting and personnel, could be shared by the two firms.
Present textile labor can be easily retrained, since the majority of jobs in plastics processing are semi-skilled in nature. Individual situations would have to be examined to determine the extent to which other facilities, such as warehousing, steam capacity, and materials handling, could be commonly utilized.
Re-investment of accumulated earnings in a New England plastics processing company by a textile firm can be of mutual advantage to both parties. The average plastics company has increased its capitalization and improved its financial position in recent years. This segment of the industry is dominated by the large chemical and rubber companies who have typically invested several millions of dollars in resin production facilities. Resin production usually accounts for a relatively small portion of their total sales. Resin production, furthermore, is most economical as a highcapacity operation. Monomer plants are usually located close to the source of basic raw materials (coal, salt, lime, water, natural gas, petroleum, sulphur, and cellulose), most of which are not found in New England. Polymer plants, however, can be located close to the market for the resin, as is generally the case with the plastic materials producers which have located in the Northeast.
Methods of Entry Entrance into the plastics fabricating and processing industry by a New England textile firm can be accomplished in a number of ways.
Requirements in terms of capital, machinery and plant space would permit an entrepreneurial entrance into this area. The addition of a single key man, thoroughly familiar with this field, could permit this operation to be successful. On the other hand, the increasing competitive nature of the market in some cases would favor diversification through the acquisition of a going concern with an established market position. This approach would bring to the diversifying textile company experienced technical labor and administrative personnel who have been operating as an organized productive team. The sales organization and marketing channels will have been set up.

Acquisition of a going concern would permit the textile company to contribute its strengths most effectively to the operation.
Seasoned business judgment and financial management ability of an older textile form may be particularly valuable to a small growing plastics enterprise. The textile company would be able to perform the various staff administration functions, such as accounting and personnel, more economically, by combining these functions for the two firms. Common activities, such as shipping, might be combined for the two operations, and, in many cases, additional needed plant space might be provided at low cost and the two operations combined more economically under one roof.

THE ELECTRONICS INDUSTRY Because of the diverse nature of the electronics industry, an appraisal of its exceptional growth can be meaningful only if its segments are individually reviewed. Although they are not mutually exclusive, the following product classifications, listed in order of increasing attractiveness for the textile industry, provide the most appropriate structure for this type of analysis: 1) Receiving Tubes 2) Military Equipment 3) Household Products 4) Commercial Products 5) Components In reviewing product classes, particular attention was given the growth prospects of small companies. Because of requirements for technical skills, personnel, and facilities, the only reasonable means for entry of the electronics industry by a textile company is through the acquisition of a relatively small going concern. Successful companies of considerable size have been thoroughly screened for acquisition in recent years by the many larger concerns interested in electronics diversification.
Consequently, attractive companies might only be available at unrealistic prices. Small operations, on the other hand, are frequently undercapitalized, and, in some instances, are actively seeking sources of working capital. Small companies, ranging in size of tangible net worth up to $1,000,000, should be carefully examined for growth potential through product development.
Exhibit XV lists the past and projected growths of the various segments of the industry. Those product groups which reflect the greatest rates of expansion are the ones providing the best opportunities for product development. Remarkable growth of an industry segment is frequently the result of unusual success of a few individual products, rather than uniform growth of all products which comprise the group.
Receiving Tubes Receiving tube manufacturing provides the least attractive opportunity for diversification in the electronics industry, in view of the dominance of the nine large manufacturers, who supply more than 90 percent of the total market requirements. In this and other segments of the industry, there are only restricted opportunities for small electronics manufacturers to supply standard products in direct competition with the major producers.

Military Equipment Military equipment manufacturers participate in an area characterized by instability and moderate profitability. However, approximately 80 percent of electronics manufacturers supply products which, at least to some extent, find end use by government agencies.
Many manufacturers attempt to secure a favorable balance between their government and civilian volumes, retaining the former for the following reasons: 1) Government contracts provide additions to total volume which make possible operating efficiencies, tending to provide stability for the duration of the contracts.
2) As a result of government research, development, and manufacturing contracts, many companies have acquired technical staffs and production skills which are of substantial value to nongovernmental activities.
However, in view of current defense requirements and the present climate of this segment of the electronics industry, the exclusive production of military equipment, such as radar and guided missiles, does not provide attractive diversification opportunities for the textile industry.
Household Products Household products, including radios, television sets, and phonographs, are manufactured by a few very large companies, who compete principally on the basis of marketing and production efficiencies, rather than product or circuit designs, which have become relatively standardized. Because of the high entry costs and the brand dominance of present manufacturers, a small producer of "packaged" household equipment must contend with intense competitive pressures which seriously affect his growth and stability.
Broadcast Equipment This field includes entire systems which involve the transmission and relay of radio signals. The market for these systems is mature, has little growth prospects, and is adequately serviced by large companies such as RCA, GE, and Westinghouse.
Mobile and Amateur Radios The market for mobile radio equipment is expanding moderately, but is dominated by major concerns, minimizing the opportunity for growth for smaller companies. The market for amateur radio equipment is highly competitive, with many small companies participating actively. Because of the relatively modest growth potential and the intensely competitive nature of the market, it is not considered a suitable diversification opportunity for the textile industry.
Heating Apparatus Induction heating is used in soldering, surface heating, and heat treating of metals. Several large electronic companies, such as GE and Westinghouse, have supplied this market for many years, providing a high measure of application engineering. Competitive positions in this field are well established.
The market of dielectric heating has grown during the war, although its development is substantially below that originally forecast. This equipment is used for heating non-conductive substances such as plastics, sand cores, wood veneers, and is used in medical therapy. The market is small, its growth is uncertain, and it is intensely competitive among small specialized companies. Although a small company may have opportunity for growth in the dielectric heating field, market expansion has been so hesitant that it does not provide a sound area for textile diversification.
Radiation Instruments There are approximately 100 manufacturers of radiation devices, of which less than 10 dominate the market. The industry is keenly competitive, highly specialized, and overproduced. At the present time, it does not appear to offer a diversification opportunity.

Industrial Television The field of industrial television is developing rapidly, but is expected to account for only 3 per cent of the total market for commercial products in 1960. Closed-circuit television is used to a limited extent at public meetings, security posts, and institutions for observation. The market for these systems is relatively limited and competition among manufacturers is very intense. Most of the large manufacturers of consumer television equipment are not active in this field. Consequently, industrial television does not appear to offer a satisfactory diversification environment for the textile industry.
Microwave Relays Microwave relays include communication systems used for telephone and television transmissions. The market is small, but affords an excellent growth potential. However, because entire systems are required and a great amount of engineering is necessary, only large companies such as Federal Telephone and Radio, Raytheon, and GE, can compete effectively.
Marine Equipment (Non-military) This field includes navigation and communication systems. The market is limited and has little growth potential, relative to other segments of the electronics industry. Manufacturers of this equipment are principally suppliers for the military, their civilian lines being outgrowths of military contracts. Therefore, this field is subject to most of the difficulties of military equipment manufacture.
Aviation Equipment (Non-military) Aviation equipment includes navigational and communications devices used in civilian aircraft and by ground control installations. The market is small and is serviced by large companies. As with marine equipment, manufacturers of this equipment are essentially producers for the military.

Components The manufacture of components is considered the most suitable electronics activity for textile manufacturers interested in diversification.
Components are usually of relatively simple design and construction; are produced in large volumes, requiring a considerable amount of manual assembly; are supplied principally by small companies; and require production skills, rather than extensive technical support. In the following paragraphs, specific types of components are reviewed and appraised with respect to the diversification objectives of the textile industry.
Hardware Hardware items include a wide variety of electronic parts, such as switches, relays, contactors, clips, clamps, insulators, plugs, cabinets, dials, and knobs. These products, which are mechanical or electromechanical, rather than electronic, are manufactured from metal, plastic, glass, and ceramic.
Excellent opportunities exist for small companies in this field on the basis of product design and production efficiencies. Since purchases of standard hardware components are usually based on price alone, textile management experienced in the control of production, costs, and personnel may be able to participate actively in the administration of an acquired manufacturing operation.
In affording an opportunity for entering the electronics industry with a minimum of risk and capital expenditure, the manufacture of hardware also provides considerable product stability. A producer normally supplies widely diverse manufacturers, his volumes not being dependent on specific end uses or individual customers. Consequently, he may select the types of accounts most appropriate for him in view of industrial trends.
Opportunities for a New England producer appear to be particularly attractive because (1) frame houses are completely acceptable to New England consumers and (2) the density of New England's population means more potential buyers within the producers' market area.

SPORTING GOODS Products of the sporting goods industry include fishing tackle, golf and tennis goods, baseball, football, basketball, and boxing equipment, roller skates and ice skates, skis, gymnasium and playground equipment, billiard and pool tables, and bowling alleys and equipment.
Wheel goods, durable toys, boats, and pleasure aircraft may or may not be considered part of this industry.
The Market The market for sporting goods has changed significantly in the past 30 years, due largely to changes in the distribution of national income and increased leisure time. Activities which relatively few could enjoy a generation or two ago, are now recreation for many.
Exhibit XIX indicates the comparative growth of expenditures for wheel goods, durable toys, sports equipment, boats, and pleasure aircraft (the broad definition of "sporting goods"), and the growth of the national economy. The outstanding growth of sporting goods is readily seen.
With expected increases in personal income Fortune estimates that expenditures for all recreation will increase from the actual $8.6 billion spent in 1953 to $10 billion in 1959. Since expenditures for sporting goods have increased more rapidly in the past than expenditures for all recreation, due to increased interest in participant recreation, it is expected that the market for sporting goods will continue to increase its share of the recreation market.

There are between 800 and 900 producers in this industry with about one percent, several of them with complete lines, producing about one-third of the industry products, in terms of value added by manufacture.

NON-WOVEN FABRICS Non-woven fabrics are a recent development in the textile industry. They are produced from a web of fiber and a chemical bonding agent. The web is made of fibers from three-eighths of an inch to an inch and a half in length. Cotton, rayon, nylon, dynel, Dacron, or Orlon can be used to produce the fiber web, and cotton and rayon waste are often used. The newer fibers are expected to account for the largest growth in this field.
The two most common methods of bonding are: (1) mixing in the fiber web a proportion of thermoplastic fibers, such as plasticized cellulose acetate, which under heat and pressure soften and serve as a bonding medium, and (2) impregnating the web of fiber with a liquid bonding solution. Sometimes the web is passed through a resin bath so that the entire fabric is impregnated, or it may be "printed" with resin in a pattern.
One well known non-woven fabric is Pellon, manufactured by Pellon Corp. of New York City. Pellon is used as an interlining for clothing. It gives permanent body, is very light, very porous, and can be washed or dry cleaned. In common with other non-woven fabrics, it does not ravel and does not have to be cut on the bias; it consequently has become very popular both with manufacturers and home users.
Other manufacturers have produced these fabrics for a wide variety of industrial uses. One important field is that of disposable cloths, such as wipers, dentists' towels, cleaning cloths, and disposable bed covers for hospitals. Other important uses are linings and paddings stitched to vinyl fabrics in the automotive field and in luggage, in personal products, oil field tapes, and backings for laminated panels, as well as use for a variety of filters.
The Market It is estimated that in 1955 nearly 50 million pounds of fiber will be used in non-woven fabrics, and a total market of $100 million per year is predicted by 1960. This market has grown from only a few thousand pounds in 1945. Uses for non-woven fabrics are continuing to 1.

Industry Structure About 15 textile manufacturers are active in non-woven fabrics.
Among the six largest, two are located in New England. Typically, the manufacture of non-woven fabrics has been added as a new product by a textile company, rather than established by newcomers to the industry.
The market for these products is not localized, nor is proximity to raw materials critical, except perhaps when cotton or rayon waste is used extensively. However, most of the companies producing vinyl fabrics for the automotive industry, one of the major markets for non-wovens, are located in the Northeast.
Up to the bonding, manufacture of non-woven fabrics is similar to that used in woven fabrics. The fiber web is produced on textile cards. The width of the card determines the width of the fabric, as loom size determines width in woven fabrics. Handling the bonding process requires new techniques, but the drying equipment is similar to that used for textile printing. Textile personnel can be utilized for the manufacturing operations. In at least one company, non-woven fabrics are handled by a supervisor whose previous experience was in woven fabrics.
However, a good deal of technical knowledge has been developed in this field, and a textile company would be well advised to hire an expert to head a new operation.
End-use is an important determinant of the manufacturing process. One company reports 800 different types of fabric for as many end-uses. The type of fiber (random or oriented), the bonding agent used, and the bonding method are all important in producing a suitable product. Various methods of manufacture are available, but technical improvements and development of new end-uses will mean at least some changes in the manufacturing process for some time to come.
Non-wovens have already been accepted as the conventional material for some applications, but development of new end-uses is still a very important ingredient of success in this field. Most manufacturers are continuously trying to develop new uses for their present non-wovens, as well as to develop new fabrics to meet special conditions.

Diversification Opportunity Relative to other diversification opportunities, the manufacture of non-woven fabrics offers a textile company maximum utilization of existing skills in the production of a new product with an expanding market.
Production techniques are transferable to such a new operation, although additional ones must be obtained; in addition, marketing of these products for some companies will be similar. Of greatest significance, however, is the opportunity which the manufacture of non-woven fabrics gives to exploit textile management's understanding of the textile industry.
Expansion of the market for these products offers an existing textile company an opportunity to use present skills in a more profitable area. The new character of these products gives a textile company an opportunity to get in on the ground floor in this industry. Companies with abilities in the textile industry probably have a real advantage over newcomers in getting started in the production of non-woven fabrics, since the existing production of non-woven fabrics is, in most cases, an adjunct of a textile operation.
Diversification into this area, however, would be a challenging assignment for any textile company, particularly since establishment of a new operation is the only means of entry. Management policies, production, techniques, and marketing strategy all necessary for success, would be sufficiently different to demand considerable flexibility and real ability. Since, however, this opportunity allows maximum utilization of existing abilities, while at the same time offering a textile company a chance for a "fresh start" within an area of familiarity, non-woven fabrics should be among the first considerations of a company planning diversification.

OLIN-MATHIESON CHEMICAL CORPORATION In 1947, the Mathieson Chemical Corporation was primarily a regional alkali producer, with plants in New York, Virginia, and Louisiana.
Sales of about $25 million represented a substantial share of the alkali business of the country. Since 1947, the company has embarked upon an expansion program which has resulted in: (1) a broader position in base organic chemicals; (2) a growing organic chemicals operation based on both coal and natural gas; (3) a principal position as producer of the country's fertilizers and agricultural chemicals; and (4) a major place in drug manufacture, through merger with a leading drug and pharmaceutical house. In the six years following 1947, Mathieson's operations in industrial chemicals shifted as a percentage of company sales from 100 to 40 percent. The difference was made.
up by sales in agricultural chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and related products.
Mathieson's objectives were to achieve an integrated raw materials position by extending operations into organic chemicals, to make basic inorganics captive, and eventually to reach the consumer directly. All three of these objectives have been accomplished.
In 1949, Mathieson acquired by purchase the Standard Wholesale Phosphate and Acid Works, Inc., and, with six plants engaged in its output, became a major producer of sulphuric acid. By self-expansion, Mathieson entered the rapidly growing organic chemicals field in 1952 with a plant in Kentucky to produce ethylene-based chemicals for natural gas.
Acquisition of John Powell and Company, a leading formulator and distributor of agricultural specialties, has been an important addition to Mathieson's distribution facilities for pelletized fertilizers, pesticides, and veterinary supplies, all products resulting from Mathieson's own internal diversification.

Merger in 1952 with E. R. Squibb and Sons, a drug and pharmaceutical house, gave Mathieson a strong position in the drug manufacturing field. Squibb manufactures and markets four general classes of products: (1) antibiotics, (2) nutritional products, (3) medical specialties, and (4) household drug goods. None of these product areas were directly related to Mathieson's previous line, but they do provide a greater margin of profit.
The effect on the corporation's sales by this expansive diversification was an increase from about $25 million in 1947 to over $240 million in 1953.
Mathieson's diversification program continued in 1954 through merger with Olin Industries, Inc., to form Olin-Mathieson Chemical Corporation. This broadens Mathieson's already extensively diversified operations, for Olin had participated in metal fabrication, sporting arms and ammunition manufacture, film packaging, forest products distribution, and the making of collapsible tubes, toiletries, industrial explosives, flashlights, batteries, and automotive products.
A broad-base corporation of impressive size has been erected by Mathieson, largely through a combination of purchase and merger. THE GENERAL MILLS COMPANY The General Mills Company produces more than 15 percent of the nation's flour, processes and packages about 20 percent of America's ready-to-eat cereals, and has a sizable share of the U. S. packaged food business. Since shortly after the Company's formation in 1928, however, it has been aware that the milling industry is of a narrow base and lowprofit nature, and further, that the per capita consumption of flour was falling (200 pounds per year in 1920 to 146 pounds in 1947). Diversification into other market areas was necessary to strengthen the company's position by lessening its dependence upon a declining market.
One year after its formation, the company began its expansion by purchasing the Larrowe Milling Company, which gave General Mills not only a line of feeds but also a string of New England food stores.
In 1935, the Company announced discovery of a new vacuum distillation process for Vitamin E production, and two years later, with Eastman Kodak Company, formed Distillation Products, Incorporated, for the production and sale of vitamins. General Mills Laboratories, established in 1929, were engaged in product research which from the beginning has served not only to implement the food product line (dehydrated foods, several types of mixes for pies, cakes, and icing), but also to develop specialized production machinery.
The manufacturing of packaging machinery began shortly before World War II, when an ordnance contract from the Government put the mechanical development staff to work on an order for precision instruments.
This group was awarded the status of a division of the company to carry out the venture. By the war's end, the division was producing an extensive line of control mechanisms in its own plant and had accumulated $1,000,000 worth of equipment.

The Mechanical Division maintained its size, however, by continuing to make food-packaging machinery, and recently embarked on an entirely new line of fabrication machinery under the name of "Autofab" for electronics manufacturers.
The Special Commodities and Chemical Divisions were formed by the company for the marketing of such diverse products as resins, nitrogen derivatives, pharmaceuticals, vitamin concentrates, and wheat germ oils. All these were developed or improved in company laboratories, and the additional production facilities were purchased out of earnings. The company has thus developed a very broad market base in areas more salutary from the aspect of both growth and profit return.
Diversification by purchase was also used by General Mills in the case of O-Cel-O, Incorporated, a small manufacturer of synthetic sponges.
Due to high excess profits taxes, O-Cel-O was drained of the capital needed to build up fixed assets, short-term debt, inventories, and credit in the face of a booming market. General Mills, in an excess capital position, offered to exchange stock with the owners of O-Cel-O. As a result, the cellulose sponge enterprise has access to working capital and the research and promotion support of a large corporation.
TRACERLAB, INC.
Tracerlab, Inc., was formed in Boston, Mass. in 1946 for research in electronics and the manufacture of electronic instruments and equipment. It soon became the leading domestic commercial processor of isotopic tracers, and a maker of Geiger- Mueller counters and other precise 105.
measures of radioactivity. It also began making protective shields, containers, and remote-control "hot-lab" tools, and continued to offer consultant research service in nuclear chemistry and physics. By 1951, the company had attained a volume of $3, 000, 000, divided in almost equal shares into commercial research and production, government research, and government production. Disturbed by the fact that a solid commercial market had not been achieved and the heavy reliance being placed on government contracts, the company's directors looked for ways to diminish this symptom of serious market instability. There was insistence that Tracerlab remain in the technical product field, but diversification was a promising means of market reinforcement.
Accordingly, after investigating several related industries, the directors of Tracerlab singled out the Kelley-Koett Manufacturing Company of Covington, Ky., for several reasons. First, almost all of its sales were non-governmental. Second, although the company manufactured x-ray machines for sale to the medical market, a large portion of its business was in the sale of film, tubes, supplies, and accessories. Third, the company was highly stable. Kelley-Koett's sales volume was almost three times that of Tracerlab in early 1951, but the latter company had access to capital because of growth potential, and the acquisition of Kelley-Koett by stock purchase was effected.
Through research, Tracerlab had found applications in industrial controls for x-ray equipment of the Kelley-Koett Corporation, and thereby has effected beneficial diversification of its own product line as a direct result of the Kelley-Koett purchase. Sales of Tracerlab and its subsidiary were almost $13, 000, 000 in 1953, and equally important in the directors' view, a more stable operation has resulted from the decreased importance of military products to the point where they comprise less than one-third of Tracerlab's volume.
OTHER FIRMS The following cases are short examples of diversification to new product lines, classified by motive.

Diversification for Survival Libby, Mcneil, Libby, Inc.
In 1946, Libby, Mcneil, Libby, diversified into the frozen food industry as a protective device for a packing company engaged in a market apparently seriously threatened by technological developments in the preservation of foods by freezing.
Owens-Illinois Glass Company The Owens-Illinois Glass Company, a manufacturer of glass containers, glassware, TV bulbs, glass tubing, and other special purpose glass equipment, diversified into the manufacture of tin containers, when beer cans threatened their major market of bottles.
Stow Manufacturing Company Stow Manufacturing Company of Binghamton, N. Y., was faced with loss of a government contract on its flexible shafts and flexible shaft concrete vibrators. By confining its search to products marketable through its own sales organization and distributors, Stow used caution in the selection of a rotary trowel design for manufacture. With it, Stow successfully broadened its product line and lessened its dependence upon its major products.
Diversification to Offset Cyclical or Seasonal Slumps Borg-Warner In 1929, Borg-Warner sold 85 percent of its total volume to the automotive industry. By a diversification program executed to avoid the sharpest effects of depression, Borg-Warner in 1936 made 52 percent of its sales to non-automotive manufacturers. It had added consumer goods, including refrigerators, washing machines, and ranges, and increased profits by doing so.
American Metal Products Company This Detroit manufacturer of fabricated metal products for automotive and aircraft industries purchased all assets of the Alliance Ware Inc., organization of Alliance, Ohio., in early 1955. Alliance Ware turns out porcelain-on-steel bathtubs, lavatories, sinks, and related items. Alliance has 250 employees, and in fiscal 1954 had sales of $9,000,000, and profits before taxes of $750,000. The American Metal Products Company intended by this purchase to offset the automotive seasonal slumps and to participate in the growth of a company which stands to take advantage of the boom in the construction industry.

Diversification To Increase Rate Of Growth The United Aircraft Products, Inc.
This Dayton, O., firm formed a wholly-owned subsidiary to take over manufacturing, engineering, and development of the parent company's "Metallic O Ring. "This product, a stainless steel ring used as a joiner in place of a gasket, is resistant to heat and to acids. Thousands of uses outside the aircraft industry are envisaged for this product.

The F. L. Jacobs Company This Detroit auto parts manufacturer acquired the Colson Corporation of Elyria, O., for $2,000,000. Colson makes casters, hand trucks, barrel trucks, fork lift trucks, hydraulic lifters, and other material handling items. It employs 1000 workers and has plants in Elyria and Somerville, Mass. Its 1954 sales were $14,000,000. F. L. Jacobs recently acquired the Mills Industries, a Chicago vending machine producer, and Selmix, a manufacturer of soft drink dispensing equipment.
All these areas into which the parent company diversified are potential growth industries.
The Norris-Thermadore Corporation This Los Angeles firm, already diversified as a manufacturer of auto wheels, cooking utensils, ammunition, and with a small venture into press metal plumbing ware, purchased the Cook Manufacturing Company of Walnut, Calif., for about $1,000,000. By doing so, the Norristhermadore Corporation now engages in the production of vitreous china, toilets, and lavatories, and will enlarge the new facilities so as to have a complete line of plumbing fixtures. Included in the purchase is a plant used for forming aluminum sheet aircraft parts, but easily convertible to other uses. Norris-Thermadore thus acquired management familiar with an industry which N-T was itself just beginning to enter in order to participate in the high profit rate.
Diversification To Acquire New Management Skills Hooker Electrochemical Company This organization purchased the Durez Plastics and Chemicals Company, North Tonawanda, N. Y. Durez, a national leader in producing plastic resins and molding compounds, will be a new division of Hooker, and will retain the Durez name. The Hooker assets at the time of the purchase were $62, 000, 000, those of Durez $21,000,000. Hooker's products include caustic soda and hydrogen made from decomposing a salt solution in electrolytic cells. The major reason for Hooker's purchase of Durez was the desire for management skills. Hooker was just getting into the plastics industry on its own, and when the possibility of enormous expansion in the plastic field became apparent, the desire for skilled management was felt.
